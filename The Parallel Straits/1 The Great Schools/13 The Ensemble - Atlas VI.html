Prelude · what was wrong with the first ensemble

Seven people, one shape.

The first cast was well made and identically made: seven mid-career professionals, ages 29 to 41, one per house, each defined by a single contradiction of the same design — a faithful practitioner meets the case where their tradition fails. That is one good character repeated seven times, and it produces seven parallel arcs that cannot collide.

Four changes fix it. First, widen the ages: sixteen to seventy-one, so that some characters are formed and some are still being formed, and so that the argument passes down a family rather than across a committee. Second, vary the arc shapes. A cast needs a corruption arc, a reversal arc, a confession arc, a disillusion arc and at least one flat arc — a person who does not change and instead changes everyone around them.

Third, and most important: bring in people the schools do not contain. The atlases have never had a character with no education, no doctrine and no institutional loyalty, which means the whole world has been narrated from inside the seven buildings. A hawker, a boat pilot, a trades-school welder and an afternoon-shift sixteen-year-old are not diversity ornaments; they are the only people who can say what the schools look like from outside, and their testimony is what makes the schools' self-descriptions unreliable.

Fourth, ground the drama in matter. In the earlier ensemble two characters had supernatural problems — non-local cognition, borrowed memory. Both have been converted into material ones: a statistician's nine-year-old data fraud, and a listener going deaf. The drama grows, because every character now answers to consequence.

What follows is organised in three tiers — the seven signatories, the six the atlas forgot, the four who are too young to be listened to — and every entry ends with a betrayal, because a character in this world is only useful at the point where their private conviction rubs against the tradition that formed them.

Nobody here is a representative. They are all disappointing somebody on principle. Atlas VI · casting rule
Tier one

The seven who will have to sign something.

Each is the person their house would send, and the wrong person to send. Their arcs now take five different shapes, and three of them are directly implicated in each other's failures.

Balai Pusaka · 39 · Bukit Temenggong

Nur Iskandar

Legal custodian of the eastern watershed

A scholarship child in a house of inherited duty, which means he has all of the obligation and none of the standing. He is the best custodian of his generation because he had to learn the ritual grammar consciously, and everybody can hear the seam.

He is asked to represent a watershed against a transport and water scheme that will, on any honest accounting, keep several million people supplied. His house's doctrine has no formula for a duty that must be weighed against a larger duty; pusaka is inseparable, and inseparability is exactly what the situation forbids.

“I can tell you what I inherited. I cannot tell you what to do when two things I inherited want opposite outcomes.”
Wants
To be trusted with what was handed to him
Believes, falsely
That an obligation can be discharged without dishonour if the reasoning is good enough
Pressure
Tengku Sofia, the actual heir, wants custodianship abolished and says so publicly
Arc · corruption as growth
Signs the release. Becomes the traitor who was right. Ends as custodian of a place that no longer needs one, which he continues to visit.
Betrays
His own house, in writing, on a Tuesday afternoon, with a witness present
Jit Sin · 31 · Lam Keng district

Lin Yanshi林衍时

Architect, essayist, editor of the quarterly

Third generation in a printery family, and the sharpest internal critic her school has produced in forty years. Her thesis — that Jit Sin's love of transformation has hardened into an ideology blind to a good conservation — made her famous at twenty-six and unemployable inside the school for three years.

Then a nineteen-year-old with no institutional standing produces a basin model demonstrably better than anything the schools have, and Yanshi discovers that she does not want it accepted. The model is right. What troubles her is who made it, and what accepting it would cost the practice of argument she has spent her life inside.

“I have been calling myself a radical since I was twenty-two. It turns out I am a person who likes the room to be a certain way.”
Wants
To be the one who reforms her house from within, and be thanked
Believes, falsely
That she is a radical, beyond being a very good editor of other people's radicalism
Pressure
Sol Soren-Iyer's model; and her own father, who set the type for her first essay
Arc · reversal
Argues against the better idea, wins, and spends the last third of the book publishing her own dissent from her own victory
Betrays
Sol — by delay, which she considers a meaningful distinction from lying and nobody else does
Toa Keng · 34 · Institution Gate

Elias Tan

Statistician of collective cognition

The most quoted researcher of his cohort, brilliant in the specific way that comes from having something to outrun. Nine years ago, finishing a dissertation on distributed decision-making, he smoothed eleven observations. He has called it housekeeping ever since. It was not housekeeping, and the entire downstream field — including the models the water hearing will rely on — sits on top of it.

He is a man who did one small dishonest thing at twenty-five and then did nine years of excellent work to make it retrospectively unimportant, which is a strategy that works until somebody needs the raw data.

“Eleven points. You could fit them on the back of a receipt. I have not been able to put them down for nine years.”
Wants
To remain the person everyone quotes
Believes, falsely
That a small correction becomes true if the work built on it is good
Pressure
The hearing subpoenas primary data; a seventeen-year-old editor gets to it first
Arc · confession
Loses the career, gains the book's most useful witness. The only character who ends lighter than he began.
Betrays
Himself first, at twenty-five; then a graduate student he let take the blame for four days
Al-Merikani · 36 · Merican Quays

Samira Bharucha al-Haddad

Trust architect

Old-family enough to find old-family status embarrassing, and good enough at instruments to know exactly how much of her family's dignity is a legal fiction that has held. She inherits responsibility for a Hundred-Year Trust whose stated purpose — the maintenance of the eastern catchment in perpetuity — has become the single largest obstacle to fixing the water supply.

Merikani law was built for discontinuity: promises that survive governments, deaths, currencies. It was not built for a promise that becomes harmful while remaining valid. Breaking it correctly will take her fourteen months, cost her every family relationship she has, and provide the money that saves the basin.

“The instrument is perfect. That is the problem. Nobody drafts a way out of a perfect instrument.”
Wants
To be worthy of a document written before she was born
Believes, falsely
That money can be made to keep a promise if the drafting is careful enough
Pressure
Her cousin Idris, suing for the family's residual claim, who is sympathetic and partly right
Arc · disillusion into power
Breaks the trust legally, correctly, publicly. Ends holding more influence than anyone in the book and despising the fact.
Betrays
Four generations, itemised in an annex, with the citations that make it lawful
Sané-Kora · 33 · Listening Quarter

Amadou Sane

Second Hearer; official listener to the hearing

The finest listener of his generation in a city where listening is the prestige skill, and he is losing his hearing — progressively, asymmetrically, with maybe three years of usable range left. In a tradition that locates authority in the trained body, this is a disqualification his school has no doctrine for, because the doctrine assumes the ear.

He is appointed official listener to a statutory hearing whose record he cannot fully hear, and rather than resign he begins building something heretical: a Second Hearing that works through transcript, breath, delay and the shape of an evasion on paper. His school considers this a betrayal of embodied knowledge. It is also, obviously, the future of the school.

“Ask me what neither of them said. I can still do that. I have just stopped needing my ears to do it, and they cannot forgive me for the discovery.”
Wants
One more year of the thing he is best at
Believes, falsely
That his authority lives in his ears
Pressure
Uncle Sec does a bit about it, on air, three weeks before Amadou intended to disclose
Arc · flat — he changes the institution instead
Does not resign, does not recover, does not soften. Certifies the record. Leaves behind a method that will be orthodoxy within a generation.
Betrays
His teacher's central claim, in a footnote, on purpose
Nine Rain · 29 · born off-world, has never seen rain

Mayu Quispe

Habitat metabolic engineer

Second-generation off-world, sent to Earth to argue a case her own house finds obscene: that reciprocity is a planetary luxury, affordable only by people who live inside a biosphere that forgives them. Her habitat is failing metabolically. The engineering fix requires extraction, dependency and a supply contract with people who will use it as leverage.

She is the ensemble's antagonist and its conscience in the same body — the only person present with a constituency that dies on a schedule. She is also furious, continuously, in a way the Earth characters find rude and cannot answer, that dead philosophers get opinions about her oxygen.

“You have weather. You have an atmosphere that repairs your mistakes for free. Do not lecture me about obligation from inside a machine you did not have to build.”
Wants
Eleven thousand people to still be breathing in four years
Believes, falsely
That an emergency suspends the rules, when it actually reveals which ones were load-bearing
Pressure
She wins the argument in the fourth hearing, and immediately sees what her win licenses on Earth
Arc · refusal of victory
Argues for extraction, gets the vote, then withdraws her own motion — a decision that may kill people she is related to
Betrays
Her settlement's council, who authorised her to say only one thing and are watching
Sund Commons · 41 · Cooperative Belt

Erik Soren

Civic economist; chair of the Basin Hearing

Career defender of the Minimum Unowned Life — the constitutional floor of time not owned by production. He chairs the hearing, which makes him the one character with procedural power, and procedural power is the most corrupting kind because it never looks like force.

Separated from Padma Iyer-Rasmussen, whose forecast started all of this, and co-parenting a nineteen-year-old who has publicly rejected protected time in order to work eighteen-hour days on a model nobody will accept. The argument Erik has spent twenty years winning in public is being lost in his own kitchen, by someone he loves, with better evidence.

“I have never once made anyone do anything. I have only ever decided what gets heard, and in what order, and for how long.”
Wants
A fair procedure, which he sincerely believes he can deliver
Believes, falsely
That protected time is a gift he is giving people, when it is a rule he is imposing
Pressure
His own child; and the discovery, at the midpoint, that the fair procedure produces the worse outcome
Arc · corruption
Uses the order of business to engineer a result he believes is right. It works. He resigns, and no one accepts the resignation, which is worse.
Betrays
The procedure itself — quietly, in a scheduling decision that takes four seconds
Tier two

The six the atlas forgot.

None of them will sign the instrument. Between them they hold the knowledge the basin actually needs, the power to build anything, the ability to destroy any reputation in the city, and the only claim on the reader's sympathy that does not run through a doctrine.

Rumah Arus · 44 · no premises, no gazette name

Suriya Lestari

Route-keeper and boat pilot; called Roro by everyone

Fourth-generation route-keeper. What she holds is a set of channels, tidal windows, silting behaviours and old sluice logics carried as practice, inherited in the body, taught only by going. Basin Seven's original hydrology lives in her family's routes and in no survey ever filed, because filing it would have made it property, and every generation of her house has understood that legibility precedes expropriation.

The basin can be restored. To restore it, she must write down what four generations deliberately refused to write down, and hand it to a hearing that includes a Water Authority, a trust, and two schools with financial interests. She is the moral engine of the entire novel and she never raises her voice once.

“You want the routes. Fine. But understand what you are asking. After this, the channel belongs to whoever holds the paper, and that will never be me.”
Wants
To keep the routes unwritten and still save the basin
Believes, falsely
That illegibility is a form of safety
Pressure
Her own nephew is a Ferry Trades welder who will be paid to build whatever the paper says
Arc · sacrifice with open eyes
Writes it down. Watches it become an asset class within eighteen months. Was right about everything and did it anyway.
Betrays
Four generations of a promise, in a room with a stenographer
No school · 62 · Lam Keng, stall 14

Ha Kim-lian何金莲

Hawker; lay assessor to the Basin Hearing

Thirty-eight years at the same stall, in a district declared dead since 1917. She attended no Great School, holds no doctrine, and is selected as lay assessor by a statutory lottery that the schools designed as a legitimacy ornament and have never taken seriously. She takes it extremely seriously, having read every filing, at night, twice.

She is the reader's proxy for the first third of the book and something much sharper afterwards: the person who notices that all four proposed solutions have been costed in money and years, and none in whose afternoon disappears. The compromise that ends the novel is substantially hers, and the recomposition it authorises runs directly through stall 14.

“Seven schools, four plans, nine hundred pages. Not one of you has written down who has to move house.”
Wants
To be left alone; then, having read the filings, to be taken seriously
Believes, falsely
That the schools and their arguments have nothing to do with her life
Pressure
The plan she is drafting will close her stall, and she works this out before anybody tells her
Arc · reluctant authorship
Writes the mixture. Votes for it. Loses the stall. Refuses the citation in the instrument, because a citation would make her one of them.
Betrays
Her own neighbours, forty of whom stop speaking to her
Straits Water Authority · 57 · civil service, 31 years

Josefina Aurelio Bautista

Registrar of Water; author of the extraction scheme

The antagonist, and she is right. Her scheme — desalination at scale, upstream extraction, an off-world energy contract — works, on the numbers, within the window. It also breaks Balai Pusaka's custodial land status, converts water into a permanent market good, and creates the dependency Mayu Quispe has flown eleven weeks to warn about.

Thirty-one years of being the only person in the room who had read the annexes have taught her that competence is a form of virtue. It is not. It is a form of competence. The novel is not interested in humiliating her; it is interested in the moment she gets everything she asked for and understands that she was never the only adult present.

“Everybody in this room has a philosophy. I have a fifteen-year horizon and four hundred thousand connections. Tell me which of those is negotiable.”
Wants
The problem solved before the fifteen years becomes five
Believes, falsely
That being correct and being trustworthy are the same credential
Pressure
She is proved right about the timeline and wrong about the city in the same week
Arc · disillusion
Gets the core of her scheme, inside a mixture she considers sentimental. Retires early. Sends Ha Kim-lian a note nobody else ever sees.
Betrays
A junior engineer whose objection she buries for six weeks, and remembers for the rest of her life
Sané-Kora · 71 · Listening Quarter, Thursday nights

Baba Osei

Satirist; known citywide as Uncle Sec, from “second voice”

Fifty years of the most feared weekly show in the Straits, which narrates the hearing to a city that will not read nine hundred pages. He can end a career in ninety seconds and has done, eleven times, mostly deservedly. He is beloved, which he has decided is the same as being correct.

Structurally he is the chorus: the mechanism by which the novel's institutional argument reaches ordinary people, and the reason every character is performing for an audience they cannot see. Then he does the bit about Amadou's hearing — funny, accurate, three weeks early — and the novel gets to show that the second voice is a weapon as well as a virtue.

“Somebody in this city has to say the thing. I have simply never worked out how to say it late.”
Wants
To be feared and loved simultaneously, forever
Believes, falsely
That a joke has no author and therefore no consequence
Pressure
Amadou will not respond, will not complain, and continues to greet him warmly
Arc · late self-knowledge
Builds his final show around a formal right of reply that he cannot control, then sits in silence for two of its eleven minutes
Betrays
The one person in the city who had never once asked him to be kind
Nine Rain trained · 48 · Water District

Padma Iyer-Rasmussen

Hydrologist; author of the fifteen-year forecast

The number that starts the novel is hers. Fifteen years, published in a technical annex nobody read for eleven months, then read by everybody. She is a private person who becomes a public one against her will, which she manages by becoming steadily more precise as the pressure increases — a defence that reads, to a city looking for reassurance, as coldness.

Separated from Erik Soren; the two of them conduct the novel's central political argument as a custody negotiation about their son's working hours. In the fourth hearing she revises the forecast downward — twelve years, down from fifteen — and is immediately accused of timing it, which she did innocently, and which she cannot prove.

“I did not choose the number. I measured it. Those are different verbs and this city has stopped hearing the difference.”
Wants
Her measurement believed on its own terms
Believes, falsely
That being believed and being heard are the same event
Pressure
Her revision helps Bautista, whom she opposes, and Sol, whom she is trying to protect
Arc · from precision to advocacy
Ends the book arguing instead of reporting — and knows exactly what she has traded away to do it
Betrays
Her own methodological rule about publishing mid-proceeding, for her son
Al-Merikani · 29 · Merican Quays

Idris Bharucha

Family claimant against the Hundred-Year Trust

Samira's cousin, and the case against her. The family's residual claim is real, the family's decline is real, and his argument — that a trust which can be broken whenever it becomes inconvenient was never a trust — is the strongest legal argument in the novel. He is also grasping, self-pitying and frequently unbearable, because sympathetic antagonists should not be dignified.

He loses. Then, in the last part, he is proved right about something else entirely: the instrument that saves the basin does contain a clause that will be abused within a decade, and he identified it in a filing nobody bothered to read.

“You are all extremely comfortable dissolving a promise that was made to people like me. Try dissolving one that was made to people like you.”
Wants
The money, and, more than the money, to be treated as a legitimate party
Believes, falsely
That inheritance is a reward for something
Pressure
Samira offers him a settlement that would be comfortable and humiliating
Arc · vindication too late to help
Loses publicly, is right privately, and is remembered by exactly one person: the drafter who reads his filing four years later
Betrays
His own case, by making it about his dignity in front of a stenographer
Tier three

The four who are too young to be listened to.

Two of them are correct. All four are inside the plot's mechanism, which is the only defensible way to put teenagers in a novel about institutions. And the future arrives in this book the way it always does — badly dressed, in a hurry, with no manners and better data.

Sund · 19 · rejects protected time

Sol Soren-Iyer

Unaffiliated modeller

Erik and Padma's son, working eighteen-hour days in deliberate violation of everything his father's career stands for, on a basin model that is better than the institutions'. Not marginally: it forecasts the third-quarter drawdown within two per cent, which nothing else does. It also cannot be audited in the way the hearing's rules require, and it was built by a person with no standing.

He wants to be right, publicly, now. He is nineteen, so he believes a correct answer compels agreement, and the novel's job is to take that belief apart without punishing him for having had it. In the last part he finds the artifact in his own model — a measurement echo that produced the eerie regularity everyone had begun to mythologise — and has to decide whether to say so.

“You keep asking who I studied under. Ask me what the model says about November.”
Wants
To be right in public, immediately, with attribution
Believes, falsely
That a correct answer compels agreement
Pressure
Lin Yanshi could get him a hearing and does not; Owl publishes him without asking
Arc · chooses truth over admission
Discloses the artifact himself, destroying his own claim, and is offered a place he no longer wants
Betrays
His father's life's work, at dinner, in one sentence he does not realise is unforgivable
Jit Sin · 17 · editor, Weather Report

Tay Wei-en

Student editor; called Owl since she was twelve

Eleventh editor of a paper banned eleven times, and the only journalist in the city who gets Elias Tan's raw data — because she asked a records clerk a polite question that nine professional reporters were too senior to ask.

She publishes. She is entirely right that the fraud is real and the public has a claim on it. She is also the direct cause of the novel's midpoint collapse: the best available solution loses its evidentiary base, six months are lost, and the extraction scheme gains the ground it needed. Being right at speed is its own kind of damage, and she is seventeen, so nobody will let her carry it and she has to anyway.

“Everyone keeps telling me the timing was unfortunate. Nobody will tell me which month I should have chosen.”
Wants
A story that matters, under her own name, before she graduates
Believes, falsely
That speed is a form of honesty
Pressure
Elias, who does not blame her, and whose refusal to blame her is unbearable
Arc · earns her judgement
Holds the second, bigger story for eleven days. Publishes it better. Tells no one why she waited.
Betrays
A records clerk who trusted her, and is dismissed
Sekolah Petang · 16 · afternoon shift, Toa Keng building

Fadhli Zulkarnain

Second Bell; witness to the hearing

He attends the afternoon shift in Toa Keng's building — same walls, same laboratories, twelve hours apart, different world. He is exceptional and profoundly tired of being told so, since every compliment is really a statement about the afternoon.

He wins a morning place and takes it, which is the harder and less flattering choice, and it makes him the one person who can describe the building at three in the afternoon to a hearing that has only ever seen it at nine. His testimony is four pages long and changes the resource allocation in the final instrument more than any argument by any signatory.

“You have all been in this room. None of you has been in it after the fans get switched off.”
Wants
To be good at something without being an example of anything
Believes, falsely
That the morning school is the thing he has been looking at from outside
Pressure
His friends treat the transfer as defection; the hearing treats him as a symbol

Stops being evidence and becomes a witness — the difference being that a witness chooses what to say
Betrays
The afternoon, by leaving it, and then repays it in four pages
Balai Pusaka · 24 · the actual heir

Tengku Sofia binti Anwar

Custodial heir; publicly in favour of abolition

Nur Iskandar's foil and, structurally, his replacement. She holds by birth everything he earned, does not want it, and has said so in a Kora interview her family has not recovered from. Her argument is not adolescent: custodianship, she says, launders a hereditary claim as an ecological duty, and the watershed would be better held by a public trust with no ancestors.

She is right about the laundering and wrong about the trust, which will be captured within a generation. Her arc is not learning to love her inheritance. It is discovering that abolishing something requires exactly the authority she was trying to give up.

“Everyone tells me it is a duty, not a privilege. Fine. Then let me resign from it, the way you can resign from a duty.”
Wants
An ordinary life, and the abolition of the thing that prevents one
Believes, falsely
That custodianship is a costume that can be taken off
Pressure
Nur signs the release she campaigned for, and she is the only person who sees what it costs him
Arc · assumes power in order to end it
Takes the office, dissolves the custodial title, and spends her thirties chairing the public trust she does not believe in
Betrays
Her own position, in the last chapter, by accepting a title she spent six years attacking

Casting method · five tests before adding an eighteenth
1

Different arc shapes, shared doctrines

Seven people can hold seven philosophies and still be the same character. The variety that matters is structural: corruption, reversal, confession, disillusion, sacrifice, vindication-too-late, and one flat arc that changes the institution instead of the person.

2

Somebody with no doctrine at all

Ha Kim-lian and Roro Lestari are load-bearing precisely because they cannot be answered in the schools' vocabulary. Any world narrated only by its institutions will flatter them.

3

No supernatural exemptions

A material problem — a fraud, a failing ear, a stall in a demolition zone — implicates everyone around it. A phenomenon implicates nobody, and quietly excuses the character who has it from ordinary moral pressure.

4

The antagonist gets the best numbers

Bautista is right about the timeline. If the opposing case is weaker than the protagonists' case, the book is a lecture with characters in it.

5

Every entry ends in a specific betrayal

An act, with a date, a room and a person on the other end of it. That is the line that converts a dossier into a plot.
