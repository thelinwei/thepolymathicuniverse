A constitutional sketch for Singapore education

Seven Schools,
One Singapore.

What if Tay Kheng Soon’s cantonal argument were applied to education — with four permanent schools carrying Singapore’s historical memory, and three rotating foreign schools deliberately importing difference?
4 RootsHistorical Singaporean lineages. Permanent. Public. Open to everyone.
3 GuestsForeign pedagogical constitutions. Temporary charters. Here to disturb the system.
1 CommonsA thin federal MOE protecting rights, mobility, equity and a shared Singapore core.
01 · The provocation

Tay’s idea is more radical than decentralisation.

In a 2017 CNA interview, architect Tay Kheng Soon proposed dividing Singapore into five governments along the logic of Swiss cantons: five HDBs, five planning authorities — and explicitly, five MOEs. His argument was that competing systems could generate ideas that a single centralised system suppresses.

What I find productive here is the institutional proposition underneath Tay’s number five and his causal claims:

Monopoly of administration can become monopoly of imagination.
Plural governance creates places where alternative answers are allowed to survive long enough to prove themselves.

Switzerland provides the obvious governmental precedent. Responsibility for education there rests principally with its 26 cantons, while national and intercantonal bodies coordinate the things that must travel across boundaries: qualifications, structures and shared objectives.

But Singapore presents a problem Switzerland does not. We are tiny, intensely connected and residential geography is economically consequential. If Bedok had one MOE, Jurong another, and Bukit Timah a third, educational difference could quickly become land-value difference. Parents would buy their way into a canton. Housing would sort pedagogy by class. Ethnic and socioeconomic composition could harden geographically.

So I would mutate Tay’s canton.
Make the cantons non-territorial. All seven School systems operate across the island. A child in Tampines can belong to the same educational federation as a child in Queenstown. The jurisdiction travels through institutions and membership, across every postcode.

02 · The historical turn

The four local Schools already existed — before Singapore unified them.

This is the part that changes my answer to your first question most. Singapore itself supplies the genealogy, so the four local philosophies can be inherited instead of invented.

Before the modern unified system, Singapore contained four broad language streams: Chinese, Malay, Tamil and English. Each was a full institution in its own right, with different founders, funding arrangements, cultural centres of gravity, relations to religion and empire, notions of citizenship and routes into work.

In 1959, the emerging national system explicitly committed itself to equal treatment of the four streams. Over subsequent decades, practical demand for English, nation-building imperatives and the reforms associated with the 1978 Goh Report progressively unified the system. From 1984, official school directories stopped classifying schools by language medium; Chinese-medium schooling was fully phased out by the late 1980s.

Singapore’s twentieth-century achievement was to move from plural systems → a national system.

The twenty-first-century question might be whether a mature national system can now recover plurality without losing the nation.
Important: the historical streams are an archive open to every child. Any child may join any School: the Chinese School, the Malay School, the Tamil School and the Straits School each admit by choice and interest. Heritage is a public inheritance, held in common.
Root I · permanent

Chinese School · 华校
The Nanyang lineage

Historically, many Chinese schools were built by merchants, clan associations, civic organisations and missionaries alongside the colonial state. They transmitted language and culture, and became institutions of community self-organisation.

Modern constitution: bilingual scholarship; classical and modern Chinese intellectual traditions; mathematics and science; commerce; making; civic association; diaspora and Southeast Asian Chinese history; argument across Chinese and English sources.

What it must resist: becoming “PRC school in Singapore”, examination nostalgia, or an SAP system enlarged into an ethnic prestige track. Its subject is Nanyang: Chinese civilisation refracted through Singapore and Southeast Asia.

Root II · permanent

Malay School · Sekolah Melayu
The Nusantara lineage

Early Malay learning ranged from informal village and religious settings to formal Malay schools established in places including Telok Blangah and Kampong Glam. MOE’s heritage material records curricula that included religious learning, crafts and gardening — education embedded in everyday community life.

Modern constitution: Malay as national and regional language; the maritime world; ecology; archipelagic history; oral traditions; adab and ethics as objects of serious study; craft; food systems; neighbourhood institutions; ASEAN as lived geography.

What it must resist: reducing “Malay” to ethnicity or Islam alone. It is an archipelagic Singaporean school open to secular and religious inquiry, and to every child.

Root III · permanent

Tamil School · தமிழ்ப்பள்ளி
The Coromandel–Straits lineage

Tamil-language education in Singapore can be traced to the nineteenth century; Anglo-Tamil schools appeared in the 1870s. The historical system was much smaller and more fragile than the Chinese stream — MOE records few early Tamil schools, often operating with a single teacher and facing shortages of books and qualified staff.

Modern constitution: Tamil language and literary culture; South Indian and Southeast Asian histories; rhetoric, poetry and public speech; trade and migration networks; mathematics and computation; performance; multilingualism; the Indian Ocean as a connective space.

What it must resist: being treated as a demographic concession. Precisely because the historical institution was smaller, equal constitutional status now says that educational memory is not allocated by population share.

Root IV · permanent

Straits School
English-medium, mission & civic lineage

English-medium schooling grew through colonial institutions and mission schools. It became the route through which many Singaporeans entered the civil service, professions and global Anglophone world — and eventually became the common medium through which the national system was unified.

Modern constitution: English as bridge language; liberal and scientific traditions; debate; law; public administration; world literature; mission-school traditions of service without compulsory belief; Peranakan and Eurasian histories; the port-city habit of translation.

What it must resist: assuming that because English became the common medium, its worldview is neutral. It too is a historical tradition — powerful, useful and incomplete.

This asymmetry matters. Before World War II, MOE’s heritage account records roughly 38,000 students in Chinese schools, 5,800 in Malay schools and about 1,000 in Tamil schools. The four Schools therefore should not pretend they were historically equal in scale. Their equality in the new constitution would be normative: an assertion that Singapore chooses to preserve multiple lineages even where market demand or historical demography did not.

03 · The vitality layer

The other three should not have roots. They should have visas.

This is the part of your 4+3 proposition that I like most. I would make the three foreign Schools constitutionally temporary.

They are not permanent fifth, sixth and seventh tribes. They are guest operating systems: invited because a healthy educational ecology requires exposure to coherent ways of doing things that Singapore did not generate internally.

Four Schools protect memory.
Three Schools manufacture mutation.
Guest A · 15-year charter

World School
UWC / IB / intentional internationalism

This one already has unusually deep Singaporean precedent. Singapore International School opened in 1971 and became UWC South East Asia in 1975. Its useful difference is not “international curriculum” as luxury branding; it is the deliberate construction of a multinational student body around service, peace, responsibility and global interdependence.

Its experiment: What happens if national education deliberately includes a school whose primary social unit is the region and the world?

Guest B · 15-year charter

American School
choice, breadth & civic association

Singapore American School has existed here since 1956, before independence. The pedagogical import worth testing is a particular institutional grammar: broad electives, extracurricular societies, athletics and arts, student choice, school spirit, flexible pathways and the school as a miniature civic society.

Its experiment: What capacities appear when adolescents have more legitimate ways to become excellent than the academic ladder?

Guest C · 15-year charter

Dual School
Swiss–German apprenticeship & craft parity

This is a deliberate nod back to Tay’s Swiss reference. Its deepest foreign import is the refusal to treat vocational formation as the route for those who did not succeed academically. Companies, workshops, technical institutes and schools jointly educate.

Its experiment: What changes when mastery of a machine, kitchen, construction system, care practice or precision craft carries institutional dignity comparable to an academic distinction?

The rotating-seat rule: every 15 years, each Guest School must re-apply for its constitutional charter. Singapore can retain it, substantially rewrite it, or replace it with another coherent foreign model — Japanese lesson study, Italian Reggio Emilia, Danish folk-school traditions, Montessori, a future African or Latin American model we have yet to learn to see. The system institutionalises its own capacity to be surprised.

The Guest Schools would have one further obligation: all curriculum, research, failures, teacher-learning and longitudinal results generated with public money become part of a national educational commons. Their job is not to protect proprietary advantage. Their job is to infect the other six with useful ideas.

04 · Scale

Not seven equal giants. Four roots should outweigh three experiments.

I would treat this as a capacity architecture, flexible from year to year: roughly 70–80 thousand places per Root School and 30–45 thousand per Guest School.

A 75,000-person School is a federation. The existing school estate remains distributed. Each “School” spans many campuses, houses and specialist sites across Singapore. The institutional scale is large; the relational scale stays small.

05 · Seven MOEs

The central MOE becomes constitutional.

This is where I would preserve Tay’s provocation most literally. Each School needs enough sovereignty to produce genuinely different consequences. Otherwise we merely get seven branding departments administering the same syllabus.

The Federal Education Commons

What the central state controls

  1. Universal public funding and equalisation.
  2. Child protection, inclusion and special-needs rights.
  3. A common Singapore civic minimum.
  4. Minimum literacy, numeracy, scientific and digital competencies.
  5. Recognition and portability of qualifications.
  6. Transport access and cross-School mobility.
  7. National data, independent inspection and longitudinal evaluation.
  8. Anti-segregation rules and admissions fairness.
The Seven Ministries

What they are free to change

  1. Curricular sequence and interpretation.
  2. Pedagogy and classroom organisation.
  3. Teacher recruitment, formation and career structure.
  4. Assessment formats and graduation portfolios.
  5. School calendars and rhythms.
  6. Partnerships with communities, firms and universities.
  7. Architecture and use of public learning spaces.
  8. The definition of excellence beyond the federal minimum.
Competition of constitutions, so that children can flourish.

That is a crucial distinction from contemporary school competition. The unit being tested is not whether Child A can outperform Child B on one examination. It is whether a coherent educational system — its teacher culture, timetable, epistemology, institutions and assessment — produces different kinds of flourishing over ten, twenty or thirty years.

Each Ministry should therefore publish a different theory of education before it publishes its curriculum. What does it believe childhood is? What is knowledge? What is a teacher? What is adulthood for? What does a successful 25-year-old look like? What does it refuse to optimise?

06 · Mixing rules

Pluralism only works if the Schools cannot seal themselves off.

This is where history supplies the warning. A system organised around distinct traditions can become a system of parallel societies. The design therefore needs active circulation.

01 · No admissions by ethnicity, religion or parental wealth.
02 · No tuition differential between the seven public systems.
03 · No academic selection before the end of lower secondary education.
04 · Every School must maintain socioeconomic and demographic diversity bands.
05 · By age 16, every student must have completed substantial learning in at least two other Schools.
06 · At least 10% of teachers rotate, second or co-teach across School systems during each five-year period.
07 · National qualifications must be portable, but the route to them need not be standardised.
08 · Guest Schools stay publicly funded and open. Their foreignness is pedagogical.

There is international support for taking this safeguard seriously. The Netherlands demonstrates that public funding can coexist with schools organised around distinct religious, ideological or pedagogical principles. OECD work on school choice also shows that selective admissions and significant extra fees can intensify social segregation. In other words: plural provision is possible, and equity has to be designed.

One especially useful rule: a child has a home School, and moves freely beyond it. The system should make sentences like this normal: “I grew up in the Tamil School, did a year in the Dual School, took my history studio through the Malay School, and completed my capstone jointly with the Straits and World Schools.”
07 · Why now?

Singapore used centralisation to solve one historical problem. It may now be producing another.

The post-independence state had powerful reasons to standardise education: universal access, nation-building, bilingualism, industrial manpower needs, common credentials and social integration. The unified system was an extraordinary technology for building a country.

But successful institutional solutions create their own shadows. Once one system becomes excellent, every reform is asked to fit inside its grammar. Innovation becomes a pilot programme, then a committee, then an approved variation of the centre. Difference exists, but frequently as an exception granted by the same authority whose assumptions it might need to challenge.

That is where Tay’s argument feels unexpectedly current. His concern reached beyond local democracy to the conditions under which human capabilities can emerge. He explicitly connected schools to the civic and cultural “nervous system” of settlements, and argued that decentralised education authorities could compete to discover better systems.

Perhaps the next frontier is not more innovation inside one education system.

It is the protected coexistence of several education systems inside one republic.

AI makes the question sharper. When information retrieval, explanation, drafting and routine cognitive production become cheap, the system cannot justify itself merely as a high-efficiency distributor of codified knowledge. The scarce goods move elsewhere: judgment, embodied skill, interpretation, responsibility, social trust, intellectual courage, cultural depth and the ability to inhabit more than one way of knowing.

A seven-School system leaves the question of which educational philosophy best cultivates those capacities open, and does something more interesting:

It makes uncertainty constitutional.

08 · Precedents

None of the pieces is unprecedented. The combination is.

19th–20th c. Singapore · parallel educational systems
English, Chinese, Malay and Tamil streams developed under different state, mission and community arrangements. Singapore has already lived with educational plurality — and also knows its integrative difficulties.
1959 onward · equal treatment, then national consolidation
The self-governing state initially affirmed equal treatment of the four streams while pursuing a common nation-building agenda. By the 1980s, English-medium schooling had become the common national system.
Switzerland · cantonal responsibility
The 26 cantons retain primary responsibility for schooling while coordinating qualifications and common requirements through federal and intercantonal arrangements. This is Tay’s explicit governmental analogy.
Netherlands · publicly funded philosophical diversity
The constitution protects freedom to establish schools around religious, ideological or educational beliefs, with equal public funding subject to statutory requirements. It demonstrates a state can fund difference without operating every school identically.
Singapore international schools · long-running imported laboratories
SAS dates to 1956; Singapore International School/UWCSEA to 1971; GESS’s lineage to 1971. Singapore already hosts foreign educational systems beside the national one — but mostly for populations outside the mainstream public system.
The proposed leap · make the experiment public
Instead of foreign pedagogies sitting beside Singapore education as private alternatives, bring a small number inside the public constitutional ecology, accessible to Singaporean children and accountable to the whole system.
09 · What I think

The 4 + 3 structure is much stronger than my first seven.

My first answer made the seven Schools seven abstract epistemologies: Observation, Making, Meaning, Relations, Care, Governance, Possibility. I still like those as learning lenses, running through every School. The constitutional basis of the Schools is now their history.

Your addition introduces something the abstract version lacked: time.

The four historical Schools are not theories somebody invented in a ministry workshop. They are residues of how Singapore actually became Singapore. They contain unresolved inheritances — colonialism, language, religion, migration, trade, class, community self-help, assimilation, cosmopolitanism. Precisely because they are messy, they have cultural depth.

The three Guest Schools perform the opposite function. They have no claim to ancestral legitimacy. Their legitimacy comes from difference, evidence and their capacity to produce useful disturbance.

The four roots ask

What have we inherited that the unified system made difficult to see?

They deepen memory. They stop “Singapore education” from becoming synonymous with the particular system that happened to win the twentieth-century consolidation.

The three guests ask

What are we currently unable to imagine from inside ourselves?

They introduce foreign assumptions deliberately enough that they cannot be domesticated into a one-year pilot before anyone has learned from them.

There is also something wonderfully Singaporean about this structure. We are a port. We have always metabolised the foreign. The question is whether the educational system can do that consciously: retain deep local memory while maintaining ports through which pedagogical difference continuously enters.

Not seven tribes.
Not seven rankings.
Not seven postcode systems.

Four roots. Three open ports. One commons.

And I think the most important institutional sentence may be this:

The central MOE’s job becomes stewardship of the search.
Its task is to make sure Singapore can safely sustain several serious attempts at finding the best way to educate itself.
