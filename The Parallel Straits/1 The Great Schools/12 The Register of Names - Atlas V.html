Prelude · why the old names were half-right

A name is a fossil, not a thesis.

Four of the seven names in Atlas I were doing real work. Three were describing their own institution's worldview, which no real institution has ever been permitted to do. This volume fixes the three, gives the four their etymologies, and adds the friction that names generate when the city refuses to update them.

The test for an institutional name is whether it could have been chosen by someone who did not yet know what the institution would become. Raffles Institution is named after a man. Tao Nan is named after a direction. Jit Sin, in Penang, is named after a line scratched into a washing basin three thousand years ago. Penang Free School is named after a fee structure. None of these names describe a pedagogy, and all of them have outlived the pedagogies they were attached to.

Measured against that test, Toa Keng Institution passes easily: the city's oldest school is named after a large drain, and its students have spent two hundred years converting the insult into a doctrine. The Sund Commons passes, though for a reason the earlier atlas did not notice. Al-Merican College passes only once its etymology is corrected, at which point it becomes the best name in the set.

Three names failed. Lam Keng College failed most interestingly: it is euphonious, it means nothing in particular, and it rhymes with Toa Keng, which is a collision no real city would tolerate for a century without inventing nicknames to escape it. The House of Nine Rains failed because it sounds like a name invented for a novel — plural, atmospheric, ungrounded. Kora House failed because it borrowed an instrument and appended the one English noun a school of carried knowledge would never accept.

What follows keeps the sound of the world and gives every name a cause outside itself. In four cases that means an etymology. In three it means a revision, plus the grievance the revision creates — because the second gift of renaming a school is the ninety years during which nobody complies.

A school that can explain its own name has not had it long enough. Atlas V · first principle
The system

Every house carries three names, and only one of them is official.

This is the mechanism that made the earlier atlases feel curated: each school had exactly one name, used identically by everybody. Real institutional names arrive in triplicate, and the gap between the three is where class, affection and contempt are stored.

First name

The gazette name

What appears on the deed, the statute and the envelope. Usually assigned by a registrar who misheard something, and never used by anyone inside the building. It is the name that gets a school sued.

Second name

The mouth name

What the city actually says: shorter, older, often geographic, frequently the name of a place the school left. It cannot be changed by any authority, and attempts to change it are the funniest recurring event in Straits public life.

Third name

The rude name

What rivals say, and what the school's own students say once they are fond enough of it to be cruel. Almost always a mundane object: a drain, a bowl, a bolt of cloth, a laundry, a rain gauge. Affection in this city is expressed by refusing grandeur.

Part I · the seven revised entries
Entry 01 · unchanged · gazetted 1823

Toa Keng Institution

Officially The Institution at Toa Keng; the preposition was dropped by usage, not by decree

The name records a location that no longer exists in the form that produced it: 大坑, a large storm drain cut through soft ground on the eastern edge of the old harbour, around which a subscription school was built because the land was cheap and nobody else wanted it.

The Institution has never petitioned to change the name. It has instead spent two centuries arguing that being named after drainage is the correct condition of an epistemic institution: everything the city produces eventually passes through it, unsorted, and the work is downstream.

The rude name and the mouth name have therefore fused, which is what happens when a school wins an argument about its own insult. The student paper is called The Drain without irony and with considerable pride.

The only name in the set that needed nothing. It was already a fossil.

Entry 02 · etymology corrected

Al-Merikani College

Formerly rendered Al-Merican, which invited the wrong continent

Al-Merican College

The school is named after a cloth. Merikani — also marikani, kain merikani — was unbleached cotton sheeting that moved across the Indian Ocean in such volume that its origin became its trade name, then its unit of account, then a family fortune, then a college. The founding houses were cloth factors before they were anything else, and the name is a ledger entry that got out of hand.

Everything the school teaches follows from a bolt of fabric: standard measure, credit across distance, the trust instrument that survives the death of the parties, the hospitality that makes a stranger's promise enforceable. A school named after a nation would have had to defend a nation. A school named after a fabric only has to defend the quality of the weave.

Students carry a small metal token for life, and the token is stamped with a selvedge mark rather than a crest. Rivals call the school the Bolt, which the school affects to find commercial and secretly finds accurate.

Same sound, entirely different world: the name now comes from goods, not geopolitics.

Entry 03 · second name restored

Balai Pusaka

Gazetted, against its wishes, as Sri Temenggong Institution

The house has always called itself Balai Pusaka — the hall of what is inherited. Pusaka is not heritage in the museum sense; it is the property, obligation and ritual duty that arrives together and cannot be separated. You do not own pusaka. You are next in the sequence of people responsible for it.

In 1867 a colonial registrar, requiring a name that identified an owner, entered the school under the title of the office that endowed it. The gazette name has stood ever since. The school has never once used it in its own documents, with the result that two centuries of official correspondence has been addressed to an institution that does not, internally, exist.

This is the school's favourite piece of evidence. Its whole argument is that the record and the obligation are different objects, and it has a filing cabinet proving the record was wrong from the first envelope.

The grievance is now structural rather than temperamental — it lives in the letterhead.

Entry 04 · renamed · the requested revision

Jit Sin Institute日新書院

Renamed by its own governors in 1904; the city has declined to comply for a hundred and twenty years

Lam Keng College · 南景书院

The old name was a view: 南景, southern scene, pleasant and inert. The reform decade could not live with it. The governors took the line scratched into the bathing bowl of a Shang king and preserved in the Daxue: 苟日新，日日新，又日新 — if renewed for a day, renew daily, and renew again. The school became 日新書院, Daily Renewal, romanised in the old southern pronunciation the printers were already using.

Three consequences, all useful. First, the collision with Toa Keng disappears — no two houses now share a syllable, which matters in a city that abbreviates everything. Second, the school's ideology is finally embedded in its name, which is a mistake it made deliberately and now cannot escape: a house named Renewal cannot decline a reform without appearing to betray itself, and Atlas IV's charge that Lam Keng fossilised its love of change becomes a charge readable off the signboard.

Third, and best: the district refused. The printery streets are still Lam Keng, because the neighbourhood was named before the school and saw no reason to follow it. Anyone over sixty, every taxi driver, and all four rival houses still say Lam Keng when they mean the school. The Institute finds this unbearable, since being addressed by a superseded name is a daily practical refutation of its entire epistemology.

The rude name is the Bowl — the bathing bowl of the inscription. Toa Keng cherishes it, because 君子不器: the cultivated person is not a vessel. Two centuries of insult are compressed into one syllable and a classical citation, which is exactly how this city fights.

A school called Daily Renewal, universally addressed by the name it abolished, nicknamed after a container by rivals who think it is one.

Entry 05 · singular · etymology added

The House of Nine Rain

Formerly Nine Rains, which read as weather; the singular reads as a date

The House of Nine Rains

Nine Rain is not a description of a climate. It is a day: a calendrical name in the system carried east across the Pacific by the scholarly lineage that founded the house, in which persons and institutions take the sign of the day on which they begin. Nine Rain is a founding date wearing the costume of a poem, which is why it survived translation better than anything else about the house.

The plural made it atmospheric. The singular makes it administrative, and therefore real: the house has a birthday, it is written in a calendar most of the city cannot read, and every anniversary requires a conversion argument between two systems of time that do not commensurate.

The mouth name is Rumah Hujan, rain house, which is a mistranslation the house stopped correcting around 1890. Chinese usage settled on 九雨, which repeats the same error in a second language. The rude name is the Gauge, after the rain gauges bolted to every wall of the water district, and after the house's habit of measuring things other people consider unmeasurable.

The most fantastical name in the atlas becomes the most bureaucratic: a day-sign, mistranslated twice, still on the letterhead.

Entry 06 · renamed · “House” withdrawn

The Sané-Kora Institute

Formerly Kora House; the house objected to the noun on doctrinal grounds

Kora House

The objection is precise. A house has walls, and an institution whose archive is carried in people cannot be named after a container it might lose. Every fire, seizure and eviction in the school's history has been an argument against the word. It calls itself an institute, meaning a practice rather than a premises, and it is named for the instrument and for the founding family together — Sané for the lineage that arrived with the repertoire, Kora for the twenty-one strings that carry it.

The city says Kora and always will. Students say the Room, after the acoustic examination chamber where the three-examiner oral is held: one gives testimony, one contradicts it, and the third asks what neither of them said. To have been in the Room is the whole of the school's claim on a person.

Other houses use the Room as a sneer, meaning a school that would rather listen than decide. The Institute has never rebutted this, on the grounds that a rebuttal would concede the premise.

A school that refuses the word “house” because it has been evicted enough times to take nouns seriously.

Entry 07 · unchanged · joke made explicit

The Sund Commons

The northern cooperativists named their house with the city's own word

Sund is a strait. The founders, arriving from cooperativist states in the north, named their house after the body of water it stood beside — in their own language, which produced the same word the city uses for itself. The Straits has therefore contained, since 1871, a private institution called The Straits.

The city has never decided whether this was the most arrogant act in its history or merely the most literal-minded, and the ambiguity is now a permanent feature of the joke. Sund maintains it was a translation, not a claim. Nobody believes this, including Sund.

The rude name is the Laundry, after the communal washhouses that turned a workers' district into the most desirable cooperative neighbourhood in the city. Sund students use it themselves, which has drained it of insult and annoys everybody.

Retained — the coincidence in the name is worth more than any correction.

Part II · the unlisted

The register is longer than the atlas.

Seven is a number the schools chose for themselves. A naming register cannot honour it, because registers include whatever has a name — including a timetable, a body of pilots, and the institution that quietly trains most of the city's engineers.

Not an institution · a timetable

Sekolah Petang

The shift schools have no gazette name because a shift is not a body corporate. They are recorded as “afternoon usage” of buildings belonging to other houses. Mouth name: petang, the afternoon. Rude name, used by morning students and adopted by afternoon ones: the Second Bell — now the title of the yearbook.

Not registrable · no premises

Rumah Arus

House of Currents. Cannot be registered, since registration requires an address, and its curriculum is a set of routes. It appears in statute as an unincorporated body of pilots, a phrase drafted in 1884 by a clerk who wanted the problem to go away. Its members call themselves route-keepers. Nobody has a rude name for them, which is itself a form of respect.

Nobody writes atlases about it

The Ferry Trades Institute

Jalan Basah. Founded 1921 to train pump-men, welders and boat engineers; now educates roughly two-fifths of the city's working engineers, including most of the people who will actually build whatever the Great Schools decide about water. Mouth name: Basah, meaning wet. It has no doctrine, no crest worth reproducing, and a hundred per cent employment.

Part III · six rules for naming anything else in this world
1

Name it after the ground, not the idea

Drains, hills, wet roads, quays, day-signs, cloth. If a name states a philosophy, it must have been chosen by the institution itself in a fit of self-belief — and the city must be shown refusing to use it.

2

No two houses share a syllable

Cities abbreviate. Two names that collide under abbreviation would have been separated by usage within a generation, so they must already be separate on the page.

3

Every grand name has a mundane shadow

The Drain, the Bowl, the Bolt, the Gauge, the Room, the Laundry, the Garden. Affection here is expressed by refusing grandeur, and a house without a rude name is a house nobody loves.

4

Let one name be wrong on the record

A registrar's error, uncorrected for two centuries, does more for verisimilitude than any amount of invented history. Balai Pusaka's envelopes are the model.

5

Translate badly, at least once

Rumah Hujan and 九雨 are the same mistake in two languages, and the house stopped correcting either. Worlds that translate perfectly have only one language in them.

6

Keep the old name alive in a place-name

Institutions rename themselves; districts do not. Lam Keng the neighbourhood outliving Lam Keng the college is the cheapest and most convincing kind of historical depth available.
