Premise · the argument reaches the body first

Nobody is persuaded of an epistemology. They are dressed in one at seven.

The houses believe they transmit their theories of knowledge through teaching. They mostly transmit them through cloth, food, buildings and the four minutes in a corridor — and this is why the traditions survive arguments they have already lost.

Take Sund's principle that ability creates obligation. No student has ever been convinced of it by the founding documents. What happens instead is that a child of eleven is handed a jacket with three names inked inside the hem, is told the fourth space is hers, and is expected to hand it on in a condition that permits a fifth. The doctrine arrives as a garment with a used lining. By the time she can argue about redistribution she has already spent four years unable to think of a coat as hers.

Or Temenggong's ethic of custodianship, taught less in the Balai Lama than in the removal of shoes outside it, five thousand times, before anyone explains why.

So this volume is organised by domain. Reading it school by school would repeat the atlas's mistake in a new register — nine handsome portraits, each internally consistent, each alone. Put them in one domain instead and they collide immediately: they must all clothe a body, feed it at one o'clock, decide what counts as a made thing, and put a roof over a room. The disagreements become concrete, and something unexpected surfaces — in every domain the school that supplied the city's actual habits is the one the atlas excluded.

The city's food belongs to its hawkers. The city's visual language is duplicator purple. The city's characteristic building is a two-session shift school, one plan, built two hundred and forty times and adapted badly each time by whoever was there. What the houses supply is prestige and vocabulary. What the shift schools supplied is the material culture almost everyone actually grew up inside.

Four domains follow. Each ends in the street, because that is where the objects stop belonging to institutions.

Domain 01 · Cloth

The uniform is a position.

Every school in the city dresses a body it cannot yet argue with, and each answers the same question differently: what should a garment make impossible?

The port is two degrees off the equator, so nobody can claim their uniform is climatically necessary. Every choice is therefore visible as a choice, which is why cloth in this city is read with a fluency outsiders find alarming — a tailor in Rochor can name your house from a collar seam at eleven metres.

Note what the nine actually prohibit. Prohibitions are where the doctrine lives; permissions are just fashion.

01 · Toa Keng Institution

Worsted, and one deliberate stain

Tropical worsted, four buttons, a tie nobody can defend in this climate. The school has debated the tie for a century, which is the point of the tie.

Once a year, on Founding Day, every student wears a narrow sash dyed with silt from the actual creek, which stains and does not fully wash out. Old boys' jackets can be dated by how many Founding Days they have absorbed.

Prohibits · pretending the ground was clean
02 · Al-Merikani College

The good cloth faces inward

Unstructured linen jacket, cotton over-shirt, nothing branded, nothing that photographs. The outside of a Merikani boy is the least informative surface in the city.

The lining is another matter: hand-woven, sometimes older than the wearer, occasionally cut from a grandmother's cloth. It is shown to nobody except a tailor and, once, at the signing of a first contract, deliberately, as a form of introduction.

Prohibits · wealth that can be read by a stranger
03 · Sri Temenggong Institution

Kain over trousers, and no rank on the body

A length of woven kain worn over ordinary trousers, tied by a method taught in the first week and not reissued if forgotten. Nothing on the garment indicates seniority, office, or prize.

Rank appears only in the tumpal border of the kain, which faces upward at the hem and is read by teachers. A student who has been given responsibility receives cloth — and a responsibility withdrawn means the cloth is returned, folded, without comment.

Prohibits · wearing an achievement
04 · Lan Fang College · 兰芳书院

A uniform with a date on it

Redesigned every twenty years by a student committee, over the objections of the previous committee. Nine versions exist. Each is dated at the cuff.

The consequence is that the school's alumni are visibly sorted into cohorts who dislike each other's clothes, and that a Lan Fang uniform is the only one in the city that can look old-fashioned. The college regards this as an acceptable cost and possibly the curriculum.

Prohibits · a garment that pretends to be timeless
05 · The House of Nine Rain

Clothing you can read your own year off

Undyed heavy cotton, cut for work, and a woven band at the shoulder. The band is a record: its weave holds the cohort's water and nutrient figures for the year, knotted and read by anyone trained to read it.

Repairs are worked in contrasting thread and never concealed. A third-year garment is a legible history of what its wearer did to it, which is the house's entire theory of knowledge applied to a sleeve.

Prohibits · invisible mending
06 · Sané-Kora Institute

A garment that does not interrupt

Cloth selected acoustically. Everything soft, everything quiet: soft-soled shoes, jewellery that rests silently against a desk. The uniform's specification is written in terms of sound, and the sample book is judged in a quiet room with eyes closed.

Colour is otherwise unregulated, so a Kora assembly looks like a market and sounds like an empty one.

Prohibits · being heard by accident
07 · The Sund Commons

Three names in the hem, and a fourth space

Standard issue, unremarkable, engineered to be handed down: seams generous, hems deep, buttons standard across sixty years so a 1974 jacket takes a current button.

Every garment carries a small linen tab inked with the names of previous wearers. Filling the last space on the tab is a minor scandal; a student who wears out a jacket beyond passing on writes to the school explaining how.

Prohibits · a garment being yours
Entry 00 · Sekolah Petang

A white shirt, because it is the cheapest thing that can be shared

No uniform was ever designed. A white short-sleeved shirt became universal because it could be bought anywhere, bleached, and worn by a sibling in either session.

The morning and afternoon cohorts are nonetheless distinguishable, and by nothing anyone planned: the afternoon shirt is washed at night and dries indoors, so it yellows differently. Two hundred and forty schools, one garment, and the city can still tell which half of the day you belong to.

Prohibits · nothing · which is its own kind of statement
Entry — · Rumah Arus

Whatever dries

Clothing on a working vessel is what can be wet at dawn and dry by afternoon. That is the whole specification, and the tradition keeps it exactly.

One garment does survive in eleven descriptions — a shoulder cloth wound in a manner particular to a passage, used to shade the eyes at the hour the passage requires reading. The knot is the mnemonic. Nobody now living can tie it.

Prohibits · anything that cannot be worn wet
The street · what the city does with all this

The counterfeit mud sash.

By the 1960s you could buy a TKI Founding Day sash in any market in Rochor for a fraction of a school fee, pre-stained with river silt of dubious provenance. It was worn by seventeen-year-olds who had never been inside the school, at first as mockery, then as fashion, then simply as a thing a young man in this city wears.

TKI's response passed through the predictable stages — legal threat, weary tolerance, and finally the appointment of a committee to consider whether the sash's escape was in fact the fulfilment of the motto.

What the tailors actually did.

The city's tailors are the port's real institution of comparative epistemology, and nobody has ever awarded them anything. A Rochor shop will cut you a Merikani lining into a Petang white shirt, a Sund-deep hem into a TKI blazer, a Temenggong tumpal border onto a jacket cuff.

None of the houses commissioned any of this. All of it is worn daily. The achar principle is a service industry the city built around nine schools whose clothes were always being copied.

Achar note · the mixing happens at the hem, four kilometres from any campus, for money

Domain 02 · Table

Who eats first, who eats last, and who eats standing.

A curriculum can be argued with. A serving order is absorbed at eight and never revisited.

The port's cuisine is genuinely achar: a pickle in which every ingredient is changed and the result keeps better than any of them. The schools' contribution is smaller than they think, and the sequence of a meal turns out to matter more than its contents.

01 · Toa Keng Institution

The mud cake, and an argument with it

The tuck shop's signature is a dark, silty steamed kueh of palm sugar and black glutinous rice, deliberately unattractive, sold for eighty years to fund the debating society. Alumni in three ministries are sentimental about it. It is not very good.

House custom: nothing may be eaten at the long table until someone has stated a proposition. The food is hostage to the argument, which explains a great deal about TKI's graduates and their table manners.

Sequence · proposition, then rice
02 · Al-Merikani College

The gate porridge

Every day of the year, a thin savoury rice porridge is cooked in quantity and given away at the college gate to anyone who arrives, without registration, question or thanks required. It predates the college's endowment and has never been suspended, including in the war years, when it was thinner.

Students take the gate duty in turn from age twelve. The lesson is delivered in the wrist: you serve standing, to a person who owes you nothing, and you do not learn their name.

Sequence · the stranger eats before the house
03 · Sri Temenggong Institution

One tray, five people, the youngest serving

Meals are taken on the floor from a shared tray, five to a tray, and the youngest at the tray serves the others before eating. Seniority buys you nothing except the obligation to notice whether the youngest has eaten.

The house dish is fish cooked in nothing but its own water with tamarind — a technique that cannot be improved by wealth, which is why it is the house dish.

Sequence · youngest serves, eats last, is watched
04 · Lan Fang College · 兰芳书院

The dish that became something else

A braised pork and preserved-mustard dish carried from the ancestral coast in 1847 and revised every generation: chilli by 1880, tomato by 1910, an anchovy stock nobody can source the origin of, cooked now with a pressure vessel.

The recurring argument, staged annually and never settled, is whether it is still the dish. The kitchen's answer — it is the dish because we are still arguing about it — was written on the wall in 1953 and painted over twice.

Sequence · taste, then dispute the lineage
05 · The House of Nine Rain

The closed meal

Ordinary days are ordinary. Once a year the campus eats only what its own terraces, tanks and channels produced, and the meal is preceded by the public reading of the year's water and nutrient figures. In a bad year it is a small meal, and it is not supplemented.

Everyone is fed — this is a school — and the plate is thin enough that the arithmetic stays concrete. Children learn the catchment by being slightly disappointed by it.

Sequence · the figures, then the food they permit
06 · Sané-Kora Institute

Nothing crunchy before the hearing

Two kinds of meal. The loud meal, where food is deliberately noisy and conversation must be worked for. And the quiet meal before a hearing, where the kitchen is forbidden anything brittle, because chewing masks cadence.

After the quiet meal, junior students are asked to reconstruct the table's conversation — who spoke, in what order, and what was implied but not stated. Dinner is an examination and everyone knows it.

Sequence · eat, then account for what was said
07 · The Sund Commons

Whoever cooked eats last, and no dish may need an unnamed hand

The Common Kitchen is the largest building on campus. The rota is absolute; the principal cooks four times a term.

The standing rule is stricter than it sounds: a dish may not be served if any part of its preparation was done by someone the diners cannot name. This quietly bans most catering, most convenience, and — the argument that runs every decade — most imported ingredients.

Sequence · cooks last, eats last, is named
Entry 00 · Sekolah Petang

The petang plate, eaten standing in four minutes

At one o'clock the morning cohort leaves and the afternoon cohort arrives, and for four minutes both are at the same stall. Rice, one vegetable, a spoon of gravy, an egg if the week allowed it. Eaten standing, fast, often shared with someone from the other session.

By any honest count this is the most frequently eaten meal in the city's history, and it appears in no cookbook, has no name outside the neighbourhoods, and was invented by the queue.

Sequence · four minutes, standing, both cohorts at once
Entry — · Rumah Arus

One recipe survives

Fish, cut on the boat, eaten raw with seawater and a wild lime, within an hour of the catch. The only preparation available on a vessel with no fire, and by the tradition's own account, the correct one.

It survives in the city as an expensive dish in two restaurants, garnished, plated, and served with a story that has the school's name wrong.

Sequence · catch, cut, eat · nothing between
The Concord table · the city's one shared dish

The achar nobody eats their own.

The Concord of Houses is preceded by a meal, and the meal is a single vast achar. Each house contributes one vegetable, prepared its own way, and the jar is sealed three weeks before the Concord opens.

The rule that makes it worth describing: the jar is packed by last year's students and opened by this year's. Nobody eats an achar they made. You eat the one made by people who have already left, and you pack one for people you will not meet.

And who actually cooks it.

The kitchen staff are hired for the week from the neighbourhood around whichever house is hosting, which in practice means the families of shift-school children. They are paid, fed, thanked in the programme in six-point type, and not seated.

In 2019 a Sund student proposed that the Concord's opening meal should be served by the delegates to the kitchen staff, once, and the motion was ruled out of order on procedural grounds. It is reintroduced annually. The atlas records the achar and not the motion.

Achar note · the pickle is the city's best metaphor and its catering arrangements are its worst

Domain 03 · Making

What counts as a work.

Each house has a different answer to the question of what can be finished, signed, kept, sold, or performed — and the city's actual visual language came from the school with no art department.

Not "the arts" as a subject. The prior question: what kind of object does this tradition consider a completed piece of work, and who is allowed to be its author?

01 · Toa Keng Institution

The Disputation, with tickets

TKI's characteristic art form is a public argument staged as theatre: two hours, two positions, a chair who may not intervene, and a hall that sells out. Transcripts are published. Nobody wins on the night; the decision is taken by the audience four weeks later by post, which is the innovation.

The form has been borrowed by television, and the school is bitter about the version with a studio audience.

A work is · a position defended in front of people who may change their minds later
02 · Al-Merikani College

The illuminated ledger, and everyone else's paintings

Merikani makes almost nothing and holds almost everything. Its one native form is the illuminated contract: a signed agreement written and ornamented as a work in its own right, hung in the house of each party. Some are four hundred years old and still binding.

Otherwise the College's contribution to the arts is patronage, which is to say the port's three great museums are Merikani collections with civic names on the door.

A work is · a promise made beautiful enough to be kept
03 · Sri Temenggong Institution

The couplet that must name its loss

The house verse form is two lines: the first states what has changed, the second names what was destroyed to allow it. A poem with only the first line is, in this tradition, a boast.

Two centuries later, when Lan Fang proposes rewriting cities every twenty years, the constraint that saves the idea — nothing may be transformed before someone can explain what would be lost — turns out to be a verse form, applied to a constitution.

A work is · a change with its cost written into its form
04 · Lan Fang College · 兰芳书院

Cinema, obviously

The swallow leaves and returns, and a film is the only form that can hold both. The city's film industry is Lan Fang's — its studios, its cutting rooms, its habit of remaking its own hits every fifteen years with a different politics.

House genre: the return film, in which someone comes back after twenty years and the place has changed correctly. The school's own critics complain it has made the same film nine times. Its directors consider that a series.

A work is · a version, dated, expected to be remade
05 · The House of Nine Rain

Textiles that are also datasets

Weaving here is not craft adjacent to science; it is notation. A hanging in the Nine Rain gallery is simultaneously an artwork and a legible record of a catchment year, and it is judged on both.

The house's gallery labels give material, maker, year, and water consumed in production. Two celebrated pieces were withdrawn by their own makers when the figures were recalculated.

A work is · an object that can be read back as measurement
06 · Sané-Kora Institute

The recital in which the omissions are performed

A Kora recital has two parts. The account, given by one voice with the twenty-one-string instrument. Then the second part, in which another performer renders what the first left out, performing the silences in sequence, at their original length.

Recording is prohibited, and the prohibition is the doctrine: an archive that runs without a living person will eventually be consulted by people who cannot be argued with.

A work is · an account plus the performance of its gaps · unrecordable
07 · The Sund Commons

No signature, and a chair that outlasted everyone

Works are credited to a year: Commons Workshop, 1978. Individual credit is held to distort what people choose to make.

The result is that Sund produced the most influential design objects in the city — a stacking chair, a stair detail, a repairable kettle — and the world outside spent forty years trying to find out who designed them, which the school finds a fascinating illustration of its point and a mild embarrassment at the annual auction of its own out-of-print catalogues.

A work is · an anonymous object that can be repaired by its user
Entry 00 · Sekolah Petang

Duplicator purple · the city's real visual language

The shift schools had a mimeograph, a stencil, one colour of ink and a teacher with an hour. Out of that came the port's actual graphic vernacular: the smudged violet line, the hand-lettered heading, the diagram redrawn slightly differently each printing, the correction in the margin that the next class inherits.

Every hawker sign, election poster, funeral notice, coffee-shop menu and union pamphlet in this city descends from it. Not one museum collected the stencils. When the last duplicator went, the aesthetic survived as a nostalgic typeface sold by a design studio in Merikani's old quarter.

A work is · a sheet, corrected by whoever used it next, authored by nobody
Entry — · Rumah Arus

Eleven passages, performed once a year

The route is the art form: a sequence of swell, bird, cloud, smell, bottom-colour and star, held in verse because verse survives a wet crossing and paper does not. Composition was permitted; a new passage could be taught only after being sailed three times.

Eleven remain. They are performed once a year by a man in his eighties who says, each time, before beginning, that he is no longer certain of the fourth. Nobody can correct him. That sentence is now part of the performance, and will be all that survives of it.

A work is · a passage that is true only if a vessel can still be taken through it
The street · what got out

Nine traditions, and the city took the cheap one.

Given a choice between brass-engraved Latin, illuminated contracts, unrecordable recitals, gallery textiles with water footnotes, anonymous Sund furniture and a smudged purple stencil, the city adopted the stencil — because it was reproducible by anyone with an hour and no permission.

This is the finding of the whole volume, arrived at by accident. Material culture does not spread from the most sophisticated tradition. It spreads from the most copyable one.

The awkward corollary.

Kora is right that an unrecordable archive cannot be consulted by people who will not be argued with. Kora is also the reason its own art form is nearly gone, while the shift schools' aesthetic — which nobody protected, valued or archived — is on every street in the port.

Both facts are consequences of the same decision about reproduction. Neither house would trade.

Achar note · the city's visual identity was set by a machine that cost less than a Merikani lining

Domain 04 · Building

The plan that was built two hundred and forty times.

Nine architectures, of which the atlas describes seven, and the eighth is the most-constructed building type in the port's history.

Architecture is where these institutions are most articulate and least honest, because a campus is the one artifact that lasts long enough to be mistaken for the doctrine itself.

01 · Toa Keng Institution

Additions that refuse to match

A Victorian tropical quadrangle, continually invaded by laboratories, archives and extensions, none of which pretends to be contemporary with the original. Every era's addition is legible as an argument with the era before it.

The school has twice refused to commission a masterplan, on the stated grounds that a resolved campus would misrepresent the institution.

Principle · the building is a transcript of its disputes
02 · Al-Merikani College

A door that tells you nothing

Courtyards, arcades, teak shutters, geometric screens. From the street: a wall, a modest door, no indication of what is behind it. The wealth is entirely interior and entirely spatial — you discover it as depth, one court after another.

The screen is the college's argument in one component: it permits a person inside to see out and a person outside to see nothing, and it is the most beautiful object in the building.

Principle · privacy as a form of manners, and of power
03 · Sri Temenggong Institution

Landscape first, and an axis that refuses to run straight

Pavilions, water, old trees, raised roofs. Ceremonial routes bend, deliberately, so that nobody arrives at anything without having been slowed down by it.

The most sacred structure is the low timber Balai Lama, which is also the least impressive and would be demolished by any consultant asked to improve the site. Shoes come off. No ruler or headmaster is depicted inside; the wall records teachers.

Principle · the ground is the institution; buildings are guests on it
04 · Lan Fang College · 兰芳书院

Designed for its own demolition

Rebuilt in large part every twenty years, with structures detailed for removal — bolted, foundations reused, a stated design life on a plate by each entrance.

The demolition ceremony is the school's most attended event. A student reads the account of what this building did well and what will be lost by its removal, out loud, before anything is touched. The account is filed. Twice in the archive it has stopped the demolition.

Principle · a building must be able to be let go of, on the record
05 · The House of Nine Rain

Infrastructure made deliberately legible

Stepped gardens, open channels, stone passages, an observatory court recalibrated to the equatorial sky. Water is never buried: every litre can be followed by eye from where it enters the site to where it leaves.

The one concealed system is the drainage of the staff quarters, added in 1961 by a contractor. Nobody has ever explained the exception and the house does not enjoy being asked.

Principle · nothing structural or metabolic may be hidden
06 · Sané-Kora Institute

Rooms measured in speech

The courtyards are sized by voice: the boundary of the great court is the furthest point at which an unamplified adult speaking normally can still be understood, established by testing and marked in the paving.

Listening rooms are shaded, soft-floored and deliberately visually dull. Acoustic design is the scholarly infrastructure here, and the House's architects are trained singers.

Principle · the plan is a diagram of who can hear whom
07 · The Sund Commons

Modest, repairable, ruinously expensive

Timber, brick, glass, standardised components, no monumental gate, a Common Kitchen more important than the principal's office. Everything is designed to be repaired by the people who use it, with a parts store on site.

It is also an international design icon and costs a fortune to maintain, which the school reports in its annual accounts under a line item it insists on calling the price of appearing not to spend.

Principle · nothing monumental, everything maintainable, published cost
Entry 00 · Sekolah Petang

One plan, 240 times, adapted badly by whoever was there

A standard double-session plan: two flanking blocks, a hard courtyard, a covered walkway, a canteen sized for half a school because only half is ever present. It was drawn once and stamped out across the port with no adjustment for orientation, so roughly a third of the classrooms face the afternoon sun and are unbearable in the session named after it.

Every adaptation was local, unbudgeted and undocumented: a raised louvre here, a tree planted in the wrong place that turned out to be the right place, a wall knocked through by a principal who is still spoken about. The city's best architects almost all learned in these buildings, and what they learned was how to fix a plan you did not draw.

Its greatest space is four minutes long: one o'clock, when the covered walkway holds both cohorts at once and the whole thing is briefly a city.

Principle · none stated · the most-built and least-designed architecture in the port
Entry — · Rumah Arus

No architecture, which was the disqualification

A hut on a beach for the wet season, dismantled when the season turned. The school proper had no premises, because the premises were the passage.

The one structure associated with it — a stone beacon on the eastern reef, built by fishing families and maintained for two centuries — was never owned by the house, is now a heritage site, and its plaque credits the colonial harbour board.

The street · what the port is actually made of

Shophouse, walkway, void deck.

The city is not built of quadrangles, courts, terraces or commons. It is built of shophouses, covered walkways, market halls and the undercrofts of housing blocks — none of which any of the nine houses invented, all of which the houses' own architects grew up in.

The transfer runs the other way from how it is usually told. Nine Rain did not teach the port to make infrastructure legible; the port's open monsoon drains, which everyone can see and children play in, made the house's doctrine immediately intelligible to a local audience. Temenggong did not teach the city to slow an arrival; that is the five-foot way.

The one thing the houses gave back.

Words. The city acquired a vocabulary for what it already had: Kora's voice boundary is now used by market hall designers, Lan Fang's design life plate is on public buildings, Sund's parts store is a planning requirement, and Temenggong's bent axis is taught in every school of architecture in the port.

That is not nothing. Naming a practice is what allows it to be defended in a committee. But the practice was in the drain, the walkway and the shift-school courtyard first, and the naming came from institutions that had the time to name things.

Achar note · the houses supplied the vocabulary; the port supplied the buildings

Coda · what the objects settled

The argument was never in the argument.

Four domains, and the same result in each. The Concord is where the city stages its intelligence; the hem, the one o'clock plate, the duplicator stencil and the covered walkway are where the city's actual dispositions were formed. A student arrives at the Concord already carrying a serving order, a rule about whose coat this is, and a habit about whether infrastructure should be visible. Those were installed by objects, years earlier, without argument, which is why they win.

Two consequences worth holding onto. The first is that material culture is the most durable transmission a tradition has, and also the least deliberate — none of the nine designed the thing that carried it best. TKI's sash escaped into a market. Sund's chair went anonymous and became furniture. The shift schools' stencil became the visual language of a nation without anyone deciding it should.

The second is harder. Reproducibility beats sophistication, every time, in every domain. The forms that spread were the cheap ones, the copyable ones, the ones that needed no permission — and the houses that most carefully protected their practice are the ones with the least of it left. Kora's prohibition on recording is intellectually correct and has cost it nearly everything. Rumah Arus needed no building, and having no building is what allowed a clerk to erase it.

Which suggests the fifth domain this volume has not written, and probably should: language. Whose grammar does the Concord actually run in, who does the translating, and what happens to a tradition whose key term has no equivalent in the language the minutes are kept in. The translator is the city's real eighth profession and appears in no atlas so far.

or it is transmitted by nobody.
