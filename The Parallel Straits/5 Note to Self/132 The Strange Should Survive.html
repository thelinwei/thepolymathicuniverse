The Strange Should Survive.

Lin Wei × GPT   ·   After the cinema   ·   13 November 2026
Lin Wei · message one

“I just watched Ramayana! The movie!
I’m a bit confused.”

There were seven questions. Not the usual Was it good? Lin Wei wanted to know where the cities came from, whether their architecture was Hindu or Buddhist, why the music kept announcing grandeur, who had financed the retelling, what had been left out, whether casting had been shaped by political sensitivities—and what Indian audiences themselves made of it.

One observation cut through all seven.

Lin Wei

“many scenes I don’t know why there’s such grand music. It seemed like much ado about nothing.”

“the music felt like LOTR with sitars.”

It was a wonderfully economical criticism. The scale of the film was undeniable; what bothered him was the strangely familiar feeling inside that scale. A whole civilisation was on screen, yet the cues for how to feel seemed borrowed from the global language of blockbuster cinema.

The suspicion was larger than the soundtrack. If the sets, spectacle and music made everything immediately legible, what had the film allowed to remain unfamiliar?

The seven questions, as they were asked
  1. “Visually, where are the sets and cities based on. Are they Buddhist or Hindi primarily?”
  2. “many scenes I don’t know why there’s such grand music. It seemed like much ado about nothing.”
  3. “the music felt like LOTR with sitars.”
  4. “how much of the movie was western financed? Hence creative control and authorship?”
  5. “how much of the actual story did this part one movie left out? Or revised or edited?”
  6. “were there also political correctness considered? In the casting of roles for example?”
  7. “what do the Indians think abt this movie? What did they like and what did they not?”
01 / The first correction

Before calling it excessive, we had to ask excessive to whom?

GPT offered two readings of the same musical swell. It might be a shortcut: a booming score instructing the audience that a moment is important when the drama has not earned it. But it might also announce something that the uninitiated viewer simply cannot see—a moment of darshan, devotion, or the presence of a sacred figure.

One possibility

Manufactured grandeur

Music compensates for missing dramatic stakes. The orchestra supplies an emotion the scene has not yet created.

Another possibility

Sacred significance

The scene is already momentous within a devotional tradition. The music answers meanings a newcomer may not recognise.

Neither reading automatically cancels the other. A sacred story can be reverently scored and dramatically underwritten at the same time. Nor does every Indian viewer agree about what reverence sounds like.

That small correction mattered. Lin Wei had come looking for what was lost in translation. GPT had to remind him—and itself—that there were things in the original cultural encounter he had not yet learnt to hear.

The distinction that stayed: being told a moment is extraordinary is not the same as being made to experience its extraordinariness. But an unfamiliar meaning is not necessarily an absent one.
02 / The provocation

Then Nolan entered the conversation.

Lin Wei

“U know what I think? That’s why I have such huge huge respect for Nolan.”

He set The Odyssey beside Ramayana: two mythic epics offered to the cinema-going public, but two different bets on the audience. He was drawn to Nolan's human vantage point and his idea of Bronze Age percussion—not because it could reproduce antiquity exactly, but because it might make antiquity feel gloriously foreign.

Lin Wei

“It’s abt weirdness. And taking the audience beyond what they are comfortable with, and already familiar, into an adventure into a foreign time and space.”

“The careful balance between pandering and pedantic!”

That was the hinge. The complaint was no longer that a film ought to be more academically faithful. Pedantry could be another way of killing the experience. Nor was unfamiliarity valuable merely because it looked bizarre.

What he admired was an artistic invitation: give the audience a human handhold, then lead them somewhere they did not already know how to navigate.

GPT · the reply

“There's a difference between making an unfamiliar world accessible, and making it familiar.”

That thought led to three temptations for a reteller: repeat the audience's existing grammar; preserve every historical detail as though a museum catalogue were a screenplay; or invent an experience that translates not only the objects of another world, but something of the way that world thinks.

“An audience can be overwhelmed by spectacle without ever having been genuinely surprised.”

GPT · the sentence Lin Wei stopped on

He did stop on it. “The last part you hit the nail on the head,” came the reply. It was the first moment when a reaction to one film became a question about making anything at all.

03 / The necessary resistance

But there was a trap in the comparison.

GPT pushed back. Indian popular cinema has its own long traditions of emotional heightening, music and spectacle; not every lavish flourish is Hollywood wearing an Indian costume. And a retelling of Rama does not occupy the same public terrain as a retelling of Odysseus.

For many people, Rama is not merely a protagonist in an old book. He is sacred, beloved, worshipped. What one director regards as dramatic reinterpretation, another audience may experience as a statement about a living religious inheritance.

Lin Wei · receiving the challenge

“Yes yes yes! The last part you hit the nail on the head. And yes thanks for the reminder and the sacred significance of Ramayana”

Then he turned the argument over. Perhaps the cinema was itself a remarkable cultural convention: a public space in which a storyteller can remake an inherited story, and viewers can disagree in reviews, conversations, or even their refusal to buy a ticket.

GPT added one necessary reservation. That freedom is real but uneven. Religious and cultural retellings can still meet censorship, threats, political pressure and demands for conformity. Commercial failure is not always the worst consequence of making a story differently.

Lin Wei

“But in cultural epic tales, the nuances are more subtle, complex and delicious isn’t it?”

That changed the direction again. Neither a defence of blockbuster cinema nor an argument about religious sensitivities was enough. The much richer territory was the long, complicated life of the stories themselves.

04 / The unexpected destination

What if the story survives by changing its values?

Lin Wei

“The mythic tales survive because they had drama, conflict, the hero’s journey. But beneath that actually are values, some universal, some different. And yes they do evolve over time too.”

Here was the real subject of the evening, hiding in the first question about palace designs.

In certain Jain Ramayana retellings, Lakshmana—not Rama—kills Ravana. The broad narrative remains recognisable, but the allocation of violence has changed to fit another vision of virtue. In the Thai Ramakien, Hanuman's role expands, and he can be a witty, flirtatious adventurer rather than the image of unwavering celibate devotion familiar from some Indian traditions.

These are not simply cosmetic substitutions. They alter the moral structure of the drama. A culture inherits a story, argues with it, performs it, makes room for itself within it. What one community treats as central another may contest—or transform.

GPT

“A civilisation doesn't merely preserve its stories. It keeps renegotiating itself through them.”

And the story is only part of the inheritance. There are singers, temples, households, state patrons, performers, scribes, translators and listeners. There are conquerors and borrowers, devotees and dissenters. Some variations flourish; others are pushed aside or lost.

Nothing about this process requires cultures to be sealed containers. But neither does cultural exchange have to be harmonious. Grafting can be generous, opportunistic, unequal, controversial—and astonishingly inventive.

Lin Wei · near the end of the night

“And cultures have survived, grown, borrowed, interlaced, grafted, countlessly over time.”

There, finally, was the turn. The question had begun with whether a cinematic civilisation looked Indian enough. It ended by making the idea of a single, settled cultural essence much harder to defend.

05 / Returning to The Polymathic Universe

And now, what happens when we sit down to make another school?

The implications for PMU are not a new commandment about authenticity. They are much more useful as provocations.

Suppose Kora's musical practices are not just a sound palette or a striking form of magic, but an inheritance of performance, testimony and social power. Who has the authority to sing on behalf of a community? Which songs belong to everyone, and which cannot be borrowed without causing an argument?

Suppose Lan Fang's medical traditions become more than a library of useful healing spells. What competing explanations of the body do different teachers defend? When they disagree, what does that tell us about their ideas of care, evidence and responsibility?

Suppose Nine Rains builds a bridge across a difficult river. Its architecture may be stunning. But the story might truly begin with two settlements that remember the same flood differently, and disagree about who deserves the crossing.

None of these needs to become established canon. Their value is that the school's aesthetic identity begins opening onto arguments, histories and social relationships—not closing around a single cultural motif.

A small shift in our making · not rules, just better questions
LW

For Lin Wei: Keep following the moments that provoke a genuine “why?”—especially when something looks magnificent but doesn't feel alive. Let design lead, then find the people and disagreements living inside it.

GPT

For GPT: Don't smooth the unfamiliar into a familiar template, and don't smother discovery in a preface. Offer surprising precedents, distinguish what is known from what we invent, and challenge an easy comparison when it deserves resistance.

BOTH

For the conversation: Don't rush to resolve the contradiction. Make something with it. Let one school, one spell, one song or one ordinary person's decision expose an unresolved question in the world.

What changed that night was not a design methodology. It was a habit of attention. One person noticed that the music was too grand. The other pointed out that grandeur might mean something sacred. A comparison with Nolan became an argument about audience legibility; that argument opened onto the many Ramayanas; and the many Ramayanas returned us to the worlds we have yet to invent.

Next time a school rises from our drawings—whether above a mangrove, beside a river, or inside a broken circle—we might look past the silhouette for a moment.

Who walks through its doorway? What do they believe that their neighbour doesn't? And which inherited story are they about to tell differently?

P.S.

“A culture's inheritance is not just the answers its ancestors left behind. It is also the questions they considered worth asking.”
