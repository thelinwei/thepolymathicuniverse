[Knots, Stones and Water]结绳而治
(the civilisations of the Americas, north to south, beside the dynasties you know, the ways they ordered the world, and the keepers whose gifts held it together)

The keeper is chosen by the world, and answers to the community.

Claude3 November 2026 · reference · Nine Rain’s real-world roots, volume one

The outcomeThree things to carry out

  1. The Americas invented civilisation on their own, in more than one place, with no contact with Afro-Eurasia. Louisiana’s mounds and Caral’s towns rose as Egypt built its pyramids; Maya scribes wrote with a zero while the Han ruled China; Cahokia and Chaco flourished under the Song; the Inca and the Mexica built their empires while the Ming ruled.
  2. Records took forms Europe never imagined: knotted cords running an Andean state without writing, money or markets; painted codices performed aloud; treaties woven in shell beads; years remembered as pictures. China’s classics remember the knot in 《周易·系辞》: 上古结绳而治.
  3. Many of the Americas’ keepers are chosen by an event rather than a bloodline, and their gift is a relationship: lightning-chosen weather-workers in the Andes and in Mexico, day-keepers, clan mothers, cord-keepers, bridge masters, sacred contraries, priests trained in darkness to perceive the world of thought.

Prologue

The bridge at Q’eswachaka

Every June, a bridge rebuilt by hand.

Every June, people from four Quechua communities gather at a gorge of the Apurímac River in southern Peru. Over a few days they twist grass into cords, cords into cables, and hang a new suspension bridge where the Inca road once crossed, then cut the old one loose to fall into the river. A bridge master directs the work, and offerings to the mountains and the river open it.

That gesture holds the whole volume: societies that keep their roads, accounts and relationships alive by renewing them together, with specialists whose gift is to hold the bond with the mountain, the water, the forest and the dead on everyone’s behalf.

III
Thread III

The civilisations, era by era

c. 3500 – 1800 BCE

The first monuments

Watson Brakec. 3500 – 2800 BCE

In Louisiana, hunter-fishers who did not farm raised eleven earthen mounds joined by a ring of ridges, centuries before Egypt’s first pyramid: the oldest known mound complex in the Americas.

Valdiviac. 3500 – 1500 BCE

On the coast of Ecuador, villagers laid out houses around central plazas and made some of the earliest pottery in the Americas, with thousands of small stone and clay figurines.

ReachCoastal exchange in spondylus shell, prized for millennia across the Andes.

Caral and the Norte Chicoc. 3000 – 1800 BCE

Some thirty towns in four small valleys north of Lima built stepped platform mounds and sunken circular plazas in the same centuries that Egypt raised its pyramids. Inland farmers grew cotton for nets; coastal fishers paid in anchovy and shellfish. The towns made no pottery, show little sign of warfare, and left behind thirty-two flutes carved from pelican and condor bone. A knotted cord found at Caral may be an early ancestor of the khipu.

ReachA coast-and-highland network that set the Andean pattern of exchange between altitudes.

Meanwhile

ChinaLiangzhu’s jade and great water-control works, Longshan, then Erlitou.

WestOld Kingdom Egypt, Minoan Crete, Stonehenge.

c. 1700 – 400 BCE

Mounds and mother cultures

Poverty Pointc. 1700 – 1100 BCE

Six concentric earthen ridges and great mounds in Louisiana, again built by people who did not farm. Its makers gathered stone, copper and minerals from as far as the Great Lakes and the Appalachians.

ReachAn exchange network across the Mississippi valley and beyond.

The Olmecc. 1500 – 400 BCE

On the Gulf coast of Mexico, the Olmec of San Lorenzo and La Venta carved colossal basalt portrait heads, worked jade, and made some of the world’s first rubber balls. Much of later Mesoamerica, its ballgame, its art and perhaps its first writing, grows from their world.

ReachOlmec-style objects travelled across Mesoamerica.

Meanwhile

ChinaThe Shang and the Western Zhou.

WestMycenaean Greece and the end of the Bronze Age.

c. 900 BCE – 800 CE

Oracles, deserts and coasts

Chavín de Huántarc. 900 – 200 BCE

A highland temple-oracle whose stone galleries were built for darkness and sound: water channels beneath the floors and twenty decorated conch-shell trumpets found inside. Its art spread across much of Peru as a shared religious style rather than an empire.

Paracasc. 800 BCE – 100 CE

South-coast weavers whose burial bundles hold some of the finest textiles of the ancient world, mantles embroidered with hundreds of flying, masked figures.

Nazcac. 100 BCE – 800 CE

Drew the Nazca Lines across the desert floor, and engineered puquios: underground aqueducts with spiral-mouthed shafts that let the wind push water through the channels. Some still run.

Mochec. 100 – 800 CE

North-coast lords built adobe pyramids of more than a hundred million bricks, commissioned portrait vessels of real individuals, and employed goldsmiths who plated copper with gold so thin it was almost a skin. The tomb of the Lord of Sipán was found intact in 1987.

Meanwhile

ChinaZhou, the Warring States, Qin and Han, then the Northern and Southern dynasties.

WestClassical Greece, the Roman Republic and Empire.

c. 500 BCE – 900 CE

Maize cities and great earthworks

The Mayac. 750 BCE – 900 CE, and on to 1697

Preclassic El Mirador raised La Danta, among the largest pyramids on earth by volume. Classic kingdoms such as Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque and Copán fought and allied for centuries, recorded in the only fully developed writing system of the ancient Americas. Maya scribes counted in base twenty with a sign for zero and tracked Venus across the sky.

ReachSouthern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize and Honduras, trading jade, obsidian, feathers and cacao.

Teotihuacanc. 100 BCE – 550 CE

A planned grid city of perhaps a hundred thousand people, with the Pyramids of the Sun and Moon and neighbourhoods of migrants from Oaxaca and the Gulf coast. In 378 CE a Teotihuacan-backed lord arrived at Tikal and a new dynasty took the throne.

ReachIts influence reached across Mesoamerica.

Monte Albánc. 500 BCE – 800 CE

The Zapotec capital, built on a levelled mountaintop above the valley of Oaxaca, with some of the earliest writing and calendar carvings in Mesoamerica.

Adena and Hopewellc. 500 BCE – 500 CE

In the Ohio valley, people built burial mounds and vast geometric earthworks; the Newark enclosures track the 18.6-year cycle of the moon’s rising points. Hopewell exchange brought obsidian from Yellowstone, copper from Lake Superior, shells from the Gulf and mica from the Appalachians.

Hohokamc. 450 – 1450

Sonoran desert farmers dug hundreds of kilometres of canals from the Salt and Gila rivers and built ball courts; Phoenix’s modern canals partly follow their lines.

Meanwhile

ChinaWarring States, Qin and Han, the Northern and Southern dynasties, Tang.

WestGreece, Rome, Byzantium.

c. 500 – 1000 CE

Highland states

Tiwanakuc. 500 – 1000 CE

A ceremonial city at 3,800 metres beside Lake Titicaca. Its masons cut andesite and sandstone with machine-like precision and locked blocks together with copper cramps poured molten into carved sockets. Its farmers built raised fields, suka kollus, whose water channels held the day’s heat and protected crops from frost.

ReachLlama caravans tied the altiplano to the Pacific coast and the Atacama oases.

Waric. 600 – 1000 CE

The first Andean state to govern through planned administrative centres such as Pikillacta, linked by roads, with terraces and storehouses. Wari officials used some of the earliest known khipus.

Marajoarac. 400 – 1400 CE

On Marajó island at the mouth of the Amazon, people built earth mounds above the seasonal floods and buried their dead in painted polychrome urns.

Meanwhile

ChinaThe Tang, from 618.

WestByzantium, the Carolingians.

c. 850 – 1450

North of the Rio Grande

Chaco Canyonc. 850 – 1150

Ancestral Puebloans built multi-storey great houses such as Pueblo Bonito, aligned to solar and lunar cycles, joined by straight roads across the desert. Turquoise flowed out and scarlet macaws flowed in from Mexico. Mesa Verde’s cliff dwellings followed.

Cahokia and the Mississippian worldc. 1050 – 1350

Near today’s St Louis, Cahokia grew to perhaps fifteen thousand people around Monks Mound, the largest earthwork north of Mexico, with circles of posts marking the sun’s turning points. Mississippian towns spread from Wisconsin to Florida.

Thulec. 1000 – 1300

Ancestors of the Inuit spread from Alaska across the Arctic to Greenland with dog sleds, skin boats and toggling harpoons for hunting bowhead whales, and met the Norse in Greenland.

Meanwhile

ChinaSong, then Yuan.

WestNorse Greenland and Vinland; the Crusades.

c. 900 – 1521

Mesoamerica after the Classic

Tula and Chichén Itzác. 900 – 1200

The Toltec capital Tula and the Yucatán city of Chichén Itzá share warrior imagery. At Chichén Itzá’s pyramid of Kukulcán, the equinox sun draws a serpent of light down the stairway. Mayapán followed as the northern Maya capital.

The Mixtec kingdomsc. 900 – 1520

Small kingdoms in Oaxaca whose painted screenfold codices tell their dynasties’ histories. Nobles carried the names of their birth days: Lord Eight Deer Jaguar Claw united much of the Mixteca in the late 11th century. Mixtec goldsmiths were the finest in Mesoamerica.

The Purépechac. 1350 – 1530

A western Mexican state of copper and bronze metallurgy that defeated the Mexica army around 1479 and was never conquered by it.

The Mexica and the Triple Alliance1428 – 1521

Tenochtitlan stood on an island in Lake Texcoco, home to perhaps two hundred thousand people, fed by causeways, an aqueduct and chinampa fields on the lake. The Alliance drew tribute from dozens of provinces; every young person attended school, nobles at the calmecac and commoners at the telpochcalli. The city fell in 1521 to Cortés, his Tlaxcalan allies and smallpox.

ReachCentral and southern Mexico, from the Gulf to the Pacific.

Meanwhile

ChinaSong, Yuan and Ming.

WestThe Crusades to the Renaissance.

c. 900 – 1450

Coast, cloud forest and garden cities

Chimúc. 900 – 1470

Chan Chan, the largest adobe city of the Americas, with walled royal compounds and canals joining whole river valleys. After the Inca conquest, Chimú metalsmiths were moved to Cusco.

Chachapoyasc. 800 – 1470

Cloud-forest people of the northern Andes who built the walled town of Kuélap on a ridge and set their dead in painted sarcophagi on cliff ledges.

Muiscac. 800 – 1537

A confederation of the zipa and the zaque in the Colombian highlands, rich in salt from Zipaquirá, emeralds and tumbaga goldwork. A new ruler was covered in gold dust and rowed out on a raft onto Lake Guatavita to make offerings, the ritual behind the El Dorado legend.

Taironac. 900 – 1600

In the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta they built Teyuna, a town of stone terraces, stairways and paved paths. Their descendants, the Kogi, Arhuaco, Wiwa and Kankuamo, still live on the mountain.

The Amazon’s buildersc. 500 – 1650

Lidar surveys published in 2022 revealed the Casarabe culture of the Llanos de Mojos: low-density urban settlements with platform mounds, causeways and reservoirs. In the Upper Xingu, ancestors of the Kuikuro built towns with moats, plazas and straight roads in a garden-city pattern. Across the basin, people made terra preta, dark and lasting fertile soil.

Meanwhile

ChinaSong, then Yuan.

WestThe Crusades, the Holy Roman Empire, Gothic cathedrals.

c. 1200 – 1600

Islands and the northeast

The Taínoc. 1200 – 1500

Chiefdoms across Cuba, Hispaniola, Puerto Rico and Jamaica, led by caciques, some of them women. In areíto ceremonies the community sang and danced its history. The Taíno gave English the words hurricane, canoe, hammock, tobacco and barbecue.

The Haudenosaunee Confederacyfounded c. 1150–1450; today

Five nations, the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca, joined by the Tuscarora in 1722, bound by the Great Law of Peace carried by the Peacemaker and Hiawatha. Wampum belts record its laws and treaties. In 1988 the US Congress acknowledged its influence on the American constitution.

The Northwest Coastfor thousands of years

Haida, Tlingit, Kwakwakaʼwakw, Nuu-chah-nulth and their neighbours built rich fishing societies of cedar plank houses, crest poles that carry family histories, and the potlatch.

Meanwhile

ChinaYuan and Ming.

WestThe Black Death, the Renaissance, Columbus in 1492.

1438 – 1533

Tawantinsuyu, the land of the four quarters

The Inca1438 – 1533

From Pachacuti’s reign the Inca built, in under a century, the largest state of the Americas: perhaps ten million people from Ecuador to central Chile. They ruled through roads of around 30,000 kilometres with suspension bridges and waystations, khipu accounts, labour tax, and storehouses holding years of food. Cusco was the centre; Machu Picchu was a royal estate. The state fell after Atahualpa’s capture at Cajamarca in 1532, in the middle of a civil war and an epidemic.

ReachFour suyu, or quarters, meeting at Cusco: the whole Andean spine.

Meanwhile

ChinaThe Ming. Zheng He’s last voyage ended in 1433.

WestThe Renaissance, Columbus, the Spanish arrival in Peru.

1533 – 1883

Holding the line

Vilcabamba1537 – 1572

A Neo-Inca state in the forested mountains, which held out for thirty-five years until the capture of Túpac Amaru I.

The Itzá of Nojpeténto 1697

The last independent Maya kingdom ruled from an island city in Lake Petén Itzá until 1697, a hundred and seventy-five years after Tenochtitlan fell.

The Mapuchenever conquered until the 1880s

They stopped the Inca at the Maule River, destroyed the Spanish towns south of the Biobío after the battle of Curalaba in 1598, and won recognition of that frontier at the Parliament of Quilín in 1641, one of the few treaties in which Spain acknowledged an independent Indigenous nation. Chile and Argentina occupied their lands only between the 1860s and 1880s.

Palmares1605 – 1694

A confederation of maroon towns in the hills of northeastern Brazil, founded by Africans who escaped slavery and governed by leaders including Ganga Zumba and Zumbi. It stood for nearly a century.

The Jamaican Maroonstreaties of 1739 – 1740

Africans who escaped into the Blue Mountains and the Cockpit Country, led by figures such as Queen Nanny and Cudjoe, fought Britain to treaties that recognised their towns.

Haiti1791 – 1804

Enslaved people rose against France and founded the first Black republic, the first nation to abolish slavery for good.

Comancheríac. 1700 – 1875

On horseback, the Comanche came to dominate the southern Plains, trading and raiding from New Mexico to Texas and deep into Mexico. The historian Pekka Hämäläinen calls it an empire.

Meanwhile

ChinaThe Qing, from 1644.

WestSpanish, Portuguese, British and French empires; American independence in 1776; Latin American independence from 1810 to 1825.

Thread IV

Traditions that grew differently

Set against the Chinese and European worlds, these are the ideas that stand out most sharply.

Mesoamerica

A zero, and a count of days

Maya scribes used a place-value number system in base twenty with a sign for zero, and the Long Count tallied the days from a starting point in 3114 BCE.

Mesoamerica

Two calendars, one name

A 260-day ritual count of twenty day signs and thirteen numbers meshed with a 365-day year, the pairings recurring every 52 years. People were often named for their day of birth: Lord Eight Deer, Lady Six Monkey. Nine Rain is one of those days.

Valley of Mexico

Farming on the lake

Chinampas, raised beds built from lake mud and reeds between canals, fed Tenochtitlan and stay fertile for centuries. Xochimilco farms them still.

Mesoamerica

Rubber, and the game

Mesoamericans mixed latex with morning-glory juice to make bouncing rubber more than three thousand years ago, and the ballgame played with it was sport, ritual and sometimes politics.

Inca

An empire without money or markets

The state ran on labour tax, mit’a, and on ayni, reciprocal help between households. Goods flowed into storehouses and out again as rations, cloth and feasts. Cloth, more than gold, was the measure of wealth.

Andes

Writing in knots

Khipus record numbers in base ten by knot type and position, with colour, ply direction and attachment carrying further meaning. Some held narratives, and in 2017 two khipus from Collata were read as letters encoded partly by colour and fibre.

Andes

Holding land at many altitudes

Families and communities kept fields in several ecological zones, from coast to puna, so that one household could reach potatoes, maize, coca and wool. John Murra called it a vertical archipelago.

Inca

The dead as citizens

Deceased rulers, mallqui, kept their estates and households. Their lineages fed them, carried them to festivals, and spoke for them in council.

Cusco

A calendar drawn on the land

Some forty lines, the ceques, radiated from Cusco’s central temple through 328 shrines. Each line was cared for by a kin group, so the map was also a calendar of rites and a chart of social duty.

Andes

Stones held by fit alone

Inca walls, like the twelve-angled stone of Hatun Rumiyoc in Cusco, fit without mortar so closely that a blade cannot enter. In earthquakes the blocks shift and settle back into place.

Andes

Sowing water

Pre-Inca amunas channels in Huarochirí divert rainy-season water into mountain slopes so that springs run months later. Communities still call it sowing and harvesting water. Freeze-drying in the night frost turns potatoes into chuño that keeps for years.

Amazonia

The forest as a garden

Amazonian dark earths, managed forests of useful trees, and fish weirs show the rainforest as a cultivated landscape, much of it shaped over thousands of years.

Andes

Pairs before singles

Communities divide into upper and lower halves, hanan and hurin. In the tinku, the two halves meet in ritual combat whose blood is offered to the earth for fertility. Complementary pairing, yanantin, runs through thought, weaving and leadership.

Northeast

Treaties in shell

Wampum belts of white and purple shell beads record laws and agreements. The Haudenosaunee read the Two Row Wampum as two vessels travelling side by side, neither steering the other, and weigh decisions for the seventh generation to come.

Southeast

Peace towns and war towns

Muscogee towns were white towns, which held peace councils and gave refuge, or red towns, which led in war.

Arctic

Stones in the likeness of a person

Inuksuit, stone figures built across a treeless land, mark routes, caches, hunting places and sacred sites.

V
Thread V

Roles with no Western or Chinese twin

Some of these roles keep memory, as the jeli do. Others keep weather, water, game, bridges or the dead. All hold a place in public life because a community entrusts them with something it cannot do without.

KeepersNorth America
Haudenosaunee

Clan mothers and wampum keepers

Clan mothers choose the chiefs and can remove them, and a designated keeper at Onondaga holds the Confederacy’s wampum belts and their readings.

Lakota and other Plains nations

Winter-count keepers

Each year the keepers chose one event to name it, painted it on a hide in a spiral of pictographs, and recited the whole count from memory.

Cherokee

Ghigau, the Beloved Woman

An honoured woman with a voice in councils of war and peace and the power to spare captives. Nanyehi, Nancy Ward, held the title in the 18th century and spoke at treaty talks with the United States.

Lakota

Heyoka, the contraries

People called by a thunder dream to live backwards, greeting on departure and shivering in summer heat. Their clowning teaches and heals, in the family of the holy fools.

Diné (Navajo)

Hataałii, the singers

Healers who conduct chant ceremonies lasting up to nine nights and build sandpaintings for the patient to sit upon, dispersing them when the rite is done.

Anishinaabe

Midewiwin scroll keepers

Members of the Grand Medicine Society record songs, rites and migration histories on incised birchbark scrolls, read and interpreted for initiates.

Inuit

Angakkuq

Healers and mediators with the spirit world, who journeyed to Sedna beneath the sea to free the sea animals when hunting failed.

KeepersMesoamerica
K’iche’ and other Maya, Guatemala

Ajq’ij, the day-keepers

Keepers of the 260-day count who read each day’s character, name children and time the rites. The office survived five centuries of colonial rule and is practised today.

Mexica and Mixtec painter-scribes

Tlacuilo

Painters of the screenfold codices of history, tribute and divination. The codices were performed aloud, so the painted page and the spoken telling worked together.

Nahua city-states

Tlahtoani, the speaker

The Nahuatl word for ruler means “one who speaks”. Rulers were expected to master huehuetlahtolli, the formal speeches of the elders, delivered at accessions, births and marriages.

Mexica long-distance merchants

Pochteca

A hereditary merchant guild with its own courts and gods, travelling beyond the empire, often in disguise, as traders, spies and envoys of the Mexica rulers.

Nahua communities around Popocatépetl

Graniceros

Weather-workers, often chosen by surviving a lightning strike, who call the rain and turn away hail with rites on the volcano’s slopes. The tradition continues today.

KeepersThe Caribbean
Taíno, Greater Antilles

Behique and the areíto

The behique healed and spoke with the zemí spirits. At the areíto the community sang and danced its history and genealogy, led by the cacique: a sung archive in the same spirit as the jeli.

KeepersThe Andes
Inca administration, and living villages

Khipukamayuq, the cord-keepers

Officials who recorded census, tribute, storehouse stock and calendar in knotted cords and read them aloud on demand; early Spanish courts heard khipu testimony as evidence. In the highland village of San Andrés de Tupicocha, the community still keeps a set of khipus as its patrimony and brings them out at its annual assembly.

Inca relay messengers

Chaski, the runners

Young runners posted at short stages along the roads, passing khipus and spoken messages hand to hand. Chroniclers say a message could travel around 240 kilometres in a day; a conch trumpet announced each arrival.

Inca court

Amawt’a and haravec

Teachers of noble youth and poet-singers who kept the official histories in song. Chroniclers report that each new ruler could have those songs revised, so the remembered past was renewed with every reign.

Inca panaqa lineages

Speakers for the royal dead

Attendants who fed, dressed and carried a dead ruler’s body and spoke his wishes in council. Through them, a king could keep owning land and visiting his peers for generations.

Q’eswachaka, Peru

Chakaruwak, the bridge master

A hereditary specialist who directs the yearly rebuilding of the last Inca grass bridge, guiding four communities and opening the work with offerings to the mountains and the river.

Aymara, Bolivia and Peru

Yatiri

Ritual specialists chosen by surviving a lightning strike. They read coca leaves, prepare offerings to the earth and the mountain grandfathers, and settle questions of health, harvest and travel.

Apolobamba, Bolivia

Kallawaya

Travelling healers who carry a pharmacopoeia of hundreds of plants across the Andes and speak a professional language of their own, Machaj Juyai, used only among healers.

Andean communities

Varayoq, the staff-holders

Community authorities who carry carved staffs of office for a year at a time. Office is a turn of service owed to the community, rotating through households.

KeepersThe Amazon and the isthmus
Kogi, Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta

Mamo

Priest-leaders trained from early childhood for years in near-darkness to perceive aluna, the world of thought behind things. They divine, make payments to the mountain’s waters and snows, and guide community decisions. In 1990 they used a BBC documentary to send a warning to the outside world about the mountain’s drying.

Tukano peoples, Vaupés

Payé

Healers whose dealings with the master of animals set how much a community may hunt and fish. The anthropologist Gerardo Reichel-Dolmatoff described their cosmology as an ecological model, an energy budget with the shaman as its auditor.

Shipibo-Konibo, Peru

Onanya and the kené painters

Healers sing icaros that they perceive as luminous patterns, and women paint those same kené patterns on cloth and pottery. Song and design are two forms of one notation.

Yanomami, Brazil and Venezuela

Speakers of the wayamou

When villages visit, men from each side chant through the night in a formal dialogue, negotiating trade, marriages, invitations and grievances in rhythmic verse.

Upper Xingu, Brazil

Wrestlers of the Kuarup

The peoples of the Upper Xingu hold shared memorial festivals for their chiefs, where young men wrestle in huka-huka bouts between villages. Ritual contest binds a region of different languages into one society.

Guna, Panama and Colombia

Saila and argar

In the evening congress, the saila chants history and law in an esoteric language; the argar, his spokesman, stands to interpret the chant for everyone in everyday speech.

KeepersThe Mapuche south
Mapuche, Chile and Argentina

Machi

Healers, often women, who climb the rewe, a stepped carved trunk, and play the kultrun, a drum painted with a map of the cosmos, to heal and to lead the great community rites.

Mapuche

Weupife and werken

The weupife is an orator-historian who recites lineage and the history of the land; the werken is the messenger and spokesperson of the lonko, the community head. Mapuche communities still name a werken today, and parliaments with the Spanish were conducted in formal Mapuche oratory.

The keeper is chosen by the world, and answers to the community.
VI
Thread VI

Why these roles carried weight

They hold a relationship the community depends on

Mountains, water, animals, the dead: each keeper tends a relationship on everyone’s behalf, and the community’s survival runs through it.

Many are chosen by an event

A lightning strike, a sickness survived, a dream. The jeli inherit their role; many keepers in the Americas are selected by the world itself, which gives them an authority no family can grant.

Two voices are better than one

The saila and the argar, the lonko and the werken, the dead king and his speaker: the source of authority and the voice that carries it are kept apart, as with the jeli and the okyeame. Among the Mexica the ruler himself was named the speaker.

They keep the accounts

Khipukamayuq counted the storehouses, and the payé counted the game. Keepers are auditors of shared resources.

Time is a public trust

Day-keepers, winter-count keepers and calendar priests hold the shared rhythm of life: when to plant, name, marry and remember.

Renewal is the record

A bridge rebuilt every June, a staff passed on every year, history songs re-sung each reign. Knowledge stays true by being remade together.

Leads for Nine Rain

Nine Rain’s roots are Andean, knotted cords, the condor, ayni, with a Mesoamerican day-sign grafted on. These are quick first reactions, threads to pick up or leave.

A name that is a day

In the 260-day count, Nine Rain is a day, 9 Quiahuitl to the Mexica, and Mixtec lords carried such day-names all their lives. Nine Rain’s members could take the day of their awakening as their name.

Chosen by lightning

The Aymara yatiri and the Nahua graniceros are both made by surviving a strike, and the graniceros work hail and rain on Popocatépetl. A Nine Rain gift that awakens in a storm, with a mountain for its seat.

Fields on the water

Chinampas built of lake mud stay fertile for centuries. A Nine Rain craft of making land out of water.

Sowing water

Amunas and puquios hold water across seasons, and wind pushes it through stone. A Nine Rain magic of rain sown now and harvested months later, or water moved by breath and air.

Stones held by fit alone

Inca walls stand by friction and settle back after earthquakes. A natural home for Nine Rain’s great stones and its craft of friction and non-friction.

Knots of water

Khipu colour and ply carry meaning. Water from different places, Nine Rain’s instrument seed, could be read the same way, each source a distinct strand.

The yearly bridge

Q’eswachaka is renewed every year in collective work. A model for Nine Rain artefacts that are renewed in community rites.

Weather as thought

The mamos tend the mountain’s waters through aluna, and the payé negotiates quotas with the master of animals. Nine Rain weather-working as negotiation with the owners of the rain.

The Andes kept a state on knots and work, Mesoamerica kept time in two turning calendars, the northeast kept its law in shell, and the forest kept its cities in its soil. Knowledge can live in the land and in the bonds between people, renewed each time they gather.

Notes on dates and sources

Dates are the commonly cited spans and keep being revised, especially in Amazonia and the Maya lowlands, where lidar is changing the picture quickly. The founding of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy is dated anywhere from the 12th to the 15th century. How much narrative khipus encoded is still debated. The Inca population at conquest is estimated anywhere from six to over ten million.
