00 · What we are assuming

The premise before the interpretation.

The organising ambition is a world in which many societies retain the resources, institutions, and political room to produce different sophisticated futures. Europe remains part of that world. No region is confined to a preserved premodern identity.

User premise

Worlds and eras

PMU-m is the magical branch. PMU-c is the catastrophe branch and is outside this atlas. PMU-bm and PMU-am mark before and after magic within PMU-m. These are working labels, not necessarily in-world names.

User premise

Historical divergence

History diverges most substantially around the 1400s. Earlier histories can remain broadly recognisable without requiring every event after 1400 to occur on schedule.

User premise

Plural centres of knowledge

Western epistemic institutions never acquire our world’s degree of dominance. Multiple societies retain resources and authority to develop, teach, test, and circulate knowledge.

User premise

Smaller European colonial footprint

European colonies and independent successors exist, but overseas conquest is more limited and its devastation substantially smaller. European societies remain active, inventive, and politically diverse.

User premise

Africa without imposed continental partition

The European partition and its arbitrary national borders do not occur on our scale. The Atlantic trafficking of African people is much smaller. Free movement remains a strong intended feature; local conflicts and restrictions still need histories.

User premise

Plural Americas and strong Mexico

No continental USA forms. Several former European colonies become separate states, while Indigenous powers and a stronger Mexico shape the continental balance. Other American regions retain their own agency.

User premise

A different Chinese republic

China transitions from imperial rule to a republic without our Chinese Civil War or Cultural Revolution. Provinces remain individually strong, collectively powerful, and unevenly conservative, with limited central unity.

User premise

No world wars

Neither World War I nor World War II occurs. Smaller conflicts remain. This does not require every ruler to be peaceful or every hierarchy to be benign.

User premise

Magic redirects development

Magic is discovered before full fossil-fuel industrialisation. Coal and petroleum never become necessary foundations of civilisation. Production and infrastructure can become sophisticated through other means.

User premise

No computer / AI trajectory

PMU-m does not develop computers and AI along our historical path. Mechanical calculation, record systems, and magical instruments may exist; general-purpose magical computation and artificial minds remain undecided.

User premise

Outward opportunity

Magic eventually permits access to other worlds. This reduces the incentive to colonise neighbouring societies on the home world; the effect is a desired historical outcome, not an automatic law of abundance.

Proposed for testing

Discovery anchor

1680 is AM0: a period of public, reproducible demonstrations, not necessarily the first magical occurrence. Before this anchor, all effective power in this model is non-magical. Several earlier discovery sites remain possible.

Proposed for testing

Gateway anchor and present

Reliable interworld travel begins around 1860 / AM180. The atlas horizon is 2040 / AM360, an arithmetic comparison date rather than a claim that their calendar or present must match ours.

Proposed for testing

Distributed capability

Magic is learnable by many people, supports several technical approaches, and has limits. No one culture, bloodline, or empire obtains a permanent monopoly.

Open

Other-world status

Whether accessible worlds are inhabited is unresolved. The expansion model tests available destinations without presuming empty worlds, unlimited resources, or permission to occupy inhabited land.

Proposed for testing

The powers and names below

All political sequences, new charters, organisations, and dates below are fictional proposals. Familiar cultural and historical names are reference points, not claims that living peoples share one constitution or destiny.

Interpretive rule: Africa without colonial partition still contains many polities, alliances, land systems, and disagreements. A nomadic government can maintain laboratories; a kingdom can provide universal schooling; a republic can be coercive. Governance and technical sophistication are separate axes.

01 · Potential reasons and causes

Divergence through several reinforcing changes.

These are deliberately authored counterfactual mechanisms, not claims that a single changed event would certainly generate this world. In particular, magic discovered in 1680 cannot explain why a conquest failed in 1520.

1435–1500 · Maritime continuity

Proposed mechanism
Commercial charter networks survive changes at the Ming court; South and Southeast Asian, Arabian, and East African partners continue investing in ports and convoys. This does not require a permanent Chinese imperial fleet.

Intended consequence
A European ship entering the Indian Ocean meets organised local competitors with credit, ships, and diplomatic options.

What still needs to work
Strong ports can still exploit inland suppliers; a maritime coalition requires maintenance and trust.

1450–1650 · Conquest pays less reliably

Proposed mechanism
Local coalitions deny durable monopolies, fort leases are revocable, and foreign merchants struggle to acquire tax-collecting authority. Rival European states sometimes support local resistance to competitors.

Intended consequence
Overseas commerce continues; a smaller share becomes territorial extraction capable of financing further conquest.

What still needs to work
No single treaty can explain centuries of resistance. This requires several locally specific successes and occasional defeats.

1500–1680 · Surviving American sovereignty

Proposed mechanism
First-contact routes and timing differ. Epidemics still occur, but political fragmentation among invaders, surviving food systems, and Indigenous alliances prevent disease crises from becoming universal conquest.

Intended consequence
Recovery happens under many Indigenous and mixed governments rather than one continental settler order.

What still needs to work
This is a substantial counterfactual commitment. Quarantine alone cannot plausibly erase early epidemics, and post-1680 healing cannot rescue the 1500s.

1500–1800 · A smaller captive-labour economy

Proposed mechanism
Restricted plantation expansion, resistance by enslaved people, anti-raiding coalitions, limits imposed by selected African authorities, and alternative commercial income reinforce one another.

Intended consequence
Far less population loss and coercive export; greater scope for local production and institution building.

What still needs to work
Internal slavery and complicity may persist. A smaller Atlantic trade needs a different history of abolition, not an assumption of universal virtue.

1680–1860 · Energy takes another branch

Proposed mechanism
Reproducible magic becomes affordable enough for waterworks, transport, manufacturing, and food systems before coal infrastructure dominates investment.

Intended consequence
Coalfields and oil reserves do not determine the main geography of wealth. Existing craft, hydraulic, and scholarly centres can grow into advanced production systems.

What still needs to work
Magic must have maintenance costs and access rules. Otherwise it erases too many economic and political stakes.

1680–2040 · Knowledge stays institutionally plural

Proposed mechanism
Patronage is distributed across courts, towns, communities, guilds, and mobile associations. Translation and shared demonstration protocols connect them without requiring one universal university model.

Intended consequence
Several centres can set research agendas and certify expertise. Scholars can disagree while testing whether a bridge, treatment, or instrument works.

What still needs to work
Plurality does not guarantee equal funding or truthful claims. Institutions need criticism, comparison, and ways to correct error.

1830 · China negotiates succession

Proposed mechanism
Provincial revenue control, shared debt guarantees, elite bargains, popular pressure, and technical interdependence make a negotiated republican compact more attractive than a bid for total central victory.

Intended consequence
A commonwealth preserves shared defence and infrastructure while leaving strong provincial governments.

What still needs to work
The bargain must include more than provincial elites if it is to improve ordinary lives. Some local hierarchies survive.

1860–2040 · Expansion turns outward

Proposed mechanism
Several independent gateways become viable; access is not concentrated in one empire. Negotiated opportunities elsewhere compete with costly conquest at home.

Intended consequence
Migration, investment, and rivalry increasingly concern passage and new settlements rather than annexing neighbours.

What still needs to work
Inhabited destinations require diplomacy and consent. Gateway monopolies, extractive charters, and coercive settlement are still possible failure modes.

1800–2040 · War remains regional

Proposed mechanism
No equivalent coal-steel imperial arms order consolidates. Trade coalitions vary by issue, defensive magic limits selected offensives, and gateway access offers alternatives to some territorial disputes.

Intended consequence
Conflicts do not combine into the particular global wars of 1914–18 and 1939–45, or equivalent total world wars within this model.

What still needs to work
Distributed magic could also increase destructive power. The no-world-war outcome requires political restraint and contingent crisis settlements; multipolarity alone does not deliver it.

The branching century

Ports, courts, mobile confederacies, farming communities, and scholarly networks remain several centres of initiative. Maritime continuity and stronger local coalitions begin to limit monopolies.

No effective magic in this model. Sailing, animal and human power, water, wind, workshops, and existing technical traditions remain central.

1500–1640180–40 BM

A contested oceanic order

European colonies exist on a smaller scale. American epidemics and invasions remain severe but produce different political outcomes. African anti-raiding and commercial alliances constrain captive export.

Manufacturing and experimental inquiry develop unevenly. No society is assigned a single natural specialty.

1640–168040–0 BM

Several early modernities

Proposed Lian succession in China; independent commercial and scholarly institutions gain bargaining power in several regions. Some empires fragment; others negotiate durable autonomy.

Sophisticated manufacture and civic infrastructure remain possible before a fossil-fuel industrial order. Magic is not secretly doing the causal work yet.

1680–1720AM0–40

The public demonstrations

Repeatable magic is demonstrated at multiple sites within a generation. Researchers dispute priority. Court secrecy competes with municipal, guild, and community teaching.

The discovery changes what is feasible, but access is costly and uneven. The substance, source, and mechanics of magic remain open.

1720–1800AM40–120

The age of magical public works

Water, sanitation, transport, food storage, and production become major applications. New workers and technical bodies demand political standing. Some old regimes adapt; some fall.

No digital computer or AI lineage is assumed. Records, calculation devices, instruments, and human institutions carry coordination.

1800–1860AM120–180

Constitutions and the first thresholds

China’s proposed republican settlement occurs in 1830 / AM150. Elsewhere, crowns, councils, republics, and confederacies negotiate control of magical infrastructure. Unreliable interworld experiments precede stable travel.

Affordable power is increasingly routine in some centres. Universal abundance is not assumed; healthcare, schooling, and access still differ.

1860–1920AM180–240

The opening of other worlds

Reliable passage begins at several sites over decades. Navigation, access law, ecological investigation, and settlement charters become new fields of power. Destinations and inhabitants remain to be designed.

Local institutions can participate in outward expansion; distance and capacity limits prevent instantaneous universal access.

1920–1980AM240–300

Passage becomes political

Disputes concern gate fees, jurisdiction, migration, labour, and dependencies. Several successful crisis settlements prevent escalation to global total war.

Some infrastructure is municipal, some communal, some royal, some commercial. Control of access matters more than a single oil geography.

1980–2040AM300–360

A world of overlapping powers

The atlas horizon: powerful regions and interworld networks coexist with autonomous smaller societies. Citizenship can be territorial, civic, mobile, or treaty-based.

PMU-m is sophisticated but has not followed our computer/AI path. Contact with PMU-c is left outside this chronology.

Why a long interval between magic and world travel?

One controllable piece of world design: usable magic need not imply immediate mastery of interworld passage. Two centuries allow distinct educational systems, workforces, standards, and political bargains to develop. Early magic can change daily life before it changes the inhabited cosmos. The actual technical obstacle remains to be invented.

Niger Bend Concords

Middle Niger, inland delta, Sahelian market corridors
Confederation · Working English label

Starting point
Mali, Songhai, and other Sahelian political and scholarly histories; no single successor is presumed.

1860–1950 · Caravan associations co-finance gateways at inland markets. Routes diversify without making the Niger valley economically disposable.

1950–2040 · An elected urban chamber, hereditary courts, and seasonal pastoral delegations jointly negotiate regional works. Some member states remain monarchies.

04 · Several kinds of government

Different constitutions.
Different ways to belong.

The categories overlap. A confederation can contain a kingdom, a city republic, and a mobile nation. Democracy is a distribution of political voice that may operate within several forms; it is not simply another word for republic. No category here is inherently more magical or more technologically capable.

Hereditary monarchy

Authority passes within a ruling house, but land, revenue, public works, or succession can be constrained by estates and councils.

Possible expression
A court adapts to magical public services without abolishing itself.

Political question
Can subjects remove an abusive ruler or refuse compulsory work?

Landed or feudal jurisdiction

Layered obligations bind households, estates, local lords, and higher rulers; the precise arrangement differs by place.

Possible expression
A conservative Chinese province retains landlord jurisdictions inside a republic.

Political question
Can a tenant leave, own land, appeal, or obtain education?

Tribal / Indigenous government

A broad family of distinct nation, clan, kinship, territorial, and legal institutions; the English word “tribal” is not a common constitution.

Possible expression
A nation maintains its own land law, public services, and treaty authority.

Political question
Who belongs, who represents dispersed members, and how can law change?

Nomadic confederacy

Membership, seasonal obligations, herding or trade routes, and shared defence can matter more than permanent residence.

Possible expression
A mobile polity owns workshops and portable infrastructure.

Political question
Can poorer households and non-mobile members participate equally?

City or guild republic

Municipal citizenship and elected or guild offices organise power; a republic may be democratic or oligarchic.

Possible expression
A port becomes rich through gateway insurance and manufacturing.

Political question
Are workers, migrants, and the surrounding countryside represented?

Confederation / federation

Member governments share selected powers; they can have different domestic constitutions.

Possible expression
China coordinates currency clearing and defence while provinces govern most daily affairs.

Political question
What can common institutions compel, and can a member leave?

Treaty and commons authority

Separate societies jointly govern a river, route, pasture, research network, or gateway without merging into one nation.

Possible expression
Seasonal passage rights cross several territorial jurisdictions.

Political question
Who can enforce an agreement when a powerful member breaks it?

Religious or scholarly estate

A religious institution or learned corporation exercises political authority over certain people, land, or services.

Possible expression
An estate maintains hospitals and teaching houses within a wider constitutional order.

Political question
Can dissenters appeal outside the estate, and is access conditional on belief?

Gateway dependency / charter

A new settlement initially answers to a sponsoring government, company, cooperative, or consortium.

Possible expression
A charter specifies public services and a timetable for self-government.

Political question
Can residents revise the charter, and whose prior sovereignty was recognised?

Membership can follow different things: a home territory; residence in a city; membership in a nation or community; a seasonal route; a profession; or an interworld settlement charter. Several can apply to the same person. Their conflicts provide real political stakes.

05 · Where influence travels

Spatial power is more than territorial size.

A · The Indian Ocean arc

East African harbours ↔ Red Sea and Arabian ports ↔ South Asian commonwealths ↔ the Straits ↔ island networks.

BM: goods, people, capital, law, and techniques circulate among existing centres. Early AM: magical practices are compared and adapted. Gateway era: insurers, translators, navigators, and settlement organisers gain influence. Singapore benefits from these relationships rather than creating them.

B · Inland water and passage

Niger and Saharan routes; Congo tributaries; Asian river basins; American watersheds.

These are several distinct networks, not one contiguous empire. Their comparable political problem is coordinating shared infrastructure without surrendering every local government’s autonomy. A watershed map and a sovereignty map do not have to coincide.

C · The continental American balance

Mexico ↔ Indigenous confederacies ↔ interior nations ↔ Atlantic successor republics; with distinct Andean and southern powers.

Before gateways, treaties, regional power, and resistance constrain territorial expansion. After gateways, outward opportunities help maintain a plural continental balance. Mexico contributes to that outcome but does not act as the continent’s sole defender or decision maker.

D · Pacific and northern Australian encounters

Island commonwealths ↔ Aotearoa polities ↔ Torres Strait and northern Australian Countries ↔ island Southeast Asian ports.

Individual relationships develop at different times and scales. Exchange can include technology, families, institutions, and disagreement. No pan-Oceanic nation or single authentic magical method is required.

E · Research without one intellectual capital

Mobile teachers, urban laboratories, royal academies, community schools, medical institutions, conservatories, and workshops.

A discovery can be demonstrated in one place, explained through a second tradition, manufactured elsewhere, and regulated by another jurisdiction. Public testing and translation develop alongside genuine disputes about evidence, ethics, and ownership.

F · Gateways and the new geography

Local gate sites ↔ shared transit institutions ↔ destination societies and settlements, whose status remains open.

A small island or inland town may become a major node without conquering adjacent land. Conversely, cities can lose influence when routes bypass them. Multiple sites and limits on exclusive charters are proposed reasons that interworld access does not produce one new global empire.

A useful changing centre of gravity: before magic, many powers bargain over labour, food, routes, land, and credit. Early AM adds technical teaching and magical public infrastructure. The gateway era adds access, destination knowledge, migration rules, and settlement authority. Earlier sources of power persist; the new layer does not erase them.

The choices that will change the next draft.

When, and where, does magic become reproducible?

1680 is the current test anchor. Possible patterns include several near-simultaneous demonstrations, one discovery that spreads rapidly, or separate discoveries that only later prove related. Priority could remain a lasting argument.

What does magic cost?

Decide limits on power, materials, expertise, maintenance, scale, and transport. A distributed teachable capability supports this atlas; a hereditary monopoly would change its politics substantially.

What are the other worlds like?

Separate ecological viability, existing inhabitants, recognisable persons, and prior sovereignty. Accessible does not automatically mean available for settlement. No empty-world assumption is adopted here.

Historical seeds · separate from the fiction

A few anchors, not historical proof.

These references support limited real-world starting points. They do not establish the likelihood of the invented timelines, treaties, populations, or political outcomes above. Except for these explicitly identified seeds, the atlas is a worldbuilding proposal rather than a historical survey.

  1. H1 · Swahili cities already participated in extensive Indian Ocean exchange. UNESCO describes Kilwa and Songo Mnara and their commercial relations with Arabia, India, and China. The proposed harbour league, defensive coalition, and magical evolution are inventions.
    UNESCO · Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara
  2. H2 · Indigenous confederacy is an existing form of sovereignty, not a fictional substitute for government. The Onondaga Nation describes distinct member nations, their internal authority, and shared Haudenosaunee institutions. The PMU regional associations are new proposals and must not be read as descriptions of all Indigenous governments.
    Onondaga Nation · Sovereignty
  3. H3 · Northern Australia had sustained maritime connections with island Southeast Asia. The National Museum of Australia describes Makassar–Arnhem Land exchange from at least around 1700. This draft does not claim the same recorded trade existed in 1400; its earlier and later fictional encounters remain to be specified.
    National Museum of Australia · Trade with the Makasar
  4. H4 · Ming maritime expeditions provide one early-fifteenth-century branch point. Columbia’s teaching resource describes Zheng He’s seven expeditions between 1405 and 1433. Commercial continuity after the court’s fleets, the Lian succession, and the 1830 republican settlement are fictional additions; the expeditions are not evidence of a naturally peaceful Chinese world order.
    Columbia University · The Ming Voyages
  5. H5 · American epidemic losses cannot be wished away by smaller European empires. The Smithsonian’s National Museum of American History identifies disease, warfare, and exchange as features of early encounters. The draft preserves severe epidemics and proposes different political survival and recovery; it does not present that outcome as inevitable.
    Smithsonian · Unsettling the Continent
