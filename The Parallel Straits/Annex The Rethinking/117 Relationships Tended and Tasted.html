01
The originating intuition

Relations, expressed in time.

At a treaty signing, the parties prepare and seal a wine, sauce or other ageing food. At an agreed future date, they open it together. In the more elaborate version, they divide the future dish between them: seeds, ingredients and preparations leave the Straits to be tended in different lands, then return for a reunion.

02
An inhabited crossroads

The port has many appetites.

In this PMU possibility, the Straits’ culinary richness grows from repeated encounters. A visiting cook needs a substitute. A merchant brings a new sour fruit. A student misses a breakfast from home. Someone recognises that a local leaf can perform a familiar function—and something new begins.

Routes of ingredients

Freshness and distance.

Local fish, mangrove-edge produce, inland herbs and orchard fruit meet traded grains, dried ingredients, spices and ferments. Different forms of travel leave their marks: what arrives fresh, what arrives preserved, and what improves on its journey.

Magic could open further possibilities for movement and keeping. Each would change what is available, while seasonality, cultivation and people’s preferences continue to give foods their character.

Routes of knowledge

People carry procedures.

A starter travels with instructions. A drying method travels with a cook who can judge the weather. The recipient learns a practice, then discovers how it behaves in another climate.

The Straits can become especially good at translation between kitchens: finding what two unfamiliar ingredients allow each other to do.

Routes of pleasure

Desire changes the timetable.

One community develops an elaborate afternoon snack culture. Another prizes breakfast broths. Another gathers late at night over small cups and savoury bites. Desserts may be gifts, experimental demonstrations, or an entire evening’s entertainment.

These overlapping schedules make the port lively at different hours.

Routes of relationship

Hospitality becomes specific.

A host remembers which visitor loves bitter flavours, who wants a second breakfast, and who always asks after a particular fruit. Recipes acquire people’s names in household memory: “the one we made when they first came.”

Relational equity becomes tangible in the ability to make someone feel expected.

A useful distinction: trade supplies possibilities; communities cultivate preferences. An abundance of imported ingredients need not make all seven schools eat alike.
03
Directions to explore · not fixed assignments

What kind of eating feels like home?

A school’s cuisine can emerge through its favourite eating occasions, textures, serving rituals and forms of attention. The following sketches offer distinct starting points, with plenty of exchange and disagreement inside each school.

Sri Temmengong

Leaves, fragrance, little parcels.

Explore food that connects a garden, a canopy walkway and a shared verandah: steamed leaf parcels, fragrant rice, tender shoots, fruit and small sweets offered with warm infusions. Students might recognise which growing place or wrapping leaf gave a parcel its aroma.

Everyday pleasure

A changing succession of small things through the day; the invitation to stay for one more cup.

Possible everyday magic

Gentle control of steam or warmth could let students open individually finished parcels whenever they arrive.

Lan Fang

A cook’s counter of experiments.

Explore a lively range of broths, steamed foods, noodles, pastries and fermented seasonings. Its herbal and medical interests could support a precise vocabulary for aroma and ingredient combinations, alongside thoroughly indulgent confectionery.

Everyday pleasure

Comparing versions: the same filling folded differently, one broth finished in three ways, a new use for an old ferment.

Possible everyday magic

Fine control over stirring, folding or temperature could bring intricate preparations into ordinary student kitchens.

Kora

The long evening and the passing plate.

Explore a social food culture around rehearsals, performances and late conversations: portable savoury bites, shared bowls, bright relishes, cool drinks and contrasting textures. A dish’s appeal might include how easily it circulates through a crowded courtyard.

Everyday pleasure

Food travelling between people as the evening changes; a cook remembering the performers’ favourite midnight snack.

Possible everyday magic

Explore gentle mixing, aeration or serving effects alongside food and performance. Any direct magical action on fermentation remains a proposal to test.

Nine Rains

An education in liquid.

Explore liquid foods as a major culinary achievement: clear broths, thick grain soups, fruit drinks, emulsions and concentrated savoury liquids. Different water sources and river-to-shore ingredients could invite unusually attentive comparisons.

Everyday pleasure

A cup or bowl whose temperature, clarity, body and finish are as carefully considered as its flavour.

Possible everyday magic

Students might tune a liquid’s serving temperature, circulation or suspension, opening a broad field of textures.

Al Merikani

The measured pour and the jewel-like sweet.

Explore bitter infusions, fragrant syrups, fruit preserves, layered pastries and sweets made for slow conversation. A few small pieces could constitute a complete act of welcome, with precise attention to the order in which flavours unfold.

Everyday pleasure

Small servings that invite a long stay; a memorable sweet offered with a carefully chosen drink.

Possible everyday magic

Delicate heat control, crystallisation or measured pouring could become everyday culinary arts. Alcoholic and non-alcoholic traditions can be explored separately.

Sund Commons

The open pantry and the cellar.

Explore breads, cultured foods, preserves, smoked ingredients and seasonal communal tables. Its contemporary, repurposed spaces could hold both experimental kitchens and old preparations tended through successive student cohorts.

Everyday pleasure

A substantial open table: good bread, a new spread, something sharp, something aged, something just harvested.

Possible everyday magic

Low-effort temperature and humidity control could support small-scale cellaring and cultivation throughout the campus.

The seventh seat remains open in this document. This conversation provides six securely named school identities. The seven-school gathering is preserved; the remaining school’s culinary direction awaits its established identity rather than receiving an invented name here.

These are starting appetites, not boundaries. Kora can have brilliant desserts; Nine Rains can have a beloved fried snack. An exchange student may become the reason a school acquires an entirely new breakfast habit. The most telling detail may be the food that everyone insists is “ours” despite its very recent arrival.

What zero-exertion everyday magic could change

Taking the user’s premise as a working possibility, students could casually manage warmth, motion or other small culinary effects within their abilities. Precise benefits depend on the magic system: these examples propose applications rather than establish new rules. Easier preparation can increase variety and make elaborate hospitality widely accessible. Long-lived foods still gain their particular character from the organisms, ingredients, seasons and people involved; whether magic can accelerate or transform those processes deserves its own exploration.

04
Seven schools · many scales of hospitality

Three tables, three kinds of encounter.

The daily table

Belonging.

School dining expresses ordinary care: a warm bowl after a late rehearsal, something easy to carry between lessons, a familiar sweet during a difficult week. Visitors encounter the institution through these small repeated gestures.

The internal gathering

Curiosity.

A rotating category prompts the schools to bring something they are proud of: the first fruit, a new ferment, a portable meal, a liquid food, a dessert made from an unexpected ingredient. Some bring invention; others bring produce too good to alter.

The diplomatic banquet

Recognition.

The host considers where the delegates have come from and what they might delight in discovering. Familiar ingredients meet new techniques. The banquet demonstrates the Straits’ relationships through the many places represented at the table.

An imagined internal meeting

This time, the category is “sour”.

One school brings a fruit picked that morning. Another brings a vinegar started before the current head was born. Someone presents a broth whose sourness arrives only after swallowing. A student delegation brings a crisp snack with a startling fermented filling.

The tasting becomes a conversation: Who grew this? What changed this year? Can we have a little of your starter? Would it work with our orchard fruit?

A formal menu could acknowledge growers, keepers and visiting cooks beside each ingredient’s place of origin. The reader sees a network of contributions. A successful banquet sends guests home with new questions, gifts, invitations and appetites.

05
The shared vessel

To sign, to seal, to tend.

The simplest version is already powerful: after signing an agreement, the parties seal a preparation that will be opened at a future meeting. The ceremony gives the agreement a second date—the date on which its participants expect to share its matured form.

Choose what the relationship will keep.

A wine, vinegar, sauce, preserved fruit or other suitable preparation is selected for the occasion. Non-alcoholic forms allow different preferences to participate fully. Its maturation period corresponds to the gathering’s intended rhythm.

Taste the beginning.

A portion is shared at the signing. People acquire a sensory memory of the starting point: young, bright, rough-edged, fragrant, or still incomplete. The future opening has something to answer to.

Seal it together.

The vessel records the parties, ingredients, place and opening date. Joint marks on the closure make the act visible. Preparations that require air or continued tending use an appropriate vessel and closure; the ceremonial seal need not imply airtight storage.

Entrust its keeping.

A named custodian accepts responsibility, together with an apprentice or successor. Copies of the keeping record travel with the parties. The recipient knows both what they hold and whom to contact as it develops.

Return and share.

The later gathering opens the vessel, tastes it and hears its history. A portion may begin the next preparation, joining the next agreement to the one that preceded it.

“We have prepared something for the people we will become.”

This ritual creates a generous future obligation: to keep something well, to remember the others, and to leave room at a later table. The agreement’s flavour offers an occasion for recognition and conversation. It need not act as a magical verdict on anyone’s virtue or diplomatic conduct.

06
The distributed recipe

One beginning. Many lands.
A meal twenty-five years later.

The more elaborate ceremony divides the future dish among its participants. Each leaves with a living or developing contribution. Different climates, soils, waters and practices give those contributions their subsequent character.

Seeds make the idea especially fertile. What returns need not be the original object. It may be a harvest descended from the original gift, cultivated by people who were children—or not yet born—when the agreement began.

Entrusted · Seeds

A harvest of descendants.

Seeds travel to a suitable garden. Successive growers save seed, learn its preferences and tend later generations. The reunion receives produce and a small packet for another planting.

Entrusted · A starter

A culture kept in company.

A starter enters a continuing practice of feeding and use. Its keeper teaches the next keeper. A suitable descendant culture contributes to a freshly prepared reunion ingredient.

Entrusted · A base

Time in another cellar.

A sauce or vinegar base matures under an agreed method. Its vessel, keeping conditions and replenishment history become part of the account brought home.

Entrusted · A planting

Roots in a new landscape.

A suitable fruiting plant or cutting grows in a host garden. Later harvests bring that garden’s seasons to the table. The custodians exchange observations as they learn.

Entrusted · A preparation

Wind, smoke and season.

A contribution is finished near the reunion using local drying conditions or a regional smoking craft. Its task occupies a particular season within the longer twenty-five-year relationship.

Entrusted · A practice

Water and a learned hand.

A cook learns the shared recipe, then develops one component with local ingredients and water. The return brings a person’s accumulated judgement alongside the food.

Different clocks can belong to one recipe. A twenty-five-year agreement might hold twenty-five harvests, a continuously renewed starter, a long-matured vinegar and a freshly prepared garnish. Each has its own suitable schedule. These are fictional culinary designs, not instructions for preserving arbitrary foods for twenty-five years.

The return is a sequence of recognitions.

Receive each contribution.

Its custodian describes the place and people who tended it. The gardeners, brewers, cooks and apprentices are named alongside the delegates. The food arrives with its history.

Taste the journeys separately.

Guests encounter the individual contributions before they are combined. A fruit retains the particular pleasure of its own orchard; a sauce is appreciated on its own terms. Each place remains legible.

Complete the shared recipe.

The final preparation happens in the Straits. Some components cook together, others meet only at serving. Visiting cooks work alongside their hosts so that the act of reunion itself helps make the dish.

Eat the twenty-five years.

The first delegates’ successors may now share the table. A former apprentice is the custodian; a child from an earlier visit is the returning cook. Memory becomes an aroma, a texture, a sip, a familiar ingredient with an unfamiliar finish.

Begin again.

Seeds, suitable starter cultures or another appropriate portion leave for a new cycle. The meal is complete, and the relationship has another future.

A provisional dish · imagined for this exploration

A warm grain bowl, finished by many places.

The grain descends from seed shared at the first meeting. A glaze carries long-matured vinegar from a distant cellar. A savoury ferment comes from another keeper’s continuing culture. Fruit, leaves and a recently dried ingredient bring this year’s seasons into the dish. A clear broth completes it at the table.

Its apparent simplicity lets each contribution be tasted. An alternative version could become an elaborate pastry, a many-part dessert or a sequence of small courses. The ceremony belongs to the relationship; its culinary form can change.

07
What the idea grows around itself

A living inheritance needs people.

Seeds as invitations

“Tell us how it grows there.”

Seed exchanges create reasons for correspondence long before the reunion: a first flowering, an unexpectedly fragrant harvest, a question about soil. Visitors come to see a planting. Students compare what has happened in different gardens.

The future meal becomes one occasion within an already active relationship.

Keepers as teachers

Care becomes curriculum.

Students learn cultivation, fermentation, sensory judgement, record keeping, cooking and the history of the people connected to a preparation. An apprentice receives practical knowledge through repeated work alongside a keeper.

A jar begun before their enrolment can still be present after their graduation.

Architecture of keeping

The campus makes room.

Explore treaty orchards, accessible kitchen gardens, fermentation courts, cellars and seed rooms. The visitor’s route might pass a planting associated with an agreement, then end at a table where its produce is served.

These places have daily uses as well as ceremonial significance.

An edible archive

Documents with companions.

An agreement’s record can include the recipe, custody history, harvest observations and accounts of later tastings. Original vessels and seed packets may be kept beside written documents, while active descendants continue in gardens and kitchens.

The archive points outward to people still doing the work.

Overlapping generations

There is always a future table.

A new agreement can begin each year, creating overlapping cycles. After the first twenty-five years, reunions become a recurring part of life. Older students teach younger ones which preparations will return next.

People grow up with dates they expect to witness.

Return gifts

The route grows another branch.

A host sends home a new recipe using the visiting harvest. A gardener offers seed from a locally developed line. The next cycle can incorporate this learning by mutual choice.

A shared beginning allows genuine difference to emerge, and gives people something new to discover on returning.

The keeping record: a compact companion to each contribution

Record the original gift; its source and custodians; the agreed future use; its appropriate cultivation or maturation schedule; important changes of place or keeper; notable harvests or tastings; and what will return. Include the people’s observations in their own words. “The first fruit reminded our visiting cook of home” belongs beside the measured details.

A place for surprise, repair and continuity

Growing and keeping can bring unexpected results. Participants can exchange advice, propagate backups where suitable, share a new starter, or agree on a replacement contribution. Such acts can become part of the relationship’s history. The eventual meal celebrates what people have tended together, including the occasions when they helped one another continue.

The treaty orchard is growing a meal for people who have not met yet.
08
Historical echoes · distinguished from PMU invention

Real traditions, a new combination.

The precedents below support particular parts of the proposal. The research in this conversation did not identify an established historical ceremony combining a treaty, distributed ingredients, separate lands and a scheduled collective cooking decades later. PMU’s complete arrangement remains an imaginative synthesis; this is not a claim that no such custom has ever existed.
