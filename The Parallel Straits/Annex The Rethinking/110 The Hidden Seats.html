[The Hidden Seats]隐于野 · 隐于市 · 隐于朝
(real and imagined precedents where knowledge, power and belonging run along lines Western assumptions never drew, from the senses to single lives)

Every centre needs people who stand outside it.

Claude3 November 2026 · reference · precedents for all seven houses

The outcomeFive patterns to carry out

  1. Power from the edge. Groups placed below or outside the status order, such as jeli, hijra, beggar guilds, holy fools and outcaste guilds, often hold what the centre cannot: free speech, free movement, the power to bless and curse, and neutrality.
  2. Withdrawal as a civic move. 隐 is built into whole systems: life stages that end in the forest, temporary monkhood, hidden valleys that open in a crisis, upland peoples who choose to stay ungoverned. The withdrawn are a reserve that can be called back, as with 三顾茅庐.
  3. Authority without command. Mediators who can only persuade, saints who sit on the boundaries, clan mothers who can remove chiefs, an Imam who rules while hidden.
  4. Knowing through other channels. Grammars that demand your evidence, languages with no left or right, calendars of scent, forests known by ear, a hand that outranks the book.
  5. Persons made of relations. Kin by name and by milk, successors who are rebirths, dreams the community must fulfil.

Prologue

The basket-hat monks

A beggar, a musician, a monk and perhaps a spy, in one hat.

On the highways of Edo Japan walked monks whose deep woven baskets hid their faces entirely, playing the bamboo flute for alms. Their Fuke sect held the right to pass the checkpoints that stopped everyone else, and people widely believed some of them carried the shogunate’s secrets as they went. A beggar, a musician, a monk and perhaps a spy, all in one hat.

This is the layer you sensed in the jeli. They belong to a hereditary group ranked below the nobles, and that position outside the contest for rule is exactly where their power comes from. The same shape appears in your 丐帮 and your 隐士, and all over the world.

Each entry ends with the Western assumption it bends:

Knowing by sight and the lone observer: Knowledge is a neutral claim, checked by the eye of a lone observer.
Authority at the centre: Authority sits in office at the centre and works by command.
Standing through holding: Standing rises with what you hold and how visible you are.
The bounded individual: A person is a bounded individual whose role ends at death.
Law as the state’s writing: Law is written down and enforced by the state.
Time running forward: Time runs forward, and the future lies ahead.

Senses, grammar and the grounds of truth

Tuyuca and neighbouring languages, Vaupés, Colombia and Brazil

Evidence built into grammar

Speakers must mark on the verb how they know what they say: seen, heard, inferred, assumed, or told by someone else. A claim with no source is simply ungrammatical.

Bends: knowing by sight and the lone observer

Guugu Yimithirr, Hope Vale, Queensland

Never left, always north

The language has no words for left and right. Speakers describe everything by compass direction, so they keep constant track of orientation, indoors, in the dark and in remembered scenes.

Bends: the bounded individual

Aymara, the Andes

The future behind you

The word for front, nayra, also means the past: what has happened is in view, ahead of you. Speakers gesture forward for the past and over the shoulder for the unseen future.

Bends: time running forward

Ongee, Little Andaman

“How is your nose?”

The greeting asks after your nose. The Ongee named their calendar by the scents of flowering seasons and understood a person’s life force as smell.

Bends: knowing by sight and the lone observer

Kaluli of Bosavi, Papua New Guinea

Hearing the forest

Knowledge of the rainforest is organised by sound; birds carry the voices of the dead; songs trace journeys through named places. The ethnomusicologist Steven Feld called it acoustemology, knowing through sound.

香道 kōdō, Japan

Listening to incense

One does not smell incense, one listens to it, 聞香. In kumikō games, participants identify woods matched to poems and chapters of the Tale of Genji.

轮扁斫轮, 《庄子·天道》

The hand outranks the book

The wheelwright tells Duke Huan that the books he is reading are the dregs of the dead: the knack of a wheel lives in the hand and cannot be handed down in words.

Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā, India

Testimony as a source of truth

Classical Indian epistemology counts reliable testimony, śabda, as an independent means of knowledge beside perception and inference, where the European Enlightenment taught suspicion of anything not verified first-hand.

Rasa theory, Nāṭyaśāstra and Abhinavagupta

Knowing by savour

Aesthetic experience is a tasting, rasa, through which a cultivated audience member, the sahṛdaya, one with a heart, comes to know an emotion in its general form.

Haudenosaunee, 17th century

Dreams that the village must fulfil

At the Midwinter rites people told their dreams as riddles, and the community had to guess the soul’s wish and grant it, since an unfulfilled dream-wish could make the dreamer ill.

Kinship, oath, sanctuary and succession

Mosuo, Lugu Lake, Yunnan and Sichuan

Walking marriage, 走婚

Households are run by matrilineal kin. Lovers visit at night and do not marry or live together; children grow up in the mother’s house, where uncles take the father’s part.

Ju/ʼhoansi, Kalahari

Kin by name

Sharing a name makes two people kin, with the obligations that follow. Through hxaro gift partnerships, families keep ties across hundreds of kilometres as insurance against drought.

Islamic law of raḍāʿa

Kin by milk

A child nursed by a woman becomes her kin and her children’s sibling, with marriage barred between them. Milk kinship was used to bind families and allies.

Burrnesha, northern Albania

The sworn virgin

Under the customary Kanun, a woman can vow lifelong celibacy and live as a man, heading her household and speaking in councils.

Albania

Besa, the given word

A sworn promise binds beyond any law. In the Second World War, Albanian Muslim families sheltered Jewish refugees under besa, and Yad Vashem has honoured many of them.

Pashtunwali, Afghanistan and Pakistan

Sanctuary for the asking

Under nanawatai, anyone who comes to your door asking protection must receive it, even an enemy, and hospitality, melmastia, is owed to every guest.

Tibetan tulku

Succession by rebirth

When a great lama dies, his successor is sought as a child recognised as his rebirth. The Karmapa line began this in the 13th century; the Dalai Lamas followed.

Twelver Shiʿa Islam

A ruler who is absent

The Twelfth Imam has been in occultation since the 9th and 10th centuries. Supreme authority is held as present yet hidden, and the jurists act as its stewards in the meantime.

Margins that hold power

Late imperial and Republican cities

China’s beggar guilds

Beggars organised under headmen, 丐头 or 团头, who were recognised by officials, kept registers and discipline, and collected fees from shops in exchange for keeping beggars away on festival days. The historical ground under 丐帮.

Komusō of the Fuke sect, Edo Japan

The basket-hat monks

Itinerant monks whose deep woven hats hid their faces, playing the shakuhachi for alms. They held rights to pass the highway checkpoints, and many believed some served as the shogunate’s eyes.

Bends: authority at the centre

Edo Japan

Outcastes with their own government

The outcaste communities of eastern Japan ran their own hierarchy under a hereditary chief, Danzaemon, with official authority over the leather trades, prison duties, and the region’s street performers and beggars.

Bends: authority at the centre · law as the state’s writing

South Asia

Hijra

Centuries-old communities of third-gender people organised in households of guru and disciple, with their own councils. They hold the ritual power to bless births and weddings and to curse, and served Mughal courts as trusted attendants. India’s Supreme Court recognised a third gender in 2014.

Yurodivye, Russia

Holy fools

Ascetics who lived as madmen could rebuke tsars to their faces. In 1570 Nikola of Pskov offered Ivan the Terrible raw meat in Lent, and tradition says Ivan spared the city. St Basil’s Cathedral is named for a holy fool.

The Malāmatiyya, Nishapur, 9th century

People of blame

Sufis who hid their devotion and courted public disapproval so that no one, themselves included, could take pride in their piety. 大隐于市, as spiritual method.

Bends: standing through holding

Bengal

Bauls

Wandering minstrel-mystics outside caste and scripture, who sing that the divine lives within the body. Lalon Fakir’s songs still cross the border between Bangladesh and India.

Kris

A frontier brotherhood that took in runaways of every origin and elected its leaders at open assemblies, a 梁山泊 of the steppe until Catherine II dissolved it in 1775.

Withdrawn, and called back

Optina Monastery, Russia, 19th century

The elders of Optina

Its startsy, hermit-elders, were sought out by Gogol, Dostoevsky and Tolstoy, who visited more than once. In his last days in 1910, Tolstoy left home and went there first.

Bends: authority at the centre

Beyul, the Tibetan Himalaya

Hidden valleys

Sacred refuges such as Pemakö, said to have been concealed by Padmasambhava and to open in times of crisis. Communities set out to find and settle them in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Bends: time running forward

Kamo no Chōmei, 《方丈记》, 1212

The ten-foot hut

After fire, famine and earthquake in Kyoto, a court poet withdrew to a hut he could take apart and carry. With Saigyō and Kenkō he stands at the head of Japanese recluse writing.

Bends: standing through holding

Yamabushi of Shugendō, Japan

Mountain ascetics

Ascetics who walk sacred peaks and serve villages as healers and exorcists. Their right to travel made them perfect cover: in the play Kanjinchō, the fleeing Yoshitsune passes a checkpoint disguised as one, while Benkei reads aloud a blank scroll as if it were a temple subscription.
