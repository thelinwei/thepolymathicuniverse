IV
Thread IV

Counted in streams

A race is a census box. A stream is a heritage that walked a road and came through a door.

Each share on this page carries three facts: who the people are, which road carried them to the Straits, and which door let them into the house. Fifty-six streams, eight roads, four doors and seven houses. Change any one of the three, and the house changes.

Heritage alone would put the city back into the charter’s four boxes. With the road attached, a Chinese share in Nine Rain says something different from a Chinese share in Toa Keng. One married the silver. The other sat the examination.

V
Thread V

Majority first, and why

Read from the largest stream down: the centre of gravity first, then how far the edges reach.

The largest stream is the house’s founding body, the people who carried its belief across water. Each smaller stream is a step away from that centre, and the last ones show the farthest reach of its weddings, neighbours and hires. Reading in this order, each house shows its root and then its reach.

Sri Temenggong
Nusantara first, at 62, because every road lands on this ground. Others married into the custodian house far more than it married out, so the root stays highest in the city.
Lan Fang
Chinese first, at 58, carried by the busiest road in the Straits. The second stream is Dayak and Peranakan, because the kongsi married where it settled, in Borneo first.
Al-Merikani
South Asian first, at 32, by two points over Arab and Persian. The house is the Indian Ocean itself: Arabic is its law and liturgy, Tamil and Sanskrit its sky and grammar.
Sané-Kora
African first, at 42, and the next three streams arrived on the same boats. Sané-Kora was already mixed when it landed.
Toa Keng
European and Eurasian first, at 44, and under half. The rest came by examination, which is the one door Toa Keng holds open.
Nine Rain
Americas first, at 44, then the Austronesian crews who sailed the galleons. The third stream is Chinese because silver married the people who counted it.
Sund
Nordic first, at 46, the least mixed house. Every other stream signed on as crew.
VI
Thread VI

Roads bring people to the city. Doors bring them into a house.

Four doors, each with its own speed and depth.

Born in is the founding body and those who arrived alongside it. Married in brings whole households: kitchens, home languages, ways of mourning. Next door brings the neighbours’ children who enrolled where they played, and with them gods, festivals and habits of mind. Through work brings crews, clerks and apprentices who joined for the trade and stayed.

Trade is the road and work is its door: fast, wide and shallow. Marriage is slower and deeper. Proximity is slowest, and it changes people most.

VII
Thread VII

The rings, read from the doors

No house was placed by hand. The doors decide.

A house sits in Proximity when at least 8 per cent of its people came in from next door. Otherwise it sits in Marriage when at least 10 per cent married in. Every other house sits in Trade.

Read this way, four houses stand in the centre, two in marriage and one in trade. The rule moved Sané-Kora into the centre on its own: fifteen per cent of its people are neighbours’ children who came for the songs. Toa Keng and Nine Rain have married but have no one from next door. Sund has only hired.

Thread VIII

Roads into houses

Seven hundred people-points, every one carried by a road.

The northeast monsoon and the Cape route carry the most people, and both reach every house. Galleon silver carries fewer, yet it also reaches all seven. The Sahel and hajj road flows into one house only, which is why Sané-Kora needs Al-Merikani as its bridge to the city. The northern river road is the faintest thread on the page: eight points, linking Sund to Al-Merikani through Baghdad’s silver.

XVI
Thread XVI

The history beneath

The fiction walks on these.

  • Spanish and Mexican silver dollars were the everyday currency of the Straits Settlements through much of the nineteenth century. The silver came largely from Potosí and Mexico, and much of it crossed the Pacific on the Manila galleons, many of them crewed by Filipino sailors.
  • Hakka miners of the west Borneo kongsis, the Lanfang kongsi among them (1777–1884), commonly married Dayak women.
  • Hadhrami Arab families such as the Alsagoff, Aljunied and Alkaff, and Tamil Muslim Chulia traders, shaped early Singapore. The royal house of Perlis is of Hadhrami descent.
  • Peranakan Chinese, Jawi Peranakan, Chitty Melaka and Kristang are peoples born of intermarriage in the Straits.
  • Malagasy, the language of Madagascar, is most closely related to Ma’anyan of southeast Borneo.
  • Polynesians grew the sweet potato before Europeans entered the Pacific, and genetic studies point to contact with South Americans around 1200.
  • Mansa Musa’s pilgrimage of 1324 tied Mali to Cairo and Mecca, and Timbuktu became a centre of Arabic-script scholarship.
  • The Siddi are communities of African descent in western India, carried across the Indian Ocean over centuries.
  • Lascars, sailors largely from South Asia, crewed European ships across the world’s oceans; Kru sailors from the West African coast were renowned crews of the nineteenth-century Atlantic.
  • Tens of thousands of Arab silver dirhams lie in Viking-age hoards on Gotland, and Ibn Fadlan met the Rus on the Volga in 921–922.
  • Missionary presses at Melaka and Singapore, from the 1810s, were among the first printers in the Straits.
  • Chinese Singaporeans pray to Datuk Gong, Malay keramat spirits, and the Keramat Habib Noh at Tanjong Pagar draws visitors from every community.
  • Agnes Joaquim, of Singapore’s Armenian community, bred the orchid that became the national flower.
  • Singapore’s Chinese, Malay, Indian and Others categories descend from colonial census classification.
