The ocean is the road, and the keepers know how to cross it.

Claude3 November 2026 · reference · Nine Rain’s real-world roots, volume two

The outcomeThree things to carry out

  1. Austronesian and Polynesian voyagers spread across more than half the world’s longitudes, from Madagascar to Rapa Nui, in sailing canoes navigated without instruments, and reached South America centuries before Columbus. Aboriginal Australians made the first known sea crossings of all, tens of thousands of years earlier.
  2. In the Pacific, ledgers, maps and constitutions live in unexpected vessels: stones too big to move whose ownership is held in shared memory, swell maps made of sticks and memorised on land, the seating order of a kava circle.
  3. The keepers here are navigators, orators who speak for chiefs, women whose call opens every encounter between peoples, envoys carrying carved sticks as passports, and weather-holders whose power over typhoons bound an archipelago in mutual aid.

Prologue

Hōkūleʻa, 1976

Four thousand kilometres with no instrument aboard.

On 1 May 1976 a double-hulled voyaging canoe named Hōkūleʻa left Maui for Tahiti, about four thousand kilometres away, with no compass, chart or instrument aboard. Its navigator, Mau Piailug of the tiny Micronesian island of Satawal, steered by stars, swells, birds and the colour of the sea. Thirty-four days later the canoe sailed into Papeʻete, where some seventeen thousand people came down to the shore to meet it.

That voyage reopened a world: the ocean as a road and a home, crossed by people whose gifts were navigation, oratory, exchange and the power to make strangers into kin.

III
Thread III

The peoples, era by era

before 3000 BCE

Sahul, the first sea people

Peoples of Australia and New Guineafrom c. 65,000 BCE

The ancestors of Aboriginal Australians and New Guineans crossed open water from Southeast Asia at least 50,000 years ago, and Madjedbebe in Arnhem Land may push that past 65,000: the earliest known sea crossings by modern humans. Theirs is the oldest continuous culture on earth.

ReachAn entire continent, held in law and story along songlines thousands of kilometres long.

Kuk Swamp and Budj Bimc. 7000 – 4000 BCE

In the New Guinea highlands, farmers drained the Kuk wetlands for taro, banana and yam 7,000 to 10,000 years ago, one of the few independent inventions of agriculture. In Victoria, the Gunditjmara built stone channels and weirs at Budj Bim to farm eels some 6,600 years ago.

Meanwhile

ChinaNeolithic villages; rice in the Yangtze, millet in the Yellow River valley.

WestFarming spreads across Europe; the first megaliths.

c. 3000 – 500 BCE

Out of Taiwan, and the Lapita voyagers

Austronesian dispersalc. 3000 – 1500 BCE

Speakers of Austronesian languages moved from Taiwan into the Philippines and Island Southeast Asia. Around 1500 BCE some sailed over two thousand kilometres of open ocean to the Marianas, among the longest sea crossings of their age.

ReachToday about 1,200 Austronesian languages are spoken from Madagascar to Rapa Nui.

Lapitac. 1600 – 500 BCE

From the Bismarck Archipelago, makers of dentate-stamped pottery sailed into Remote Oceania, islands no human had reached, and settled Vanuatu, New Caledonia, Fiji, Tonga and Samoa within a few centuries. Their canoes carried pigs, dogs, chickens, taro, yams and breadfruit.

Meanwhile

ChinaShang and Zhou.

WestMycenaean Greece, then the Greek city-states.

c. 500 – 1300 CE

The long reach

East Polynesiac. 1000 – 1300

After a pause of nearly two thousand years in western Polynesia, voyagers settled the Society Islands, the Marquesas, Hawaiʻi, Rapa Nui and finally Aotearoa New Zealand, the last large landmass reached by humans, all within a few centuries.

Contact with the Americasc. 1200

Sweet potatoes from South America were growing in Polynesia before any European arrived, and a 2020 genetic study found Native American ancestry in eastern Polynesians dating to around 1200.

Madagascarc. 500 – 700

Austronesians from southeastern Borneo crossed the Indian Ocean and, together with East Africans, became the Malagasy. Malagasy is closest to languages still spoken in Borneo.

Meanwhile

ChinaTang and Song.

WestByzantium; Norse voyagers reach North America around 1000.

c. 1100 – 1700

Stone cities and sea chiefdoms

Nan Madol and Leluc. 1100 – 1800

On Pohnpei, the Saudeleur dynasty ruled from Nan Madol, nearly a hundred artificial islets built on a reef of stacked basalt columns, perhaps 750,000 tonnes of stone. On Kosrae, the walled basalt city of Lelu followed.

Tongafrom c. 950

The Tuʻi Tonga line built terraced royal tombs at Muʻa and the Haʻamonga ʻa Maui, a trilithon of coral stone. At its height Tonga’s sea-chiefs drew tribute and marriages from Samoa, Fiji and beyond.

Yap

Yapese carved disc-shaped rai stones in Palau, some four hundred kilometres away, and ferried them home by canoe. Through the sawei, the outer islands sent tribute to Yap and received help after typhoons.

Rapa Nuic. 1250 – 1700

Islanders carved nearly a thousand moai from the Rano Raraku quarry and, by the island’s own tradition and modern experiment, “walked” them upright to their platforms by rocking them with ropes.

Hawaiʻic. 1100 – 1795

Chiefdoms divided each island into ahupuaʻa, wedge-shaped land units running from mountain to reef, and built stone-walled fishponds that raised fish on the tides.

Meanwhile

ChinaYuan and Ming.

WestThe Renaissance; Magellan crosses the Pacific in 1521.

c. 1700 – 1900

Kingdoms meeting the world

Kingdom of Hawaiʻi1795 – 1893

Kamehameha I unified the islands by 1810. The kingdom adopted a constitution, built one of the most literate societies of its century, and sent envoys to Washington, London and Tokyo before the monarchy was overthrown in 1893.

Tongafrom 1845

George Tupou I unified Tonga and gave it a written constitution in 1875. Tonga never lost its monarchy to colonial rule.

Aotearoa1840 onward

Māori chiefs signed the Treaty of Waitangi with the British Crown in 1840, and in 1858 several iwi chose a Māori king, founding the Kīngitanga that continues today.

Rapa Nuic. 1700 – 1867

Each spring, champions raced down the Orongo cliffs and swam to the islet of Motu Nui to bring back the first sooty-tern egg; the winner’s patron ruled as the birdman for a year.

Meanwhile

ChinaThe Qing.

WestThe Industrial Revolution and the scramble for the Pacific.

IV
Thread IV

Traditions that grew differently

Set against the Chinese and European worlds, these are the ideas that stand out most sharply.

Polynesia and Micronesia

Navigating by knowledge alone

Navigators held a star compass in their heads, read swells by the motion of the hull beneath them, and followed birds and the colour of lagoon light on clouds. Carolinian navigators pictured the canoe as still while a reference island moved along the horizon, a method called etak.

Marshall Islands

Maps you memorise

Stick charts of palm ribs and shells model how ocean swells bend and cross around islands. Navigators studied them on land and left them behind when they sailed.

Yap

Money that never moves

Rai stones, some taller than a person, stay where they stand while ownership passes by common knowledge. One stone lost in a storm at sea still counted as its owner’s wealth, because everyone remembered it.

Trobriand Islands

Gifts that travel in circles

In the kula, shell necklaces circle the island ring clockwise and armbands anticlockwise. No one keeps them for long; each carries the history of its holders, and the partnerships they create last lifetimes.

Across Polynesia

Tapu, mana and rāhui

Sacred restriction, tapu, and spiritual authority, mana, organised law. A rāhui or kapu could close a reef or a forest for a season so it could recover, a conservation law that still operates.

Hawaiʻi

Sanctuary as law

Someone who broke a kapu, or a defeated warrior, who reached a puʻuhonua, a place of refuge, could be absolved by its priests and go home free.

Fiji, Tonga, Samoa, Kiribati

Seating as a constitution

Who sits where in the kava circle or the meeting house, and who is served first, states each person’s and each lineage’s standing. The ceremony is the constitution, performed.

Melanesia and Rapa Nui

Leadership won by giving and by contest

Highland Big Men gained standing by giving away pigs and shells in ever larger moka exchanges. On Rapa Nui, leadership for a year went to the patron of the winner of a race for an egg.

Aotearoa, Samoa, Hawaiʻi

Archives worn on skin and cloth

Tā moko records lineage on the face; the Samoan peʻa marks a man’s coming of age; fine mats, ʻie tōga, carry names and histories as they pass between families.

V
Thread V

Roles with no Western or Chinese twin

Across the ocean, the people who carry a society’s weight are often the ones who can cross it, speak at its thresholds, or keep its bonds in motion.

KeepersAt sea
Carolinian palu and their kin across Oceania

Navigators

Masters initiated in the pwo ceremony, who held the star paths, swell patterns and sea signs of a whole region in memory. In 1976 Mau Piailug of Satawal guided the canoe Hōkūleʻa from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti and helped revive Polynesian navigation.

Marshall Islands

Ri-meto, the stick-chart masters

Navigators who taught swell-reading on land with stick charts, sometimes lying in a canoe in the lagoon to learn the feel of each swell with the body.

KeepersSpeakers for chiefs
Samoa

Tulāfale, the orators

Orator chiefs who speak for the high chiefs in the village council, hold the genealogies, and master the ceremonial language. Their skill decides how disputes resolve and how titles pass.

Fiji

Matanivanua, the face of the land

Heralds who speak for a chief, receive visitors, present the whale-tooth tabua that seals a request or apology, and carry the chief’s word between communities.

Tonga

Matāpule

Ceremonial attendants who run the royal kava ceremony, call each title to its cup in order, and speak for their chief.

KeepersOpening the encounter
Māori, Aotearoa

Kaikaranga, the callers

When visitors arrive at a marae, an elder woman of the hosts calls out, and a woman of the visitors answers. Their call opens every encounter between groups and names the dead each side brings. Before it, a warrior may lay down a token in challenge; the visitors take it up to show they come in peace.

Aboriginal Australia

Message-stick carriers

Envoys carried carved message sticks across the lands of other language groups to summon gatherings, arrange trade and negotiate. The stick was their credential and their safe conduct.

Yolŋu, Arnhem Land

Makarrata

A peace-making ceremony in which an offender faces the spears of the wronged group, and the conflict is settled. The word has become a national term for a settlement between Aboriginal people and the Australian state.

KeepersKeepers of lineage and knowledge
Māori

Tohunga and the whare wānanga

Experts in genealogy, navigation, carving and ritual, who taught in houses of learning where knowledge was recited exactly and held under tapu.

Hawaiʻi

Kūʻauhau and haku mele

Genealogists and composers who kept chiefly lines in chant. The Kumulipo runs over two thousand lines from the origin of the world to a royal family, and hula carried the stories as danced archive.

Rapa Nui

Tangata rongorongo

Reciters of the rongorongo boards, whose glyphs are still undeciphered; about two dozen inscribed objects survive.

KeepersWeather, land and abundance
Yap and its outer islands

Yap’s weather-holders

The outer islanders believed certain Yapese chiefs could send typhoons and sickness. Through the sawei they sent tribute and received land, food and shelter after storms: a weather power bound into mutual aid across hundreds of kilometres of ocean.

Trobriand Islands

Rain and garden magicians

The garden magician, towosi, set the farming calendar with spells and rites at every stage. The paramount chiefs of Omarakana were credited with rain magic and the power to bring drought.

Hawaiʻi

Konohiki and kilo

Konohiki managed each ahupuaʻa’s land and fisheries, opening and closing harvests; kilo watched fish, stars and weather and read the signs for everyone.

KeepersMakers of bonds
Massim islands, Papua New Guinea

Kula partners

Men who sail for days to receive a famous shell valuable from a partner, holding it only until it moves on. Renown comes from what passes through your hands.

Western Highlands, Papua New Guinea

Big Men and the moka

Leaders whose standing is earned, never inherited, by orchestrating great exchanges of pigs and pearl shells between clans. Gifts that must be returned with increase turn rivalry into obligation.

Vanuatu

Sand drawers

People who draw a single unbroken line in the sand to make complex figures that record stories, rituals and messages, and once served across the archipelago’s many languages.

Kwakwakaʼwakw and their neighbours, Pacific Northwest

Speakers of the potlatch

On the far shore of the ocean, hosts gave away wealth at great feasts while speakers recited the names and privileges being claimed and the guests witnessed them. Canada banned the potlatch from 1885 to 1951; it continues today.

VI
Thread VI

Why these roles carried weight

Lives hang on knowledge no one else can check

At sea, only the navigator knows where the island is. A community that trusts its survival to a person’s memory raises that person very high.

Islands need protocols for strangers

Every landing is an encounter between peoples. Callers, heralds, kava and message sticks make meeting safe, so the keepers of protocol become diplomats by default.

Networks are insurance

Kula partners, sawei ties and moka debts spread risk across islands and clans. After a typhoon or a famine, the people who hold the bonds hold the means of recovery.

Speech is too strong for the highest rank

As with the jeli and the okyeame, the high chief stays silent while the orator or the herald speaks, so words can be shaped and withdrawn without shaming the chief.

Knowledge is ranked and sealed

Tapu governs who may learn what, and exact recitation keeps it whole. Holding sealed knowledge is itself a form of rank.

Leads for Nine Rain

Quick first reactions from the ocean side of Nine Rain’s world: threads to pick up or leave.

Reading the swell

Navigators feel ocean moisture and motion through the hull. A Nine Rain sense of weather carried in the body.

Great stones that never move

Yap’s rai change hands by shared memory alone. Nine Rain’s great stones as its heirlooms and its ledger, whose weight is in what everyone knows.

Typhoon power as alliance

The sawei binds weather power to mutual aid. Nine Rain diplomacy: the house that can send the storm is the house that shelters you after it.

Walking the moai

Rapa Nui moved giant statues by rocking them on ropes. A Nine Rain art of moving great masses by rhythm and lowered friction.

Spit and water

Kava was once prepared by chewing the root and mixing it with water, and the bowl orders the gathering. A direct echo of Nine Rain’s instrument seed of spit or water from different places.

Maps of interference

Stick charts model where swells cross and reinforce. Nine Rain spells drawn as wave diagrams, and rāhui as seals laid on water.

The Tongan writer Epeli Hauʻofa asked his readers to see the Pacific as a sea of islands, one great home joined by the water. Its keepers are the people who know the way across.

Notes on dates and sources

Settlement dates for East Polynesia were revised later by radiocarbon work in the 2010s, and the earliest dates for Sahul remain debated. Accounts of the Tongan maritime chiefdom’s reach and of the sawei’s full extent vary between sources.
