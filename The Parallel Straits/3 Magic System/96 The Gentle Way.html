[The Gentle Way]柔道 · 止斗
(the martial practices of the seven houses: how each moves force, meets it, or ends the fight before anyone falls)

In the Al-Merikani square two duellists sit across a small table, two cups of tea between them, and the crowd holds its breath.

Claude1 November 2026 · second edition · companion to 89 The Martial Straits

The outcomeMost fights end early

Every house trains to move force, meet it and end the fight. What differs is which one it loves.

  1. Three families of force: move it (judo), meet it (taekwondo), or end the fight before either matters.
  2. Sund fights like judo with heat, drawing it out of blades until they snap and out of arms until they slow. Al-Merikani duels are wagers of nerve on terms sealed beforehand, some never spoken.
  3. Lan Fang fights like aikido and chooses the body’s response with one touch: stillness, laughter, tears, sleep. Toa Keng makes the best archers and referees; its rulings bind.
  4. Sri Temenggong fights with roots and vines, and with animals when they agree. Nine Rain brings its own weather. Sané-Kora fights in quiet sing-offs where every verse lands.

I
Thread I

Three families of force

Move it, meet it, or end the fight.

Judo moves force: it takes an attack and sends it somewhere else. Taekwondo meets force with greater force. A third family ends the fight before either applies. Every house trains all three and loves one of them most.

移力

Move the force

Judo, taiji push-hands, aikido

Take the opponent’s own force and send it somewhere else. Unbalance first, then let them fall. The defender spends almost nothing.

迎力

Meet the force

Taekwondo, karate, boxing

Answer force with greater force, delivered precisely. Speed, power, the board that breaks. Clear and honest.

止斗

End the fight

Disarms, wagers, songs

Take away the weapon, the will or the reason to fight. The fight is over before anyone falls.

In our world

The arts the houses borrow from, kept plain.

1882Kanō Jigorō founds the Kōdōkan in Tokyo and calls his art jūdō, the gentle way. Its principles: unbalance the opponent first (kuzushi), use the least effort for the most effect, and train for mutual benefit.
1950sTaekwondo is formalised in Korea: kicks, speed and impact, with board-breaking as a test of power.
1920s–30sUeshiba Morihei develops aikido, built on blending with an attack and redirecting it.
long traditionTaiji push-hands (推手) trains two people to feel and yield to each other’s force.
long traditionFilipino kali and eskrima teach disarming as a core skill, not an afterthought.
long traditionSilat Harimau, the Minangkabau tiger style, fights low to the ground, in the manner of the animal.
long traditionGlíma, Iceland’s wrestling, is fought upright, gripping each other’s belts.
long traditionLaamb, Senegalese wrestling, is fought to drums and praise-singing; dambe is Hausa boxing; tahtib is Egypt’s stick-dance. In fencing, “measure” is the word for distance.

Seven ways to end a fight

Each house’s signature art, with its disarm.

Sund · Heat, moved

The Cooling 冷却

Sund fights the way judo throws: it moves energy rather than meeting it. A blade has its heat drawn out until the steel turns brittle and snaps on the next parry. An attacker’s arms cool until they move like honey. A blow is spread across the ring behind the fighter, or into the deck underfoot.

The heat taken from a fight is given back afterwards, to warm the wounded and boil the tea.

Roots
Glíma: upright, belt-gripping wrestling. Sund children learn to fall before they learn anything else.

Lan Fang · The healer’s art

The Hundred Responses 百应

Lan Fang fighters look like what they are: slight, quiet physicians. They fight like aikido, blending with an attack and turning it aside, and they know the body better than anyone alive. When they strike, they choose what the body does next. A touch on one meridian point immobilises a limb; another makes a raging man burst out laughing until he sits down; others bring tears, a yawn, hiccups, sudden hunger, deep sleep. The most serious strike is fatal, and is taught last, to very few.

A Lan Fang duel can end with both fighters laughing, and often does.

Roots
Aikido and taiji in our world; 点穴 in every wuxia novel. Lan Fang children learn the acupoints with their first forms, by finding them on each other.

Toa Keng · Archers and referees

The Fair Call 公断

Toa Keng makes the best archers in the world. Lens sights, prism-checked lines and a measured distance mean an arrow lands exactly where it was meant to, at any range a lens can see. Toa Keng also referees every contest in the Straits. When a Toa Keng referee sees something unfair, she grows angry, and the anger is powerful: she calls the foul aloud, and the ruling binds. The offender cannot move until the fault is put right.

Toa Keng referees are the most trusted people at any match, and the most feared by anyone who cheats.

Roots
Fencing and archery from the European Rooms. Toa Keng children learn to judge distance by eye and to know the rules of every game they play.

Al-Merikani · Fights as agreements

The Wager 赌约

An Al-Merikani duel looks bizarre to outsiders. The terms are agreed and sealed with signets before anything happens, and they can be anything: the first to blink, the first to speak, the one whose tea stays warm longest, the first to look away from the other’s eyes. Some terms are never said aloud; the two duellists settle them with a look, and the crowd has to guess. The cowboy’s duel was about speed. These are about nerve, patience and reading the other person.

Crowds bet on the duels, and the Trust House holds the stakes on the Witness Chain. The most famous duel in Straits history lasted eleven hours and ended in a smile.

Roots
Tahtib, the stick-dance, and archery. Al-Merikani children learn the stick-dance as a festival game and play staring contests for marbles.

Sri Temenggong · Plants first, animals second

The Living Ground 活地

Sri Temenggong fights alongside living things. Roots rise to trip a charge, vines bind a sword arm, a thorn hedge grows across a lane in a breath. Animals join when they agree: hornbills scout, bees swarm a raider, and once in a generation a tiger chooses to fight beside a silat master.

Animals that have fought beside a master often come back to visit her for years.

Roots
Silat, including the tiger style low to the ground. Sri Temenggong children learn to ask the ground before they stand on it.

Nine Rain · Weather, made local

The Small Weather 小天气

Nine Rain brings its own climate to a fight. A fog settles over the opponent’s eyes and nowhere else; a cold rain makes every grip slip; a heat-haze bends the air; a gust arrives at the moment of the swing.

Its fighters always know the weather of the next minute.

Roots
The hip-ball game and the Andean runners’ trials. Nine Rain children learn to read the sky before they learn to throw.

Sané-Kora · Song against song

The Cutting Song 斗歌

A Sané-Kora fight is a sing-off, like a rap battle, quieter and far more dangerous. Two singers stand close and trade verses almost conversationally. Each verse lands: one bruises, one knocks the breath out, one breaks a guard, and a master’s last verse ends the fight outright. The crowd stays silent so as not to break the lines. Between friends, the same art becomes the lull, a song that softens anger until a raised fist forgets why it was raised.

The great cutting songs are remembered, and sung again at weddings with the dangerous verses taken out.

Roots
Laamb wrestling to drums, dambe boxing, and the praise-singer’s art. Sané-Kora children learn to keep time before they learn to grapple.

The children’s classes

What every house teaches first, before anything can hurt anyone.

Sund
Learn to fall

Breakfalls on the yard mats, then rings of four practising how to share one push.

Lan Fang
Learn the points

Forms, acupoint charts, and the sand-table siege games of the Mozi College.

Toa Keng
Learn the distance

Pacing, judging and sketching the space between two people.

Al-Merikani
Learn the terms

Stick-dance at festivals, and wagers with marbles that must be honoured.

Sri Temenggong
Learn to ask

Asking the ground, the tree and the neighbour’s dog before anything else.

Nine Rain
Learn the sky

Reading clouds and making a small mist in the palm.

Sané-Kora
Learn the time

Drumming, clapping and wrestling to rhythm.

Duets

When two houses fight on the same side.

Sund × Lan Fang

The cooled channel

Sund draws the heat out of a limb as Lan Fang closes its channel; the arm sleeps cool and wakes without pain.

Nine Rain × Sri Temenggong

The rain forest

Nine Rain brings the rain; Sri Temenggong’s roots drink it and rise twice as fast.

Al-Merikani × Sané-Kora

The sung contract

The terms of a wager are sung, so every witness remembers them exactly.

Toa Keng × Nine Rain

The mirage

Toa Keng’s light bends through Nine Rain’s haze, and a whole squad appears a street away from where it is.

Working notes

Builds on 89 The Martial Straits, which gave each house two fighting lineages. This volume adds the families of force and each house’s signature art.

Sri Temenggong and Nine Rain are distinct. Sri Temenggong works with plants first and animals second, directly. Nine Rain works with weather first.

New in this edition: Lan Fang’s Hundred Responses, Toa Keng as archers and referees, Al-Merikani’s wagers of nerve, and Sané-Kora’s cutting song.
