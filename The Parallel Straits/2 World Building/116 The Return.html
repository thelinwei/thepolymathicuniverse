[The Return]回味 · 百家饭
(how the Straits eats together: the twenty-five-year rite that lets an accord be tasted, the three tables, the seven palates, and the real precedents behind them)

On the Straits, an accord is kept in a jar and opened with a spoon.

Claude4 November 2026 · worldbuilding · food and accords

The outcomeFive things to carry out

  1. The Return (回味). When the Straits seals a great accord, every party carries part of a shared recipe home. Twenty-five years later they bring it back, changed by their own soil, water, weather and hands, and cook it together on the Straits.
  2. Uneven hands. Parties hold different roles. Ground keeps the raw: seeds, water, salt, honey. Hand keeps the method: the mother sauce, the spice blend, the song. Table cooks the final integration. Toa Keng holds the Measure, the role nobody asked for. The seven houses sit among any number of foreign shores; the first Return had eleven.
  3. Tended, inherited, chained. Each year on the sealing day, every holder opens their jar, tastes, feeds and writes one line in the Register. Carrier households pass the duty to their children. A ladle of every Return seeds the jars of the next accord, so each new accord holds all the older ones.
  4. Three other tables. The Guest Table for foreign delegations, served in the order the houses came to the Straits. The Season Table, where the houses meet around one named category. The Small Seal, a single bottle that keeps a smaller agreement.
  5. Inexactness, honoured. 适量, agak-agak, andaaz, quanto basta. Most of the Straits cooks by feel. Toa Keng’s horror at this turns, over two Returns, into a science of difference.

i
Part One

The Return 回味

回味 means the taste that lingers after a meal, and the act of remembering. Read literally, it says the flavour comes back. On the Straits it names a rite.

I
What it is

An accord you can taste

Relations, expressed in time, and in land.

A Straits accord is signed in ink and sealed in food. On the signing day the parties cook nothing. They divide a recipe. Seeds go to one delegation, a jar of mother sauce to another, a verse of the method to a third, rainwater in stoneware gourds to a fourth. Every party sails home holding a piece of a dish none of them can make alone.

At home, the piece lives a life. It is planted in that shore’s soil, fed with that shore’s salt, raised on that shore’s water, dried in that shore’s wind, sung at that shore’s harvests. Twenty-five years is long enough for rice to be resown fifty times, for a clove seedling to bear in earnest, for a sauce to darken into something new, for a child who watched the sealing to become the one who carries the jar back.

Then everyone returns. The jars are unsealed one by one, each shore announced as its jar opens, and the dish is cooked on the Straits from what came back. No two Returns taste alike, because no two accords lived the same twenty-five years. The Straits says a relationship can be read in a register and tasted at a table. Al-Merikani’s ledger counts what the parties owe one another. The Return lets them eat it.

The Straits has no hinterland. Its pantry is everyone else’s land, and the Return is how it keeps that pantry in the family.
II
Who holds what

Ground, Hand, Table, and the Measure

Unequal parts, freely chosen, and a little chaos at the start.

Parties take roles by what they are good at and what they love, and nobody pretends the parts are the same size. Some shores carry a single packet of seed. Some carry three things. A house may hold the Hand for one accord and only Ground for the next.

Ground 地

Keeps the raw. Seeds, water, salt, honey, a seedling. Its land does the cooking.

Hand 手

Keeps the method. The mother sauce, the spice blend, the timing, the song that holds the recipe.

Table 席

Cooks the integration on Return day. Chosen by acclaim of the returning parties, on the day itself.

Measure 衡

Keeps weights, vessels and the written protocol. Held by Toa Keng, by its own insistence.

III
The sequence

Twenty-five turning days

Sealed once, tended twenty-four times, opened together.

SealingTurning dayThe CallingThe Return
The Sealing 封

The accord is signed and the recipe divided. Every jar is filled in front of every party, and a ladle from the last Return’s Mother goes into each, so the new accord begins with the older ones inside it. Toa Keng weighs everything. Sané-Kora sings the whole Song once, end to end; it will not be heard whole again for twenty-five years.

The Carrying 携

Each shore names a Carrier household. The role is an honour, written into the Register and passed down. In several ports, keeper of the Straits jar has become a civic title, and children are brought to watch the jar arrive.

The Turning 翻

Once a year, on the sealing day, every holder opens the jar, tastes one spoonful, feeds it with salt, beans, water, a new planting or a verse, and writes one line in the Register. On the Straits the Register is read in public, so the city hears how its friends are keeping.

The Calling 召

In year twenty-four, Nine Rain reads the monsoons and names the Return day. Ships are timed to arrive within a week of one another. Nine Rain has delayed a Return by two days to wait for the right rain, and everyone agreed it was worth it.

The Unsealing 启

Jars are opened one at a time in the order they were sealed. The Carrier names the shore, reads its first and last Register lines, and passes a spoon around the room. This part takes most of a day and nobody hurries it.

The Cooking 合

The returning parties name the Table by acclaim, and the chosen cooks build the dish from what came back, to the Song, with every party’s piece somewhere in it. The Table rarely turns out to be the house that expected it.

The Sharing and the Mother 分

Everyone eats from the same pots. The new Mother, blended from every returned jar, is kept in the founding cellar until the next accord is sealed, and so the chain goes on.

By the late nineteenth century so many accords were in the cellar that their Returns overlapped. Today some Return falls due nearly every year, and the city always has one twenty-five-year story coming to the table.

IV
The dish

The Return Table 百家饭

Five parts, one song, and whatever the years made of them.

Each Return makes its own dish, but the table always has the same five parts. 百家饭 is the old Chinese custom of gathering rice from a hundred households to cook for a child’s blessing. On the Straits the hundred households are the shores.

The Rice

百家饭

Sri Temenggong’s seed, come back as many rices, cooked together in the blended waters. A spoonful holds short and long grain, red and white, hill and paddy.

The Mother

母酱

Every returned jar of Lan Fang’s sauce, tasted and blended on the day. The blend becomes the Mother for the next accord.

The Achar of All Shores

百岸渍

Each shore’s pickled vegetable, brought or made on arrival and tossed together. The achar principle on a plate: purity was never there.

The Cup

巡杯

Sund’s travelling spirit, poured with every partner spirit beside it. The first toast is drunk in the order the casks reached each port.

The Sweet

甘

Al-Merikani’s closer: a halwa made from the returned honey, cloves and Straits sugar, cut into as many pieces as there are parties, plus one for the next accord.

The Song

歌

Sané-Kora’s method, sung whole for the first time in twenty-five years. The cooks cook to it, and the meal lasts as long as the song.

V
1867 → 1892

The first Return

A mess, at first. Then the best thing anyone had eaten.

The Salt Accord was sealed in 1867, among the seven houses and eleven shores, to keep the ports of the eastern seas open to one another. Toa Keng wrote the protocol: eighteen glass jars of equal volume, quantities in grams, a schedule of feeding. Lan Fang refused the glass, because a ferment wants the dark, and the first argument of the accord ended with Toa Keng’s glass jars set inside Lan Fang’s stoneware. You could check a Mother by lifting the lid and looking down through the glass.

Twenty-five years later the ships came in over nine days, and almost nothing matched. The rice came back as eleven rices. Quanzhou’s Mother was red. Bergen’s was a black cake, wrapped in cloth, smelling of sea wind. Hội An had turned its rainwater into fish sauce, and Cebu had turned its into vinegar. Elmina sang its verse and then three more. Porto’s cask was light, and Porto said so cheerfully.

The returning parties gave the Table, by acclaim, to Sané-Kora’s kitchen, because the Song was the one thing that held every piece. For the first time a shore cook shared the head pot: the Hội An Carrier, who knew what twenty-five years of her fish sauce could do to a pickle. Lan Fang grated Bergen’s black cake over the rice like a spice, and it became the most fought-over spoonful at the table. The Song ran four hours. They cooked slower to match it.

The Toa Keng recorder’s last line in the protocol book is two words, and every Toa Keng student since has been taught them: Unrepeatable. Excellent.

VI
Toa Keng’s horrors

The matter of “some”

The house of the scale, among people who cook by feel.

Most of the Straits cooks by eye, hand, ear and nose, and has a word for it in every language spoken at the table.

No. 1 · On the Matter of “Some” 1867
Forty-one uses of 少许 or agak-agak counted in the sung recipe. The memorandum asks that each be replaced by a weight. Sané-Kora replies that the song would no longer scan.
No. 4 · Rain Is Not a Unit 1868
A protest that Nine Rain’s gourds differ in volume by up to a fifth. Nine Rain replies that so did the clouds.
No. 9 · The Standard Return Jar 1871
A graduated glass jar of fixed volume, proposed for all future accords. Adopted, and set inside stoneware, at Lan Fang’s request.
No. 23 · Concerning the Red Mother of Quanzhou 1876
A formal query about an unlisted ingredient. Quanzhou answers that the grandmother responsible is unavailable for comment.
No. 41 · Unrepeatable. Excellent. 1892
The last line of the first Return’s protocol book, and the end of the protests.

What came next is why Toa Keng now loves the Return. After 1892 its recorders stopped asking the jars to agree and started measuring how they differed: the saltiness, sourness, colour, sweetness and smell of every returned jar against the jar it left as. They called the record the Table of Divergences. By the second great Return, a Toa Keng student could taste a spoonful of Mother blind and name its shore. The house of the scale had become the house that can read a place in a flavour. It measures the inexactness of the others, and leaves it inexact.

The Table of Divergences is now the most consulted book in the founding cellar, and the other houses quietly rely on it.

Part Two

The other tables 三席

The Return is the grand rite. The houses also eat together at three other tables, each with its own manners.

VII
Guest, Season, Small Seal

How the houses share a meal

Guests eat history, the houses eat the season, and partners keep a bottle.

The Guest Table 迎宾席

For foreign delegations.

The banquet is served in the order the houses came to the Straits, with Sri Temenggong first, so a guest eats the city’s history course by course. Each house brings its showpiece. Lan Fang serves a broth so clear it looks like hot water. Sri Temenggong lays out forty kinds of leaf and flower to be eaten raw with sambal. Al-Merikani ends the meal with a sugar model of the guests’ own harbour.

Between the Straits courses comes the mirror course: a dish from the guests’ own shore, cooked by Straits hands from what the Straits knows of it. Guests taste home as their hosts understand it, and say what’s off. That correction is treated as the first gift of the visit.

The Season Table 时鲜会

For the houses’ own meetings.

The host names a single category, and every house brings its proudest new invention or its freshest produce in it. There have been salt years, sour years, first-rice years, smoke years, and Nine Rain’s famous water year, when the houses tasted waters the way others taste wine.

The plates go round in a ring, each house serving the next, so no house serves itself and every house is fed by every other. The tasting ends when the ring closes. A line on each dish goes into the Season Book, which is the best record anyone has of what the Straits was inventing in a given year.

The Small Seal 小封

For smaller agreements.

Two parties seal one vessel of wine, vinegar, sauce or honey, and set a term by the weight of the agreement: three years, seven or twelve. Small Seals are kept on numbered shelves in the Cellar of Accords and opened by both parties together.

The custom has spread well beyond diplomacy. Business partners seal a bottle when they open a firm. Each graduating cohort of every house seals a jar of its school’s ferment, opened at the tenth reunion. Couples marrying across houses seal one sauce from each family in a single jar.

Part Three

Seven palates 七味

Trade routes brought the Straits more ingredients than any single kitchen could use, so each house leaned toward what it loved: snacks, sweets, soups, spirits, ferments, the pot, the scale.

VIII
How each house eats

Uneven appetites

Some houses eat all day. Some drink. One weighs everything.

Sri Temenggong

The snack house, and the garden.

Leans tosmall eats all day long: kuih in a hundred colours, fritters, rice cakes wrapped in leaf. A meal can be thirty raw leaves, flowers and shoots eaten with sambal, each picked that morning for what it does to the body and the mood.

KitchenThe oldest pantry on the Straits is a seed house, and every cook is part-gardener. It keeps ferments of fish, prawn and fruit for the long monsoon months: pastes, durian ferments, tiny shrimp in brine. Its patience with poisonous seeds that become delicacies after forty days in ash is legendary.

At the shared tablethe opening course at every Guest Table, and the Ground of almost every accord.

On measuringAgak-agak. The palm is the cup and the eye is the scale.

Lan Fang

The liquid house: soup, tonic, tea.

Leans tobroths simmered for hours, tonics matched to the season and the body, tea for every hour. Its kitchen and its pharmacy share a wall, and its medicine school writes menus.

KitchenThe founding pot, a master stock kept since 1786 and fed every day. Every Lan Fang student tastes it on the first morning. The Mohist workshop builds its steamers, hoods and charcoal stoves.

On measuring适量 for quantity, but exact about time. Lan Fang trusts the clock and ignores the scale.

Al-Merikani

The sweet house: coffee, sherbet, halwa.

Leans todesserts and drinks. Every negotiation opens with coffee and closes with sweets, and the length of the sweet course tells you how the talks went.

Kitchensugar work raised to architecture, rose and orange-blossom waters, and the finest spice trade on the Straits. Its halwa keeps for months and travels well, which is why it fills every carrier’s chest.

On measuringweighs spice to the grain, like the traders it is, and never weighs sugar.

Sund

The spirits house: fire, smoke, the cask.

Leans todrink, and the foods that go with it: smoked and cured fish, rye crisps, pickled herring. Sund regulates heat, and its kitchens are its smokehouses and stills.

Kitchenthe travelling casks, which ride Sund’s own ships and cross the equator at the Straits’ front door. The bar at Sund works as a second parliament, and toasts there follow protocol.

On measuringby proof and by year. Precise about the spirit and relaxed about the snacks.

Sané-Kora

The house of the pot and the beat.

Leans tothe communal pot. Grains and roots are pounded in mortars that double as drums, and the meal is eaten from one bowl with the right hand.

Kitchenrecipes kept in song by its griots, palm wine tapped at dawn, and rice cook-offs that run like sing-offs. Rival kitchens trade boasts in verse while the pots cook.

On measuringby ear: so many strokes of the pestle, so many verses of simmering.

Nine Rain

The house of water and weather.

Leans todrinks and ferments: cacao beaten to a froth, maize beer, fish cooked in citrus without fire, potatoes freeze-dried by frost and sun, food baked in earth ovens under hot stones.

Kitchena cellar of rainwater graded by storm, each with its year and its sky. Nine Rain tastes water the way Sund tastes spirit, and lets weather do as much of the cooking as it will.

On measuringby weather. The right day matters more than the right gram.

Toa Keng

The house of the scale, the glass and the written recipe.

Leans tobaking and preserving: breads, pastries, jams and pickles in clear glass, cured meats from its European partners, cakes whose recipes run to numbered steps. It is the only house whose cookbooks have indexes.

Kitchenlooks like a laboratory, because it partly is one. Toa Keng’s glassworks and optics make the clear jars, the hydrometers and the lenses that let the other houses watch their ferments work.

At the shared tablethe Measure at every accord, and keeper of the Table of Divergences. Its students are, by general agreement, the best blind tasters on the Straits.

On measuringeverything, always. Now with a footnote: see Divergences.

Precedents · eight cards

Dishes of devotion

Food where the effort is the message.

Kaifeng, Henan · Qing dynasty to now

套四宝 · Four Treasures Nested

Four birds. One skin each. Not a single tear.

A duck is boned out whole through a small opening at the neck, so its skin stays a seamless sack. A boned chicken goes inside it, a boned pigeon inside the chicken, and a boned quail inside the pigeon, each with its own filling. The set simmers slowly and is opened at the table layer by layer.

Read more

All the skill is in the boning. The cook works the bones out from inside, turning each bird like a glove, and mastery means every skin comes out whole. A torn skin lets the broth flood in and the nesting collapses. The fillings classically include sea cucumber, mushrooms, ham and shrimp, and the four meats each give their flavour to the one inside.

Kaifeng was the Northern Song capital, and the city kept a court-level cuisine long after the court had moved away. Dishes like this are its memory of grandeur: food built to be unwrapped slowly, in front of guests.

At the Straits table: nesting as diplomacy. The Guest Table could nest one shore’s bird inside another’s.

Paris · 1807

Rôti sans pareil

Seventeen birds, one olive.

In his Almanach des Gourmands, Grimod de la Reynière described the “roast without equal.” A bustard holds a turkey, which holds a goose, and so on down through pheasant, chicken, duck, guinea fowl, teal, woodcock, partridge, plover, lapwing, quail, thrush, lark and ortolan to a garden warbler. Inside the warbler sits a single olive.

Read more

Grimod was a lawyer turned gourmand who more or less invented restaurant criticism, convening a jury of tasters to judge what Paris cooks sent him. The olive at the centre was itself stuffed, with anchovy and capers, so the whole roast ends in a mouthful of the sea.

Whether anyone cooked it whole in his day is unclear, and that is part of its charm: a banquet that exists perfectly on paper. The modern turducken is its practical, much smaller grandchild.

At the Straits table: Toa Keng keeps a shelf of paper banquets, dishes written down to be imagined rather than cooked.

England and France · 14th to 16th centuries

The cockentrice and the subtleties

Half pig, half capon, all theatre.

Medieval feast cooks sewed the front of a suckling pig to the back of a capon, roasted the creature and glazed it gold. Peacocks were skinned with their feathers on, roasted, then dressed back in their own plumage with the tail fanned and the beak gilded. Between courses came subtleties: castles, ships and saints in sugar and almond paste.

Read more

Subtleties often carried messages. A sugar figure might honour a new king, celebrate a marriage, or allude to a treaty, and guests read them like a political cartoon they could eat. The feast was a medium as much as a meal.

These between-course spectacles, the entremets, grew over the centuries into the great set pieces of French pastry: towers, temples and landscapes in sugar for state occasions.

At the Straits table: Al-Merikani ends the Guest Table with a sugar model of the guests’ own harbour.

Sichuan · late Qing to state banquets

开水白菜 · Cabbage in Boiling Water

It looks like cabbage in hot water. It is the opposite.

A few hearts of napa cabbage sit in what seems to be plain water. The water is a stock of old hen, duck, ham and bones, clarified again and again by stirring in minced chicken that gathers every particle, until it runs as clear as tea. The cabbage is bathed in it until it carries the whole stock’s depth.

Read more

The dish is credited to Huang Jinglin, a Sichuan chef who cooked at the late Qing court. It later became a state-banquet dish served to foreign guests, a quiet proof that Sichuan cooking runs far deeper than chilli.

Its aesthetic is 大味至淡, the greatest flavour approaching plainness. The boast is hidden inside the humility, and only someone who knows the work will see it.

At the Straits table: Lan Fang’s first course at every Guest Table.

Oaxaca, Mexico · colonial era to now

Mole negro

Thirty ingredients, burned on purpose.

Oaxaca’s black mole toasts dried chillies, chilhuacle negro above all, until they are nearly black, then grinds them with seeds, nuts, spices, plantain, bread, tomato and chocolate, often more than thirty ingredients. It cooks down for hours into a sauce so dark it reads as night, and families make it for weddings, saints’ days and the Day of the Dead.

Read more

Making it is a many-handed affair that can run over days, with relatives taking turns at toasting, soaking and grinding. Grinding was once done on a stone metate. Today many families buy the paste in blocks at market and finish it at home.

Legends credit convent nuns, but the deeper root is the Indigenous tradition of molli, sauces, meeting the spices, almonds and sesame that arrived by sea. It is a dish where purity was never possible.

At the Straits table: a many-handed sauce is a Return in miniature. Nine Rain’s partner shores would recognise it at once.

Lake Biwa, Shiga, Japan · over a thousand years

Funazushi

Sushi’s grandmother smells like a cheese cave.

Crucian carp from Lake Biwa are gutted through the gills, packed in salt for months, then layered in cooked rice and weighted down to ferment for a year or more. The rice turns to a sour paste, and the fish becomes translucent, tangy and pungent, sliced thin with its amber roe.

Read more

This is narezushi, the ancient form that sushi descends from. Rice was originally just the fermenting medium and was thrown away. Over the centuries people began eating the rice too, then shortened the fermentation, and eventually swapped it for vinegar, which leads to the sushi eaten today.

Funazushi is still made by families and specialist shops around the lake, and given as a winter gift. It is an acquired taste, and an honour to be offered it.

At the Straits table: a dish where time is the main cook, a Return inside one household.

Peranakan kitchens of the Straits and Java

Buah keluak

Poison seed. Forty days in ash. Family treasure.

The seed of the kepayang tree contains hydrogen cyanide and is deadly raw. So the seeds are boiled, then buried in ash or earth for about forty days until the flesh turns black, oily and safe. Peranakan cooks crack each shell, dig out the paste, mix it with spices or minced meat, stuff it back and braise it in tamarind gravy: ayam buah keluak.

Read more

The taste is earthy and bittersweet, somewhere between dark chocolate and black olive. Eating it is its own small ritual: diners dig the paste out of the shell with a teaspoon or a pick, so dinner becomes an excavation.

The nut is old in Indonesian cooking too. East Java’s rawon gets its black broth from it. In Peranakan homes the cracking and scraping is slow hand work, often done the day before, and the dish signals effort and devotion at family occasions.

At the Straits table: the most local precedent here, and Sri Temenggong’s kind of patience.

Dehesa of Extremadura and Andalusia, Spain

Jamón ibérico de bellota

The pig’s last winter is the recipe.

Black Iberian pigs spend the montanera, roughly October to February, roaming oak woodland and eating acorns, putting on much of their final weight. The diet writes itself into the fat, which turns soft and rich and melts close to body temperature. The hams then cure for years.

The dehesa is itself an engineered landscape: holm and cork oaks spaced across pasture, managed for centuries so that trees, pigs, sheep and cork harvesters share the same ground. Labels certify the pig’s breed and diet, down to whether it truly finished on acorns.

It shares the logic of the 乾坤蛋 geese: what an animal eats in its last weeks becomes the flavour on the plate.

At the Straits table: the land feeds the animal that feeds the guest. Sri Temenggong’s whole philosophy in one ham.

Shaoxing, Zhejiang · recorded from the 4th century

女儿红 · Daughter’s Red

Brewed at her birth. Opened at her wedding.

When a daughter was born, the family brewed yellow wine, sealed the jars with clay and buried them. On her wedding day, perhaps eighteen years later, the jars were dug up and poured for the guests. For a son, the wine was 状元红, saved for the day he passed the imperial examinations.

Read more

An early southern botanical record, 《南方草木状》, attributed to the Jin-dynasty writer 嵇含, already describes families brewing wine at a daughter’s birth and burying it until she married. Shaoxing jars were later painted with auspicious scenes, which is where 花雕 gets its name.

Time made the wine precious, more than any price. The jar stood for a life, and opening it marked the day that life turned.

At the Straits table: the direct ancestor of the Small Seal, and the seed of the Return.

Bohol and Cebu, Philippines · 1521 and 1565

Sandugo · The Blood Compact

One cup. Two bloods. A treaty you drink.

In the Visayas, alliances were sealed by blood compact. Each party drew a little blood from the arm and mixed it into a cup of wine, and both drank. In 1565 Datu Sikatuna of Bohol and Miguel López de Legazpi performed it, and it is remembered as the first treaty of friendship between Filipinos and Spaniards.

Read more

Accounts describe the mixture being divided between two cups, with neither party allowed to leave until both were empty. Magellan’s expedition had made similar compacts with Visayan rulers in 1521. The ritual took the agreement inside the body: the friendship became part of each person.

The Philippines still honours it. The Order of Sikatuna, a presidential decoration for diplomatic service, is named after the datu, and Bohol holds a Sandugo festival every year.

At the Straits table: the neighbouring precedent, born in these waters. Cebu’s Carrier brings a cup shaped for two.

Zhou China · Spring and Autumn period

歃血为盟 · Sealed in Blood

Lips touched with blood. The oath buried with the beast.

When Zhou-era lords swore a covenant (盟), an animal was sacrificed, the parties touched its blood to their lips (歃血), and the oath text was read aloud to the spirits. One copy was buried with the sacrifice, and each party kept another.

Read more

In 1965, at Houma in Shanxi, archaeologists found thousands of jade and stone tablets inscribed in red, buried in pits with sacrificed animals. These are the 侯马盟书, covenant texts from the state of Jin around the fifth century BCE.

The covenants were buried to be witnessed by the spirits. Twenty-four centuries later they were witnessed again, by us.

At the Straits table: sealed, buried, witnessed. A Return whose opening took two thousand four hundred years.

The Levant, Arabia and Slavic lands · antiquity to now

The covenant of salt, and bread and salt

Salt doesn’t spoil. Neither should we.

The Hebrew Bible speaks of a “covenant of salt,” an everlasting bond. Salt preserves, and a promise made with salt claims the same. In Arabic, “there is bread and salt between us” (بيننا عيش وملح) means: we have eaten together, so betrayal is now unthinkable.

Read more

Across the Slavic world, honoured guests are still greeted with a loaf and a salt cellar, khleb-sol, at weddings and state visits alike. Salt was at once currency, medicine and preservative, so sharing it carried weight.

In all these traditions, eating together counts as an oath, sometimes a stronger one than ink.

At the Straits table: “bread and salt” as the city’s everyday phrase for a friendship you owe.

The Douro and London · 1373 to 2023

Graham’s port for the oldest alliance

The world’s oldest alliance, bottled.

The alliance between England and Portugal goes back to a treaty of 1373, the oldest still unbroken. For its 650th anniversary, Graham’s bottled two casks of a 2011 single-harvest tawny port, aged in barrel for over a decade, and released it only in the two countries.

Read more

The wine and the friendship grew up together. British merchants helped build the Douro’s port trade in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and a later trade treaty in 1703 lowered duties on Portuguese wine in England. The alliance shaped a wine, and the wine now toasts the alliance.

The bottle was chosen for the anniversary rather than sealed at the signing, which makes it the nearest modern echo of the Return at the level of states.

At the Straits table: the Straits seals on the signing day, and opens on the anniversary.

Japan, and Perth · Edo era to 2026

Kagami biraki · Opening the mirror

Break the lid, share the luck.

At openings, New Year and celebrations, a sake cask’s round lid is broken with wooden mallets and the sake ladled out to everyone present. In 2026, for the fiftieth anniversary of the Japan–Australia friendship treaty, a kagami biraki was held at the Perth Japan Festival to wish for friendship.

Read more

The “mirror” is the round lid, which shares its name and luck with the round rice cakes offered at New Year and broken open in January. People say “opening” (開き) because opening carries good fortune, and they avoid the word for breaking.

Companies open casks to launch ventures and couples open them at weddings. Many hands on the mallet, one cask, everyone drinks.

At the Straits table: a gesture for the Unsealing, where every Carrier lays a hand on the mallet for the first jar.

Norway to the equator and back · 1805 to now

Linie aquavit

The main ingredient is a trip around the world.

In 1805 a Norwegian ship took casks of aquavit to the East Indies. They didn’t sell, came home, and the spirit was found to be better for the trip. Ever since, every cask of Linie, in old sherry oak, sails a four-month voyage that crosses the equator twice. The back of each label names the ship and the dates.

Read more

The rolling of the ship, the swings of temperature and the sea air push the spirit in and out of the wood. Distillers have tried rocking casks on land to copy it, without matching the real voyage. On today’s route, the two equator crossings fall near Singapore and Panama.

Linie means “line,” the equator itself. The distillery behind it was founded in 1821, and more than a thousand casks are at sea at any time.

At the Straits table: the Straits sits on the line. Sund’s casks cross it at the city’s front door.

Madeira and the tropics · 17th to 19th centuries

Vinho da roda

Wine that got better by going to India and back.

Ships calling at Madeira loaded wine for long voyages. Casks that crossed the tropics and came home unsold tasted richer, nuttier and nearly indestructible, and they sold at a premium as vinho da roda, “wine of the round voyage.”

Read more

Producers later learned to reproduce the voyage on land, heating wine in warm stores (estufagem) or leaving casks in hot lofts for years (canteiro). The result is a wine that keeps for a century, and stays good long after the bottle is opened.

Madeira was a favourite in the American colonies and is said to have toasted the signing of their Declaration of Independence.

At the Straits table: heat as a cook, and the equatorial Straits as the oven.

Bangkok and Tokyo · 1945 and 1970s to now

Perpetual broth

Same pot, three generations.

At Wattana Panich in Bangkok’s Ekkamai, the beef broth has run for over fifty years. Each night it is strained and kept, and the next morning it becomes the base for fresh beef and spices. The owner is the third generation to tend it. In Tokyo’s Asakusa, the oden shop Otafuku simmers its stew in a dashi first made in 1945.

Read more

The owner says there is no written recipe, only constant tasting. The idea is old and found everywhere: the Chinese 老卤, or master stock, fed with every braise; the eel sauce of Japanese shops, said to be the first thing a cook rescues from a fire.

Seen this way, a broth is less a dish than a family line. Each day adds to it and nothing quite starts over.

At the Straits table: Lan Fang’s founding pot, and the Mother that every Return passes to the next.

The Andean altiplano · before the Inca to now

Chuño

Freeze-dried by the mountain, long before the laboratory.

High in the Andes, in the cold dry months, potatoes are spread on open ground to freeze at night and thaw in the sun by day. People tread on them to squeeze out the water and repeat this over days until the potatoes are light, hard and stone-like. Chuño keeps for years.

Read more

White chuño, called tunta, is also soaked in running streams for weeks before the final drying. Frost, sun, wind and water each take a turn at the cooking.

The Inca filled storehouses along their roads with chuño and dried meat, enough to feed armies on the march and villages through a failed harvest.

At the Straits table: Nine Rain’s natural ingredient, and the method behind Callao’s chilli chuño.
