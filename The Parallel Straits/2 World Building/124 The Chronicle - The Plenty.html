(from the Covenant to the First Door: the paper that hid the burden and the night the world held, the Monsoon Charter, the great works built by consent, magic in every kitchen, and a Door opening where the Flash fell)

A generation that nearly broke the world with paper, then built it a better one by asking first.

How to read itOne spine, two sides

Time runs down the spine. The Straits is on the left and the world on the right. Events that move both cross the spine and span the page.

The StraitsThe worldBothA blank to fill

The Covenant of 1946 sealed a peace between teachers and learners. The world it sealed into was richer than any before it, and in a hurry. Three great exchanges in the north and in Osaka found a way to make magic feel free: a certificate that sent a working’s burden somewhere else. Their cities glowed, other ports bought in, and the burden piled up in valleys, reefs and islands that had never been asked.

When it all fell due on one quarter day, the Straits did what it had done for five centuries with a quarrel at sea. Every house marshalled its network, and on the Ninth Night millions of households lit one lamp each and held the world together until dawn. Out of that night, and out of the war in the Sound before it, came one law. The Monsoon Charter named the wrong both disasters shared, harm sent where nobody had agreed to carry it, and the Straits became the place where the world kept its ledger.

What followed was a good generation. The canals were built with the blessing of the people whose ground they crossed. The oceans became roads, the ports became neighbours, and a fever found in one harbour was answered in all of them within a week. Magic moved into kitchens and tenements; children learned their first workings beside their letters; whole cities began to look like the house they had learned from.

By the 1970s the makers could build things that outlived them, and cords that remembered a whole working. At the Evenki site, where the Flash had fallen in 1908, the keepers who had listened for two generations heard something answer. In 1983 the houses opened on purpose what had opened by accident, and the world found that it was not the only one.

The Trust House watches

The world · a.m. c. 25–29

Clean Paper净券

The Three Exchanges, the Bourse at Antwerp, the House of St George at Genoa and the Dojima at Osaka, sell certificates that send a working’s burden somewhere else. A merchant casts all day and feels nothing. The burden goes to sinks nobody asked: a reef, a mountain valley, the tundra, a fishing island. Their cities become the most comfortable in the world, and other ports buy in to keep up.

Tulip mania, the South Sea Bubble, and the mortgage bundles before 2008.

c. 1947
The Straits and the world · a.m. c. 29

The Falling Due到期

The Straits

Toa Keng’s counters see it first in the shape of the renewal banks: too much burden, all falling due on one quarter day.

The world

The deferred burden comes due everywhere at once. Frost in July over the lowlands; the tundra thaws and smokes; on three islands every person sleeps for a week; artefacts across half a continent die. Ports that never bought any Clean Paper are hit hardest, because they were the sinks.

The crash of 1929.

c. 1947
The Straits and the world

The night the world held九夜

The Straits

Each house marshals its network. The Trust House confesses and becomes lender of last resort. Nine Rain spreads the debt over seven seasons. Toa Keng publishes who cast, who profited and which valley paid. Sri Temenggong asks the harmed what they need. Lan Fang recomposes thawed ground into soil. Sané-Kora’s choirs wake the sleeping islands. Sund calls a hundred thousand volunteers into rings.

The world

On the Ninth Night, millions of households light one lamp and give one almost imperceptible measure each, and medicine, water, food and shelter run until dawn. The world holds for one night. The custom of the Ninth Night lamp begins here.

After 1947
The Straits and the world · a.m. c. 30

The Monsoon Charter季风宪章

The Straits

Signed in the Convocation Hall where the Kindling happened. The Straits now hosts four institutions: the Charter Hall, where members from beyond the Straits hold the majority; the Court of Workings, whose first case is the Warden of the Narrows, once he wakes; the Burden Ledger, open to anyone; and the Teachers’ Shield.

The world

Two disasters, one law, because the war and the crash were the same wrong: harm sent where nobody agreed to carry it. In war, teachers are protected, no mind is worked without consent, no Hush falls on a whole people, land may refuse and is never poisoned, stolen knowledge is returned. In trade, every working names its sink, no sink without consent, the ledger is public, and no bundle travels that nobody can read. The Straits hosts; the world governs.

The Geneva Conventions, and Bretton Woods in 1944.

The Canal of the Two Waters两海渠

Lan Fang recomposes the spoil into bricks for the canal towns and runs the clinics; Toa Keng surveys the line and lights both mouths. Every hand in a ring is paid, and the payroll is public on the Witness Chain.

Two seas meet at Suez

Egypt, a Concord port since 1600, gets the canal it waited three centuries to build with its people’s blessing. Nobody is conscripted. The sand is moved instead of shovelled.

The Suez Canal, 1859–1869, dug by conscripted peasants.

1950s
The Straits and the world · a.m. 30s

The Isthmus Stair地峡水梯

The Straits

Nine Rain keeps the lakes full by borrowing from Pacific squalls; Sri Temenggong asks the forest to hold its water and the mosquitoes to keep back; Lan Fang builds the Fever Line; Sund’s rings lift the ships; Al-Merikani writes the Guna share into the first tile of the Chain.

The world

The Guna Council refuses a cut and accepts a stair of forest lakes over the land. It keeps a share of every toll, and its seat becomes the Uncut Seat: in every great work since, the people of the ground hold a seat with the power to say no. The first great work built by consent.

The French attempt at Panama, in which some 22,000 workers died.

1950s–1960s
The Straits and the world · a.m. 30s–40s

The great works

The Straits

Each house leads one, grown from its own field.

The world

The oceans become roads and the ports become neighbours.

  • The Blue Lines. Nine Rain’s frictionless sea lanes; the Monsoon Express reaches Kilwa in three days.
  • The Deep Song. Sané-Kora’s songs carried through the deep sound channel, relayed by whales.
  • The Heat Road. Sund’s chain of season banks sending tropical heat north and northern cold south.
  • The Green Belt. Sri Temenggong and the Mande–Songhai Federation green the Sahel’s edge by asking.
  • The Fever Line. Lan Fang’s clinics in every port; a fever found in one is answered in all within a week.
  • The Lens Chain. Toa Keng’s matched lenses: look through the Straits glass and see the harbour at Uppsala.
  • The Kite Post. Mohist kites for couriers, doctors and the very rich, never for freight.

The Witness Chain证链

Al-Merikani’s ledger kept in many ports at once: every transaction a glazed tile sealed by both parties, each tile fired with the ring of the one before, so that a single changed tile rings false in every copy.

The world

The quarrel of measures

Toa Keng wants a world survey fixed through the Lens Chain. The other houses, and the Guna, object. The zone’s boundary stones are set on glide stones and follow the rivers as they move.

The Straits and the world · a.m. 32–52

The Household Years寻常之年

The Straits

Artefacts last a year. Renewal Day becomes each house’s annual festival, and the school yards turn into a market for a week. Green kidneys clean every Straits town; the old city’s quarters are tuned.

The world

Every child learns everyday workings beside their letters, and nearly half of adults hold a simple spell. Seven modernities, one rooted in each house, spread at their own speeds, and cities stop looking like one another.

Two in the afternoon on a tenement street. The Cool Hour arrives, every shutter opens at once, and children who used to sleep through the heat run out to play in it.

1950s–1960s
The Straits

The leaps, counted

  • Fevers. Deaths fall to a tenth within twenty years of the Kindling, and keep falling.
  • Cool homes. Cold chests go from one home in fifty to nine in ten in a generation.
  • The sea. The Straits to Kilwa shortens from three weeks to three days.
  • News. A letter from Timbuktu arrives the same evening.
The world

Seven firsts

  • The first concert. Heard in every port on the same evening.
  • The first winter. Uppsala goes without lamps at noon.
  • The first mangoes. Grown in a Sahel village.
  • The first crossing. A child crosses the Isthmus Stair alone, with a letter for her aunt.
