The evening should have ended with Muara. After a long search through Malay ethical vocabulary, hermitages, community halls, trees, riverbanks, sediment and tidal mouths, one school's identity had finally found its equilibrium. Its official name was Taman Budi Pusaka. Everyone called it Muara. A Seraya tree belonged in its badge. Then came the dangerous question: what about the other six?

One answer would have been simple: find six equally beautiful words. The first instinct was to do just that. But the Muara conversation had already established a more demanding standard. A convincing name should have a reason for being spoken, not merely a satisfying definition in a dictionary.

That distinction is crucial. Muara is neither the fourth official historical stage nor a bureaucratic rebrand. It is the name people use, carrying the ecological meaning of a river mouth: a meeting place of freshwater and tide, living systems and sediment, mountain and sea.

And so the question became historical. What events, practices and social relationships might persuade generations of people to call the other six schools by names their founders never authorised?

01 · A naming problem becomes a problem of history

Who gets to name a school?

Think of all the parties who might answer differently. A school's masters defend its charter. Merchants remember the quality of its weights. Patients remember a healer's skill. Sailors name the place where they learned to read a swell. Students shorten whatever is inconvenient. Rivals invent a nickname, perhaps to mock an institution, and discover too late that it has stuck.

A school can preserve its official name for centuries while the world quietly gives it another.

Our alternative history sharpens this. No single Western or American higher-education model dominates. Instead, influential Malay–Nusantaran, Chinese, West African, Oceanic, Arabic-speaking and Nordic traditions interact without one serving as the inevitable universal template.

That does not mean names must be purified of every historical contact. It means their routes of travel, prestige and adaptation need to be explained within this world. An international school could have several equally authoritative exonyms. A doctor's word may prevail in one port, a navigator's in another. A student nickname may spread faster than the institution's official insignia.

A name from expertise

What people seek there

A pulse diagnosed, a dispute weighed, an instrument verified. A professional term gradually becomes the place's name.

A name from place

Where everyone goes

A wharf, a river mouth, a hill, a courtyard. A site-name survives even after the institution transforms its surroundings.

A name from social memory

Who first called it that

Patients, apprentices, travellers, critics and outsiders may remember something different from what founders intended.

A name from controversy

What was disputed

The name could be born from a spectacular success, an embarrassing failure, or a fight over who is entitled to knowledge.

The most important restraint: these mechanisms do not have to produce a neat set of six. Some schools may have competing informal names. Some may have none. The name already spoken by everyone may be their official name shortened to a single word.

The Straits · School 02

Lan Fang

Mutual aid, medicine, mechanics and the problem of circulation

Candidate nickname
脈 Mài

Lan Fang begins with a historical advantage: an institution born from association, shared labour and mutual support already has an explanation for why its knowledge would become public power.

The real-world Lanfang Kongsi of West Borneo offers a reference point for Chinese associations that developed more than commercial functions. It is a precedent, not the fictional school's biography. PMU's Lan Fang is already placed around 1786, with its Hakka and southern Chinese lines, medical expertise, material practices and Mohist possibilities. The nickname 脈 Mài came from the school's existing focus on meridians and bodily circulation. Pulse, vessels and routes of transmission suggest a theory that could expand beyond patients: metal has channels of stress; buildings carry forces; a spell passes through points of connection. A short word for pulse could begin among patients and become shorthand for the entire institution.

Then another possibility complicated the choice. 杏林 Xìnglín—the apricot grove—is an old Chinese term for the medical profession, tied to the story of the physician Dong Feng who accepted the planting of apricot trees in lieu of fees. That gives Lan Fang a possible medical nickname even if the whole institution retains another. 杏林 reference ↗

Important qualification. The earlier exploratory sequence 蘭芳公所 → 蘭芳醫工書院 → Lan Fang suggested plausible institutional functions, but its Chinese titles and the precise historical stages have not been authenticated or canonised. The characters 蘭芳 likewise should not be treated as independently confirmed PMU canon merely because they match the historical kongsi's name.
Why it might spread

A famous diagnosis or theory of magical circulation becomes associated with the school. Patients make the word famous.

Why Claude should resist

Mài may over-privilege medicine over Mohist engineering and other Lan Fang disciplines. A generic Chinese character may be less distinctive than the official name.

The Straits · School 03

Kora

Memory, performance, authority and who is allowed to speak

Candidate nickname
Kuma

Kora may have the most politically charged genealogy. Musical performance is only one part of the older social work of specialists who preserve histories, speak genealogies, offer praise, advise powerful people and manage memory.

The Mandinka jeli tradition provides the real-world starting point. Research and collection notes distinguish specialised forms of jeli speech, song and instrumental performance; kuma is the speech through which histories and genealogies may be related. In the proposed PMU extension, a school of performance could expand into a social institution concerned with testimony, attention, persuasion and diplomacy. Jeliya reference ↗

›
Courts of listeningA proposed fictional public forum for performance, deliberation and teaching.

Kuma arose as a possible popular name because it foregrounds not an instrument but the power of utterance. A school might become famous because its performers reconciled competing communities—or because one public performance permanently altered who could speak on behalf of them. The shift would be intellectual: from music as an art to speech and song as technologies of social memory.

There is a chronological complication we must preserve. The kora instrument's documented history is comparatively recent, with origins often situated around the eighteenth century, while Mande speech, genealogical and musical institutions have deeper roots. It could be particularly interesting if the school's ancestral teaching traditions predate the instrument that eventually becomes its official name. Metropolitan Museum: Kora ↗

The difficult question. Do we imagine Kora's founders opening hereditary expertise to outsiders? And if so, who opposed them? A neat story of generous democratisation would leave much of the power of hereditary authority unexplored. Also, kuma is grounded in a specific Mandinka context—not an all-purpose word representing every African oral tradition.
Why it might spread

Witnesses name the institution after its speech that changed a conflict, or after the performers who became trusted tellers of public history.

Why Claude should resist

The nickname may narrow Kora to Mandinka language and jeli authority, even though the school has wider African traditions, percussion, jazz-like invention and collaborative magical music.

The Straits · School 04

Nine Rains

Weather records, waves, sediment, architecture and long routes of causality

Candidate nickname
Alur

Nine Rains was already one of our most visually defined schools: a brick architecture shaped by subtractions, cones, facets, long courses and river approaches. Its water affinities reach from rain and currents to construction and materials. Yet the institution's earliest name—and even why its present name specifies nine rains—remains open.

The proposed chronology began with seasonal observers, developed into communities of navigators and hydrological craftspeople, and eventually became the school known as Nine Rains. Its candidate popular name was Alur.

Here the dictionary genuinely helped. In Indonesian, alur can mean a long groove, a channel, the proper route or course of something, and the sequence of events in a narrative. The bridge between watercourse and storyline is extraordinarily fruitful for a school that studies how an action travels through a system. KBBI: alur ↗

But a second word appeared: Dilep, a term in Marshallese navigational scholarship for a wave path, sometimes glossed as the ocean's backbone. This is an opening into Pacific navigation and wave knowledge, not a candidate to borrow casually. A name arising from the Marshall Islands would need a credible explanation for how a particular tradition became formative in a Straits institution. Research on Marshallese navigation ↗

Muara is where ecological systems meet. Alur is one of the paths along which water, matter, and consequence travel.

A useful intellectual distinction, still to be tested
The serious reservation. If Nine Rains was intended to draw on a broad Oceanic and archipelagic range of traditions, why should an Indonesian word dominate its public reputation? And might Nine Rains itself be the popular name of an institution with a much older, unknown formal one? That inversion could be stronger than Alur.
Why it might spread

Navigators, builders and water engineers make the school's reputation through its methods for tracking connected pathways.

Why Claude should resist

The school's maritime and archipelagic geography might call for a different language—or for several regional nicknames with no single winner.

02 · The second kind of institution

Then the conversation turned from knowledge to authority.

Kora and Nine Rains were imagined through embodied memory and landscape knowledge. TKI and Al Merikani pointed somewhere sharper: the power to decide which measures count and which obligations are legitimate.

This is an especially rich possibility in PMU, where magical burdens, consent, exchange, debt and cooperation already matter. Neither school needs to wield an army to become geopolitically formidable. A trusted standard may reach places soldiers cannot.

The Straits · School 05

TKI

Tembusu Knowledge Institute · Measurement, verification and the authority of standards

Candidate nickname
Tera

Imagine a merchant returning from the waterfront carrying a balance. They do not ask for a university. They ask where to have the instrument checked. Over centuries, the institution's reputation becomes inseparable from the test-mark it places upon tools and claims.

That was the attraction of Tera. In Malay, a tera can be an official mark or impression; its lexical meanings explicitly include marks indicating whether measures or weighing instruments have been tested. It is an unusually tangible word for a school interested in precision, legal procedures, quantitative claims and magical verification. Kamus Dewan: tera ↗

Balai Sukat?Exploratory Malay title for a house of measurement; not adopted.

A standards authorityTesting measures, certifying instruments, adjudicating disputes.
›
TKIThe modern Tembusu Knowledge Institute; its founding precedes Lan Fang.

The word might become famous outside the school. A harbour trusts a scale because it has been marked at TKI. Magical investigators adopt its protocols. Rival scholars argue over whether its measurements of exertion, consent and causality are meaningful. Eventually the very act of verification becomes associated with the colloquial name: *take it to Tera*.

But a standards authority is never merely neutral. Definitions govern who is recognised, which performances are dismissed, what the state can audit, and which kinds of magic remain beyond quantification. We had already imagined TKI's emphasis on measurement meeting resistance elsewhere. A nickname can carry both trust and resentment.

A modern-reader complication. International readers may hear tera- as the SI prefix for a trillion, or as a technology-brand syllable. The Malay testing-mark meaning is a stronger and more PMU-specific explanation, but we should not pretend the other association disappears.
Why it might spread

A certification widely accepted across independent ports becomes synonymous with the institution.

Why Claude should resist

Is TKI primarily a teaching institute, a regulatory body, or a school whose graduates later influence regulation? That difference changes the origin of the name.

Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka: tera

International · School 06

Al Merikani

Astronomy, entrusted records, negotiation and the balance of obligations

Candidate nickname
Mīzān

Al Merikani has some of PMU's most compelling institutional ingredients: astronomical observation, copper instruments, conditional agreements, meticulous record-keeping, and the possibility of a magical ledger through which obligations can be traced across extraordinary distances.

Its proposed popular name was Mīzān — ميزان, an Arabic word for balances or scales, with wider associations of measure, fairness and equilibrium. In our fictional extension it could become a school famous for the weighing of unequal magical burdens and the design of credible agreements between parties who do not trust one another. The conceptual encounter with TKI could be wonderful. TKI might ask whether a measurement is repeatable and independently verified; Al Merikani might ask how burdens, risks and rights ought to be distributed between parties. A measured agreement can still be unjust. A fair agreement can still rely on inadequate measurement.

A rivalry of first principles

Tera measures.
Mīzān weighs.

This is an interpretive contrast, not a fixed jurisdictional division: the schools would undoubtedly contest the territory between technical accuracy and ethical equilibrium. Their rivalry could become one of the engines of PMU's international law of magic.

One discovery interrupts this otherwise attractive plan. Al Merikani resembles colloquial Arabic forms of the word American—notably amrīkānī / merikani in some varieties. There is evidence for that association; we should avoid treating its precise historical etymology as settled without a native-speaker and context check. Colloquial Arabic form ↗

And the alternative-world premise matters. No American dominance does not necessarily mean the American continents or every use of their names vanish; rather, the reason a prominent Arabic-rooted school uses a name sounding like “American” cannot simply be inherited from our present geopolitical vocabulary. It deserves an in-world origin.

High-priority naming audit. Is Al Merikani a founder's name, a place-name, an ethnonym, an inherited trading term, or a later transcription? We may need to revisit the official name, not simply give it a nickname. This was the strongest unexpected challenge in the discussion.
Why it might spread

Other polities trust the school's records and mechanisms for distributing magical obligations, and call it after its balancing practice.

Why Claude should resist

Mīzān is evocative but semantically broad; its religious and legal resonances may not be the right fit for the non-confessional institutional identity we want.

International · School 07

Sund Commons

The youngest school · Working waterfronts, cooperatives and repurposed infrastructure

Candidate nickname
Brygga

The final school resists the neat sequence more than any other. Sund Commons is the youngest, around 1820, yet its campus may inhabit a working waterfront far older than its charter. Buildings can remember what institutions forget.

We have imagined an industrial complex—shipyard, chemical works, metalworking facilities—retrofitted by later generations. The old wharf could remain the social centre as its workshops become laboratories and its infrastructure grows parasitic extensions.

Brygga emerged from Norwegian brygge, meaning a wharf or quay; brygga is an accepted definite form, roughly “the wharf”. And sund itself names a narrow water passage between shores or islands. These are useful linguistic anchors for the setting rather than ready-made historical proof. Norwegian dictionary: brygge ↗

The most believable origin for Brygga might be completely unceremonious: the first apprentices say they are going down to the wharf. Their grandchildren still say so, even after the water's edge has been rebuilt three times and the school has earned international prestige.

Yet the conversation raised a more important restraint. Perhaps it is already known simply as Sund. A school whose official name contains a natural short form may have little reason to generate an entirely different nickname. We should resist the urge to make all six match Muara.

A historical caution. The site's industrial history should follow PMU's own technological divergence. A repurposed shipyard is compatible with craft and infrastructure, but its chronology must not assume our real-world fossil-fuel industrial revolution occurred unchanged.
Why it might spread

The place-name belongs to workers before it belongs to administrators. The campus's old waterfront outlives every institutional change.

Why Claude should resist

Brygga may sound generic to Scandinavian readers. Sund might already be the stronger colloquial name—short, durable, and unforced.

03 · What changes when there is no dominant centre?

A multipolar world invents its own shorthand.

One of the most tempting mistakes would be to treat all seven schools like modern anglophone universities: one official name, one neat nickname, one universally agreed public identity. PMU offers a different possibility.

Imagine that the same institution is known by different words along different trade routes. Patients use its healing name; jurists use its verification mark; navigators use a term learned from the ocean; students use something absurdly short. None of these names necessarily passes through English before becoming internationally influential.

It would be equally wrong to assume a peaceful exchange of names. Cultural multipolarity produces competition. The right to name a method can be a claim to have invented it. The right to certify an instrument can become a means of regulating trade. A successful institution may try to universalise its vocabulary, while other powerful centres refuse, translate or parody it.

A proposition for PMU

Names travel the same routes as knowledge.

They travel with patients, students, treaties, instruments, riverboats, manuscripts, songs, debts and rumours. A school is named by its founders; it is renamed by the relationships it creates.

That, rather than six matching nicknames, may be the real discovery worth carrying into the next debate.

04 · The handoff

Six more debates. Maybe fewer names.

At the end of the conversation, the initial shortlist contained six attractive words. But the deeper proposal was to investigate the incidents that would make them believable. Did a Lan Fang physician save a community through an unfamiliar theory? Did Kora's artists change the rules of public testimony? Did Nine Rains prevent a flood—or fail to prevent one, leaving a more enduring lesson? Was TKI's certification accepted where treaties were rejected? Did a Merikani ledger transform the exchange of magical burdens? Did Sund's wharf simply outlast the official signage?

Each possibility is deliberately unfinished. The next phase should bring cultural specificity, chronology, political conflict, everyday speech and modern reader reception into the same debate.

For Claude · Discussion brief

Interrogate the names, rather than ratifying them.

The following questions are prompts for comparative worldbuilding, not canon decisions.

  1. Reconstruct the naming history of each school from its own historical institutions, languages, social practices and surrounding polities—without imposing one university template.
  2. Test what native or fluent speakers hear in Mài, Kuma, Alur, Tera, Mīzān and Brygga. Identify connotations that sound awkward, overly generic, anachronistic or inadvertently comic.
  3. Explain who popularised each unofficial name. Students, labourers, patients, rivals, diplomats and merchants would not choose the same words.
  4. Challenge the language geography. Do Nine Rains' Oceanic influences warrant a different linguistic ancestry? How broad is Kora's Mandinka inheritance? Which languages have prestige in different parts of this world?
  5. Audit Al Merikani seriously. Its resemblance to Arabic expressions for “American” needs an internally convincing origin or a revision, given PMU's alternate geopolitical history.
  6. Refuse forced symmetry. Some schools may have two living nicknames, a nickname shared with a town, or none. A candidate should survive because the history makes it irresistible.
  7. Keep the settled canon untouched: Taman Budi Pusaka (TBP) is official, Muara is unofficial, Seraya is the badge motif. Lan Fang remains Lan Fang unless explicitly revised. Sund dates to about 1820; TKI is earlier than Lan Fang; Lan Fang dates to about 1786.

The strangest part of the evening was perhaps this: we began by asking what six schools should be called. We ended by asking what they would have done to be called those things by others.

The founders may own the charter.
The world owns the nickname.

For now, there is only one fully settled example. On paper, Taman Budi Pusaka. In the everyday speech of the Straits, Muara. The other six are still waiting to earn their names.
