II
Thread II

What the naming shows

Six patterns, read from who named whom.

1 of 7

Only the ground named itself first

Balai Pusaka had its own name before the registrar gave it another. Every house that arrived by sea was named by someone else first: a clerk, a street, a cloth, a neighbour’s ear, or its own founders’ pun.

4 of 7

The founding stream takes the house back

Four chosen names came through the born-in door. The streams that carried each house’s belief across the water used the Second Naming to say so.

+5

Lan Fang’s name was decided by those who married in

日新 lost in 1904 and passed at the Second Naming. Every vote in the margin came from Dayak and Peranakan shareholders.

0

Toa Keng chose its accident

The house with no neighbours kept the only name a street gave it, a drain, in Hokkien.

2 of 2

The outer houses are named in the language of their workers

Toa Keng is Hokkien and Sund is now Malay. Both stand outside the centre, both were filled mostly through work, and both names come from people who joined through work.

7 of 7

The city keeps every given name

The chosen names live on seals, in halls and in the mouths of members. The city goes on saying Temenggong, Merika, Kora and Rumah Hujan, as it always has.

The house has called itself this from the beginning and has never once used the gazette name in its own documents. At the Second Naming it did not choose a new name. It asked the registry to correct the old one, and the request was the act.

Born in
The founding body, the ground every road lands on

This is the only house whose own name came before the accident. It was here before the ships, so it named itself before anyone could name it. Its streams say the same: 62 per cent born in, the highest root in the city. Others married into it far more than it married out, so its name absorbed them as well.

Two centuries of letters are addressed to an institution that, internally, does not exist. The Second Naming is the first time the registry has written back.

Second Naming, by ballot

The 1904 reformers proposed 日新, from the bathing-bowl inscription in the Daxue, and lost sixty-one to forty-four. At the Second Naming the shareholders voted again, and this time it passed by five. Every vote in the margin was cast by a shareholder who had married in.

Married in
Dayak and Peranakan shareholders, the married-in quarter of the house

Lan Fang’s second stream is Dayak and Peranakan, 18 per cent, joined by marriage in Borneo before the Straits. Those families know what the kongsi actually was: a compact between unlike parties. They voted for renewal because they were the renewal. The name the reformers lost with in 1904 was won, a century later, by the people the reformers had never counted.

日新 has hung over the assembly hall for a hundred and forty years as a defeated name. The night it passes, the masthead becomes a title, and the reformers’ grandchildren find out who carried it.

Second Naming, by covenant

One house of trust, in the two languages of its founding partnership. Arabic amānah is trust held in custody; Tamil nambikkai is trust as faith. The seal carries both, in Arabic script, as Tamil Muslims have long written Tamil in Arwi, so a single inscription can be read twice.

Born in
Both founding halves, South Asian 32 and Arab and Persian 30

The stream census showed the house South Asian first, by two points. A purely Arabic name would have named its smaller half. The chosen name keeps the meanings close and the words apart, so each half hears its own word for trust, and anyone who holds both languages hears the difference between custody and faith.

Arguing over which word comes first on the seal takes longer than the covenant itself. They settle it the Al-Merikani way, by writing a contract about it.

The Institute put the family name in front of the instrument: Sané, the lineage its founding jeli carried and sang. An instrument should never again be named without the people who play it. A name said correctly binds the living to the dead.

Born in

Sané-Kora is the one house whose first name was given by proximity, before it had any. The neighbours named it by listening, and by the Basin years their children made up 15 per cent of the house. The chosen name answers the city in the house’s own doctrine: say the name properly, and say whose it is.

The Institute keeps one more name that is never written down: the list of every lineage it has absorbed, sung at each opening. The list grows longer every year, and the song with it.

Second Naming, by refusal

Offered the chance, Toa Keng filed its accident a second time. The registrar’s note reads: name retained by election of the institution. It is the only house whose chosen name and given name are the same.

Next door
The street again, by its own decision

Not one person in Toa Keng came in from next door, yet the street gave it the only name it has. The name is Hokkien, the language of its largest examination-entry stream, Chinese 18. Toa Keng is named in the language of the people who work for it. Choosing the drain is its one act of proximity: it keeps the neighbours’ word while still refusing the neighbours.

A drain is downstream of everything. Toa Keng’s refusal to rename is read across the city as either humility or contempt, and the novel never settles which.

Every anniversary still requires a conversion argument between two calendars, and the house now holds it under a name that means the argument is a gift.

The ting, where every worker who has rowed holds one vote, named the house in Malay, the language of the Orang Laut and Bugis seafarers who taught it the shoals. The commons belong to those who row them, so the house is named for the oar.

Through work

Sund has married no one yet, and its name has gone inward ahead of its people. It is the second house named in the language of those who work for it, and the second house outside the centre. The pattern matches Toa Keng’s, but Sund took the language willingly. The Sámi singers say its truest name is a joik, which cannot be written down.

A house that has never had a wedding names itself in its neighbours’ language. Its first marriage is now a question of when, and the name is the reason.

IV
Thread IV

The Stream Census

The cause of the Second Naming.

In the Basin years the Concordat retired the charter’s census of four boxes and counted the city by streams: heritage, road and door. Each house received its own numbers and was given one year to answer them with a name.

The given names stayed on the envelopes. The Concordat asked only that each house choose, deliberately and in its own way, the name it would put on its seal. Six chose new names or reclaimed old ones. One chose its accident again.

Seven Houses, Weighed is the census each house received. This volume records how they replied.

V
Thread V

How a house chooses

Four rules, each drawn from the streams.

Every house chose through its own procedure: Lan Fang by shareholders’ ballot, Al-Merikani by covenant, Sund by its ting, Balai Pusaka by petition, Sané-Kora by restoring a lineage, Nine Rain by return, and Toa Keng by refusal.

A given name is an accident
It came from outside: a clerk, a street, a cloth, a date, a neighbour listening through a wall. It records how the city first met the house, and the city keeps it.
A chosen name is a claim
It states what the house believes it is, so it is allowed to state a philosophy. The city refuses to use it, which is how the city has always treated grand names.
The language is a stream
Each chosen name is in the language of a stream that is actually inside the house. The census makes that checkable: no house could choose a language its members do not carry.
The door shows who decided
Born-in streams reclaim. Married-in streams tip votes. Working streams name houses that have no other door open. The street names houses that have no neighbours.

IX
Thread IX

The history beneath

The fiction walks on these.

  • Merikani, also marikani or kain merikani, was the name across the western Indian Ocean for unbleached American cotton sheeting, traded in great volume through Zanzibar and the Swahili coast in the nineteenth century.
  • Temenggong was a senior Malay court title. Temenggong Abdul Rahman was a party to the 1819 agreements that founded British Singapore.
  • The Lanfang kongsi governed in west Borneo from 1777 to 1884, and one account derives 兰芳 from the names of two founders, though the reading is disputed. Its founding figure was 罗芳伯.
  • 苟日新，日日新，又日新, if you can renew yourself for one day, renew yourself daily, and renew again, is the bathing-bowl inscription of King Tang quoted in the Daxue.
  • Arwi is Tamil written in Arabic script, used for centuries by Tamil Muslim communities in South India and Sri Lanka. Amānah in Arabic is trust held in custody; nambikkai in Tamil is trust and faith.
  • In the Aztec 260-day calendar, each day joins a number from one to thirteen with one of twenty signs, and people were often named for their birth day. Rain is Quiahuitl, and nine is chicnahui.
  • Ayni is the Andean principle of reciprocal labour and exchange; wasi is Quechua for house. Utang na loob, a debt held within, names reciprocity in Filipino social life, and 人情 carries a related sense in Chinese.
  • Kaabu, a Mande kingdom of the Senegambian region, was ruled by the Sané and Mané lineages. Jeli, also called griots, keep the genealogies of the families they serve.
  • Sund means a strait or sound in the Scandinavian languages, as in Øresund. Dayung is Malay for oar or paddle, and the Orang Laut were the sea peoples of the Straits.
  • The joik is a Sámi vocal tradition in which a song is understood to evoke a person, animal or place directly, not to describe it.
  • 大坑 in Hokkien is a big ditch or drain, a common source of place names in Chinese-speaking Southeast Asian towns.
