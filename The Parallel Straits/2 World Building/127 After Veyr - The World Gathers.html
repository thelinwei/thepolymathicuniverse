Part One · 01–03

The gathering 四方来聚

An attack on people, a rupture in trust, and the generosity nobody could summon.

01
01 · Veyr

An attack on people. A rupture in trust.

Teachers and students of the Straits, invited abroad to share constructive magic, were killed at Veyr. Coercion failed to extract further knowledge; instructional books were stolen. The event wounds families and exposes the vulnerability of the international exchange established at the Congress.

The human response

Grief before strategy

Families need news; survivors need care; people grieve individuals, not symbols. No grand event can substitute for the missing persons.

The institutional response

The Straits prepares

Senior practitioners begin developing defensive and destructive magic deliberately absent from the exported constructive curriculum. Security, deterrence and protection become urgent.

The relational response

Beyond the Straits

Across the world, former students, colleagues, hosts, rivals and strangers decide—freely—that Veyr concerns them too.

The first revelation is not the size of the Straits’ arsenal. It is the number of people who come.

ChronologyThis sits in the 1930s, inside the twenty-five years between the Congress of 1921 and the Covenant of 1946: after the Veyr Exchange Murders, the Empty Desks, and before the Veyr Retaliation. The Congress remains the first demonstration, not an already networked planetary age. The great musical relay described below is a remarkable new feat, not an everyday infrastructure quietly assumed to exist.

02
02 · The generosity nobody could summon

They did not all come for the same reason.

An emergency mobilisation gradually becomes something that no one organised or predicted. Every vessel offers different evidence of what decades of exchange have made possible.

Practical comfort

Food. Seeds. Shelter.

Foreign ports send grains from different harvests, preserved foods, kitchen vessels, medicinal plants, soft cloth, timber, and craftspeople. Healers bring remedies developed for climates unfamiliar to the Straits. Cooks prepare meals that remind grieving visitors of home.

These gifts are ordinary, intimate, immediately useful. A stranger may not know what to say but knows how to feed somebody.

Human solace

The arrival of the unexpected

A once-hosted student. An estranged colleague. A legendary singer whose fame had seemed remote from the crisis. A small village delegation carrying something they made themselves. Political adversaries who cannot share a platform but can share work.

Not every visitor knows Veyr’s history. Not everyone understands its politics. Their contributions are real anyway.

Institutional depth

Relationships become routes

Personal hospitality gives way to routes of transport, provision, translation and relief. Schools open unused rooms; merchant families arrange passage; workshops donate materials; local communities take responsibility for incoming strangers.

The paradox

Help without unanimity

Some guests favour retaliation. Others insist that their gifts cannot serve military goals. Others are indifferent to the larger argument and come simply because someone they loved was killed.

The Straits cannot command the world’s gratitude or solidarity. It can only receive it.

There is no single united worldview behind the gathering. That is precisely why the gathering matters.
03
03 · Two answers to the same event

Preparing to fight. Choosing to be together.

The Manhattan moment

The capability to defend

Masters, engineers, healers and tactical scholars extend constructive knowledge into protection, counterforce and destructive magic. They may seek deterrence, not necessarily a spectacular attack. Their work creates both security and new questions about the use of power.

Guiding impulse: “Never again should we be so unprepared.”

The We Are the World moment

The capability to connect

Musicians and organisers attempt an almost absurd proposition: not merely an assembly in the Straits, but a living world-wide performance with autonomous singers in distant ports, heard and joined in the very moment of its making.

The proposal comes from Harry Belafonte. Quincy Jones takes the score.

Guiding impulse: “Even separated, we can answer one another.”

The two projects have overlapping discoveries and participants, but are not the same undertaking. A participant in the world-song has not thereby agreed to any use of its principles in war.

Part Two · 04–07

The world-cast 四海同声

One night. The world’s voices. Almost no time.

04
04 · A proposition that should have failed

One night. The world’s voices. Almost no time.

The Straits cannot physically assemble every great performer. Some could never travel in time. The extraordinary alternative is a simultaneous distributed casting—part broadcast, part live exchange, part improvised performance, with every port contributing rather than merely listening.

An invitation outruns the plan

Letters, vessels and entrusted routes carry the call. The organisers discover that many legendary performers are already travelling, already offering help, or impossible to reach in time.

A score with openings

A central framework defines the shared invocation, entry cues, safety boundaries and the closing phrase. It intentionally does not prescribe every note or every response.

A conductor and a soloist in each port

The local conductor/arranger interprets the framework and coordinates singers. The legendary soloist gives that port a distinctive human voice and handles unplanned musical turns.

There is no second night

Unreliable relays, divergent timekeeping, weather, politics, rivalries and immense burden mean that a complete rehearsal is impossible. The whole is heard only in the act of creation.

Crucial distinctionA broadcast alone would carry one performance outward. This working is two-way: ports receive, reply, improvise and affect the continuing spell. It resembles a live stream with many simultaneous contributors, but is carried by PMU magic rather than an imported telegraph, radio or digital network.

05
05 · A network of local stages

Every port has its own voice.

Six stations carry the frame, each with its own role in the working. The Straits is the coordinating origin, not the only source of power. Many more ports answered; their performers follow the six.

The core architecturecentral invitation → locally interpreted score → soloist’s call → conductor’s ensemble response → relay to other ports → globally evolving performance. Delays and misunderstandings are not necessarily fatal: each conductor is trusted to adapt without abandoning the shared pattern.

06 · Anatomy of the first world-cast

Coordination is itself a new magic.

Its inventors do not first design a complete planetary medium. They improvise a temporary mesh of relays, listening stations, entrusted correspondences, locally prepared instruments, timing marks and musical cues. The emergent achievement is that remote contributions remain part of one working despite imperfect synchrony.

Broadcast

The world hears

A common melodic signal reaches distant stations, where people who cannot see the Straits can nevertheless recognise when the working has begun.

Live exchange

The world replies

Each local soloist answers in a distinct musical language. The returns affect pacing, colour and magical form at other locations.

Improvised structure

The world changes it

Arrangers protect a few shared anchors, while improvisers make decisions in the moment. The score is a framework for cooperation, not a script of uniformity.

Interactive · explore the casting’s fragility

How does the song survive an imperfect world?

Select the ports that manage to join. This illustrates narrative consequences, not an RPG power calculation or an actual spell simulator.

Press Hear it and each station sounds as a pure tone over the harbour’s hum, tuned by 三分损益, the old Chinese way of finding notes by taking two-thirds and four-thirds of a pipe. Wuxi sounds 变徵, the note Jing Ke’s friends sang at the Yi River, which sounds wrong until you miss it.

The largest spell ever cast is not a feat of one great master. It is a feat of coordination among thousands of people who were never wholly under anyone’s command.

06 · The song itself

What the world sang

The song had no single composer and no single language. It was made the way a port is made, from whatever arrives: a harbour’s hum, a pure tone drawn out of it, passages in every first language, drums where drums belong, and verses in the tongues of the Straits. It moved through four turns, the way an old Chinese verse moves: it rises, it carries, it turns, it comes home.

起It rises
The hum, and the great sound that is barely heard

It began with the harbour humming. Tens of thousands of people on the quays settled on one note together, without anyone choosing it, and Sané-Kora drew a pure tone out of that hum and sent it out. A pure tone belongs to no instrument and no people, so every shore heard it the same way. 大音希声, the Daodejing says: the great sound is barely heard.

No beat yet. Only breath: a shakuhachi from Osaka, a Tuvan singer whistling a tone out of her own throat, and in a thousand houses people humming the way they hum to a child.

Rasa · karuṇā, tenderness for the lost

承It carries
Every port in its first language

Each port sang its passage in its own tongue, untranslated, over the pure tone, with its own instruments around it. Umm Kulthum turned a line in Arabic until Cairo was in tarab, singer and listeners carried away together. A listener in Wuxi understood none of the words and wept anyway.

Drums sounded only where a port’s music lives in drums, and only at home. The shared parts stayed free of any beat, so no port waited for another’s clock. Between passages came a silence the length of a breath, what Japan calls 間, ma: the space that lets the last voice be heard.

Rasa · karuṇā, deepening

转It turns
The drum goes round the world

Once, and only once, the beat left home. In the Straits a West African talking drum spoke the names of the dead in the tones of speech, and the Malay kompang answered. Then the pulse travelled: Zanzibar’s ngoma, the kudüm of Konya, Delhi’s tabla, the batá of Havana, Honolulu’s gourd ipu, each port giving one phrase and passing it on, a single heartbeat going round the planet.

Because it travelled rather than landed everywhere at once, no shared clock was needed. People who had been weeping found themselves smiling. That was the turn.

Rasa · adbhuta, wonder

合It comes home
Verses of the Straits, and the lingering tone

The song came home to the Straits’ own tongues. Malay pantun were traded back and forth in the manner of dondang sayang, the Melaka duet in which two singers answer one another verse for verse. A Hokkien lullaby was hummed rather than sung. A Chinese quatrain was chanted over the guqin. Then everything fell back into the hum, the hum into the pure tone, and in Solo the great gong, the gong ageng, sounded once to close the cycle.

The tone did not stop when the singing did. The Liezi tells of the singer 韩娥, whose voice lingered around the rafters for three days after she left. 余音绕梁. In the Straits it lingered for three days too.

Rasa · śānta, peace

From the last turnTwo verses of the Straits

Malay · pantun, as dondang sayang

Dari seberang datang berlayar,
Singgah semalam di hujung tanjung;
Suara yang jauh kami dengar,
Hati yang luka kami dukung.

From across the water they came sailing, / stopping one night at the tip of the cape; / the far-off voices, we hear them, / the wounded heart, we hold it close.

The first couplet paints a picture; the second says what the heart means. That is how a pantun works.

Chinese · 五言 quatrain, over the guqin

潮自四方来，
声从万里回。
不知谁家曲，
一夜共低徊。

The tide comes in from the four quarters; / voices come back from ten thousand li. / No one knows whose song it is; / one night, all of us lingering in it.

The third line answers 李白’s 谁家玉笛暗飞声: whose jade flute sends its sound into the dark?

07 · One take

It almost did not happen.

By nightfall, they still did not know whether the farthest ports had received the final phrase. One vessel had arrived too late. A conductor had fallen ill. Two celebrated soloists had refused the same arrangement, and neither had entirely relented.

In the Straits, the chief arranger, Quincy Jones, stood before a gathering larger than anyone had predicted. Beyond the hall were other stages, other shores, other performers waiting for a sign that might never reach them.

The first signal came back wrong.

For several breaths, there was only the local music. Someone said the distant station had failed. A second return arrived at the wrong moment, carrying an unfamiliar rhythm. It was supposed to be an error. The nearest soloist answered it.

One conductor let the answer stand. Another port took it as its cue. Somewhere else, a singer whose planned entrance had been lost found a space nobody had written into the score.

Nothing was happening precisely as arranged. And, against every expectation, the working was still holding.

The voices multiplied. A phrase left one shore, returned changed from another, and came back altered once more. Listeners who had prepared only to witness found themselves joining. In distant houses, people who knew none of the performers began to sing.

The arrangers stopped trying to recover the original plan. They kept the frame alive and made room for what the world was giving them.

When the closing phrase arrived, it was not the one they had composed.

It held anyway.

A deliberately brief fictional moment. The importance is not a perfect rehearsal or a single hero, but that a fragile, unreproducible attempt becomes a collective achievement.

08 · What no one intended

The song does more than they asked.

Its immediate purpose may have been mourning, comfort, protection and solidarity. Yet the largest working in history touches places the arrangers did not anticipate. Most of its surprises are beneficial; a few are awkward, bewildering or inconvenient.

The greatest consequence may not be its power. It may be the discovery that the world can act together without waiting to become one people.

09 · A song that learns to heal

Relief magic. It works.

Long after the gathering, ordinary practitioners retain fragments of the casting: a refrain, a response, a rhythm for assembling help. In one place it comforts the bereaved. Somewhere else it steadies evacuation. In another, it helps a community share the burden of shelter or deliver care.

What survives

A songbook that keeps changing

The musical lineage travels by singing, listening, teaching, remembering and changing. Verses are translated. Durations shift. Communities replace local references and develop new responses. Some performers recognise the original; others have never heard the name Veyr.

What makes it magic

A form people can use

Those who understand and perform it can create real local magical effects—comfort, coordination, shelter, warning or recovery—within the established PMU mechanics of burden and resistance. It need not produce the same outcome in every setting.

They created one impossible song. The world inherited a thousand useful ones.

10 · The long inheritance

What the founders could not control.

Music

New lineages

Conductors become respected not only for interpretation but for their ability to combine unfamiliar traditions without flattening them. Formerly peripheral forms become central to entirely new ensembles.

Relations

Ports that remember

Foreign ports continue visiting, corresponding and hosting one another. Their shared night becomes a relationship, not simply a commemorative date.

Infrastructure

New communications

Innovations in temporary magical relay encourage later networks for urgent warnings, public gatherings, ceremonies and long-distance collaboration. None of this required a pre-existing planetary system.

Care

A portable civic practice

Communities discover that small, locally meaningful variations of the song can summon practical aid, coordinate relief teams and make burden-sharing intelligible.

War

An uneasy intersection

Defensive strategists learn from the coordination breakthrough. Some veterans welcome the applications; some singers reject them. Their disagreement remains part of the event’s history, not a reason to erase its achievement.

Memory

Many different histories

Anniversaries preserve differing interpretations. Descendants may no longer know the founders, the politics or the original words. A song can outlive its reasons for existing.
