Seven Wardrobesof the Straits

Each school dresses in three or four borrowings that never met in history. They are chosen for what they do to a body, then set to work arguing for the school’s idea of knowledge. One gesture survives every garment in the family, from the head’s ceremonial coat to a junior’s sports kit.

Seven heads, read left to right. The hat is the fastest way to tell the schools apart across a crowded quay, so each one carries the school’s argument in miniature.

The brief, as I read it

Familiar forms, unfamiliar company. A samurai shoulder on an Edwardian surveyor. A Byzantine emperor’s jewelled band over a bullfighter’s jacket. A Swedish boat blanket worn as a cape at the most formal gathering of the year. Each pairing should feel slightly wrong for a second, then inevitable once you know what the school believes.

The test for every borrowing is a job. It has to make the body do something the school cares about: choose a side, hold something back, depend on someone else, carry its own history, record weather, keep silent, belong to everyone.

How each school is built

  1. The recipe — the borrowings, with where they come from.
  2. The gesture — the one move that survives every garment, at every scale.
  3. The wardrobe — head of school, assembly, everyday, sport, and how a junior differs from a senior. Each school solves seniority its own way.
  4. A custom — one ritual the clothes make possible, for the novel to use.

Earlier decisions carry forward: TKI’s silt sash, Merican’s inward lining, Temenggong’s kain, the cohort date, the Nine Rains record band, Kora’s silence and free colour, Sund’s wearer tab. Your selected references are assigned to schools at the end.

i

Toa Keng Institution

Parallax — every frame shows something and hides something.

  • Surveyor’s frock coat, calf-length, tropical worstedLondon, 1900s
  • Kamishimo shoulder wing, stiffenedEdo Japan
  • Fencing jacket’s off-centre closureEuropean salle, 19th c.
  • White canvas spats over black oxfordsEdwardian street
  • A boater with half its brim cut awayThames regatta, halved

Choose your frame every morning, in public. Every TKI garment has two closing lines four centimetres apart. Close on the left and the whole front falls one way; close on the right and the silhouette shifts. The disputed tie hangs from the side you chose, so a hall of students reads like a room of positions.

Head of school
Charcoal frock coat to the calf. One stiffened kamishimo wing rises from the closing shoulder like a hand raised in debate — your articulated charcoal reference, its plates reread as the wing. Silt sash across the body, a brass surveyor’s loupe on a chain at the waist. White spats. On Founding Day the head closes on the opposite side from last year.
Assembly
Hip-length charcoal jacket with the double placket and a soft cloth wing stitched flat at the shoulder seam. Tie on the closing side. This is where your pale diagonal coat lands: its long closure line is the TKI placket already.
Everyday
White short-sleeved shirt with the offset placket; tailored shorts or trousers; a narrow silt strip at the waist. In the heat the shirt goes and an open waistcoat stays, its two edges stitched with the two placket lines.
Sport
Épée. The fencing jacket already closes off-centre, which makes TKI’s sports kit the one historically correct thing it wears, and the school is insufferable about it. Sleeveless jacket, close breeches, the red-and-black diagonal suit as the competition cut.
Junior · senior
Shorts until the third year, trousers after. TKI is the only school that kept the old colonial rule, and it debates abolishing it every year without result. The debate is the point.
Head and feet
The half-boater: straw, its brim removed over one eye, the measuring eye. Canvas spats on formal days; plain oxfords otherwise.

Custom — At the start of a formal disputation the chair calls “frame”. Every student re-buttons to the side they will argue. Changing sides mid-debate means re-buttoning in front of everyone, slowly, and it is considered the most honourable thing a TKI student can do.

ii

Al-Merican College

Amanah — what is entrusted is carried inside.

  • The bisht, floor-length, edging moved insideGulf and Hadhramaut
  • Haura — luxury hidden in a plain kimono’s liningEdo, under sumptuary law
  • Chalk stripeCity of London banking
  • Brass trade weights as togglesThe dhow merchant’s scale
  • Babouche worn with the heel folded downMaghreb

A promise you can see, if you know where to look. One front edge of every Merican garment folds back a hand’s width to show the lining. Flat, the cloth says we are talking. Turned, it says I am bound by what I have said. Merchants on the quay read each other’s edges before they read each other’s faces.

Head of school
Your white spiral reference becomes Merican’s: tonal ivory, the spiral’s fold reread as the turned edge sweeping into the sleeve, the astrolabe kept at the hip. Over it at public signings, a floor-length indigo bisht in faint chalk stripe, its gold edging sewn to the inside. From across a room it is almost severe. During the signing the head turns both edges, the whole lining appears for the length of a signature, then closes.
Assembly
Knee-length collarless indigo coat, one brass weight at the throat, the turned edge pressed flat. The inner facing is plain cloth until it is given.
Everyday
Ivory tunic to mid-thigh over indigo trousers, sleeves rolled, the weight toggle at the collar. In the heat: bare torso, a single shoulder strap, a small ledger pouch at the hip, the heirloom strip facing inward at the waistband.
Sport
Rowing. Rolled breeches, bare torso. The oar blades are painted with the college’s lining pattern — the only place it is ever shown openly, and only while moving away from shore.
Junior · senior
Invisible to outsiders. At a student’s first real contract — an apprenticeship, a first trade — family or sponsor sews a strip of heirloom cloth into the facing. Juniors have empty linings. You cannot tell who is senior unless they choose to turn an edge.
Head and feet
The counting cap: low, brimless, felt, with a banker’s grosgrain band; the student’s seal rides in an inner pocket. Babouche with crushed heels, so you can step out of them at the threshold of a room where something will be agreed.

Custom — Breaking a promise is never announced. The person simply stops turning their edge in your presence. Half the college’s gossip is about who has stopped turning for whom.

Rendering prompt

Full-body fashion concept, adult head of college, studio ground. Floor-length deep indigo cloak cut like an Arabian bisht, with a faint chalk stripe; the right front edge folded back a hand’s width to reveal a dense gold-and-indigo brocade lining; underneath, a tonal ivory long coat whose diagonal fold sweeps into one sleeve; small brass astrolabe at the hip; a single hexagonal brass trade weight used as a toggle at the throat; low brimless felt cap with a grosgrain band; leather babouche slippers with the heels folded flat. Calm, expensive, restrained. Front and three-quarter view.

iii

Sri Temenggong Institution

Communion — you are dressed by the people you belong to.

  • Kain samping with tumpal borderMalay court
  • The tanjak and its named foldsMalay court
  • The loros — a jewelled band wound round the bodyByzantine emperor
  • Chaquetilla — short, stiff, shoulder-built jacketSpanish bullring
  • Bare feet on a temple floor; terompah clogs outsideArchipelago

No one dresses alone. The samping’s knot sits at the small of the back, beyond the wearer’s own reach. Every Temenggong morning begins in pairs, and who ties whose knot is the first social fact of the day.

Head of school
An emerald songket band wound like a loros: over one shoulder, across the chest, round the back and down to the hem, its tumpal border at the end. Beneath it a short stiff jacket cut like a torero’s, ending at the lowest rib, gold only on the shoulders. Ankle-length kain. The tanjak in the highest fold.
Assembly
Short black chaquetilla over a white baju, knee-length samping knotted by a partner. Shoulder cloth folded over the forearm.
Everyday
Bare chest or a sleeveless baju; the samping over divided shorts; the shoulder cloth on one shoulder or tucked into the wrap. The knot rule holds even here.
Sport
Silat. The samping is rolled high into a compact hip wrap — your blue wrap reference with its taped wrists and shins — and in competition the knot is tied by the opponent. Untying it is a scoring move.
Junior · senior
The tanjak fold. Court folds already carry names — one real tanjak is called Dendam Tak Sudah, grudge unfinished. Temenggong keeps the naming habit and names its folds after obligations. A junior learns one fold; each year adds one. A senior’s head can be read by anyone who knows the sequence.
Feet
Wooden terompah outside, loud on stone and meant to be heard arriving. Barefoot inside the Balai Lama.

Custom — At the Balai Lama the head of school enters last, carrying the shoes of the newest student. Being chosen to have your shoes carried is terrifying, and every first-year secretly wants it.

Rendering prompt

Full-body fashion concept, adult head of school, studio ground. Very short stiff black jacket in the cut of a Spanish torero’s chaquetilla, gold embroidery only on the shoulders, ending at the lowest rib; an emerald songket band wound around the body like a Byzantine loros, over one shoulder, across the chest and falling to the hem, ending in a gold triangular tumpal border; ankle-length kain; a tall folded tanjak headcloth with one sharp peak; barefoot on a polished timber floor, wooden clogs set beside. Regal, architectural, warm. Front and back views showing the waist knot at the small of the back.

iv

Lan Fong 兰芳

Recomposition — the inheritance is up for election. (Lam Keng 南景 in the dress notebooks.)

  • Liangmao 凉帽 — flat ring hat, open crown, cloth fringeHakka women’s fieldwear
  • Crossed collar, 交领Ming scholar’s robe
  • Oilcloth miner’s apronBorneo gold kongsi
  • Deep turned-back naval cuffsVOC officer — the old enemy’s, worn as a joke
  • A slot pocket for ballotsEvery kongsi meeting

A parliament of cuts. Every Lan Fong garment is built from panels that button or tape together. Every twenty years an elected student committee designs one new panel; the rest stay. The uniform is never designed whole, by anyone.

Head of school
The Archive Coat: long, crossed at the collar, assembled from one panel of each past uniform, every seam bound in open red tape. Your silver crossing reference sits underneath as the fitted core — the wuxia cross becomes the 交领 that holds the archive together. Huge VOC cuffs. For the oath, the miner’s oilcloth apron goes on over everything. The ring hat fringed in all ten cohort colours.
Assembly
A short crossed-collar coat. The current cohort’s panel sits over the heart; the date on the cuff, as before. On the left, a slot pocket.
Everyday
A single panel worn as a vest over a bare torso — literally one piece of the uniform — with wide trousers cut for a low stance, or shorts.
Sport
Lion dance, in its competition form. Bare chest, wide-legged shorts, a sash in kongsi colours knotted at the hip. The lion head itself is rebuilt by each cohort from the last one’s frame.
Junior · senior
Juniors wear only the current panel. Seniors earn the right to add one inherited panel of their choosing — they pick an ancestor, and have to defend the choice to the committee.
Head and feet
The ring hat: open at the crown so the head is never fully covered by the institution. Cloth shoes on a thick rubber miner’s sole.

Custom — The ballot slot is for dissent. After any vote, members of the losing side fold their position onto a slip and carry it in the slot until the next vote. You can always see who lost; you can never read why without asking.

Rendering prompt

Full-body fashion concept, adult head of school, studio ground. Long Chinese crossed-collar coat built from ten visibly different fabric panels from different eras — silk, canvas, wool, printed cotton — every seam bound in bright red tape; very deep turned-back cuffs in the style of an 18th-century Dutch naval coat; a fitted silver crossed-collar layer underneath; black oilcloth miner’s apron over the front; wide flat Hakka ring hat with an open crown and a fringe of coloured cloth strips; cloth shoes with thick rubber soles. Intellectual, patched, proud. Front and back views.

v

House of Nine Rains

Reciprocity — take only what you can account for.

  • Mino — the straw raincoat of tiered strandsRural Japan
  • Gut-skin parka — translucent, seams that swell shutAleut and Iñupiat
  • Kusari-doi rain chainJapanese eaves
  • Tall wooden pattens for flooded groundFrom chopine to geta
  • Sashiko and contrasting repairJapanese and every farm household

Never larger than one year. Every garment carries strips — a woven band, a tier of fringe, a hat’s edge — and each strip is a season. When a new one is woven, the oldest is cut away and returned to the farm or village that grew the fibre. The uniform cannot accumulate.

Head of school
Undyed heavy cotton robe, dense with repairs. Over it a mantle of oiled silk, thin enough that rain can be watched running down it — gut-skin logic in a tropical cloth. Over the shoulders, a mino of nine tiers, each woven by a different partner village. The eaves hat with its rain chain.
Assembly
Knee-length undyed coat with the record band at the shoulder, repairs in contrasting thread. A coat with no repairs is considered slightly suspicious.
Everyday
Bare chest, the record band worn alone across one shoulder, undyed knee wrap or work shorts. Your green-and-white diagonal wrap lands here as the everyday lower garment.
Sport
Free-diving. Nothing that records is allowed in the water; the band stays on shore. Water is the one place a Nine Rains student is unaccounted for.
Junior · senior
Juniors may repair only their own clothes. Seniors repair other people’s — reciprocity made literal — and a senior’s stitches show up all over the house.
Head and feet
The eaves hat: a gutter rim that carries rain round to a chain at the back. Tall two-toothed wooden pattens for flooded paths; barefoot in water.

Custom — Cutting away the oldest strip is done by the person who wove it, if they are still alive. Some strips travel a long way to be cut.

Rendering prompt

Full-body fashion concept, adult head of house, studio ground. Undyed heavy cotton robe covered in visible contrasting sashiko repairs; over it a thin translucent oiled-silk mantle with water beading on it; over the shoulders a short layered straw-and-fibre cape like a Japanese mino, in nine tiers of slightly different weaves; a woven record band across one shoulder; a wide shallow ribbed hat with a gutter rim and a metal rain chain hanging at the back; tall two-toothed wooden pattens. Earthy, humble, quietly ceremonial. Front and side views, light rain.

vi

Kora House

The third voice — speaking for neither side.

  • Your wrapped plum ensemble, kept wholeThe Kora notebook
  • Tagelmust — veil-turban raised over the mouthTuareg
  • The kora’s twenty-one strings, as warp threadsMande griots
  • Pressed felt, which cannot rustleSteppe felt-makers
  • Split-toe tabi on silent solesNoh stage

Let the words stand without a face. The shoulder crossing you chose rises into a soft collar that can lift over the mouth. When a Kora mediator speaks for neither side, the veil goes up: the face withdraws so the words arrive on their own. The ears are never covered. A listener’s anatomy stays open.

Head of school
The wrapped plum stands as you selected it — the crossing, the woven borders, the headband with visible curls. Two additions: the crossing continues up into a felt collar that can lift over the mouth, and the border is woven with twenty-one gold warp threads. Each head re-weaves one thread in their tenure; the border is “tuned” over generations.
Assembly
The fixed diagonal crossing and vertical pleats from your pale wrap reference, with a soft cowl at the neck stitched down for juniors.
Everyday
One shoulder wrapped, the other bare. Divided pleated lower garment. Colour is free, as it always was; only sound is regulated.
Sport
Breath and balance. Bare torso, a secure diagonal waist facing, felt-gripped bare feet. The athletic version of the crossing is a single soft band from shoulder to hip.
Junior · senior
The collar. Juniors’ cowls are sewn down. When a student first mediates a real dispute, the stitches are cut in front of both parties. After that the veil can rise.
Head and feet
The soft roll: a wrap that rises beside the head and leaves both ears clear. Split-toe felt-soled tabi, so the foot grips the floor without a sound.

Custom — In a Kora room a raised veil cannot be interrupted. Lowering it early, before the sentence ends, is how a mediator admits they have started to take a side.

Rendering prompt

Same adult head of school and wrapped plum ensemble as the existing Kora reference — mulberry and aubergine layers, broad shoulder crossing, woven ivory borders, wrapped headband with visible curls. Add: the shoulder crossing continuing upward into a soft felt collar raised over the mouth and lower face, ears fully uncovered; a fine border of gold warp threads along the main wrap; split-toe soft felt tabi. Quiet, enveloping, watchful. Front and profile views, collar shown lowered and raised.

vii

The Sund Commons

Shared maintenance — nothing here is yours, and all of it is.

  • Standing collar and bright hem bandsSámi gákti
  • The boilersuit, top half dropped and knottedShipyard and engine room
  • Rya — long-pile blanket once used as boat beddingSwedish coast
  • Penannular ring-pinNorse and Irish
  • Glíma belts round waist and thighsIcelandic wrestling

You wear the people before you against your skin. Nothing is owned. Every garment comes from the common stock, and its hem carries a stack of linen tabs, one per previous wearer. When a garment is retired, its tabs are unpicked and sewn into the new one.

Head of school
Sund has no couture, and this is its couture. At the gathering of the seven heads, the head of Sund arrives in the oldest boilersuit in stock — dozens of tabs, patched at every stress point — and over it the rya: a long-pile cape that was the commons’ boat blanket, closed with an iron ring-pin. Everyone else is in silk. Sund comes in a blanket that a hundred people have slept under.
Assembly
Linen boilersuit with a low standing collar and gákti-style bands at collar and hem. Each band colour belongs to a workshop, never a family.
Everyday
The top half dropped, sleeves knotted at the waist, bare torso. Or the waist apron over work shorts. The knotted sleeves are the Sund silhouette from a distance.
Sport
Glíma-style wrestling. The apron straps become the belt-and-thigh harness you grip your opponent by. Strength-and-carry relays with the stock’s own crates.
Junior · senior
Inverted. Juniors are issued the oldest garments in the stock; seniors get the newest, because a senior’s tab will be the first in it. The new arrive dressed in history; the departing leave it something to begin with.
Head and feet
The three-panel workcap; for ceremony, the same cap washed and pressed, turned to show its repairs. Open leather sandals with replaceable soles, resoled in the commons workshop.

Custom — When someone from Sund dies, the stock is searched for every garment carrying their tab. For one day, everyone wearing one walks with the sleeves tied at the front instead of the back.

Rendering prompt

Worn pale linen boilersuit with a low standing collar and bright yellow, red and teal bands at collar and hem in the manner of Sámi dress, patched at the knees and elbows, a row of small linen name tabs visible at the turned-up hem; over it a long, heavy, shaggy-pile rya cape in faded ochres and greys, fastened at the shoulder with a large iron penannular ring-pin; soft three-panel workcap with a short brim; open leather sandals. What changed from the notebooks. Each school now has one gesture doing the work that crests and colours did before: a placket, an edge, a knot, a panel, a strip, a veil, a tab. They are all ways the body is handled — buttoned, turned, tied, elected, cut, lifted, inherited — so every school’s identity is something a wearer does, daily, in front of others. That gives the novel scenes for free.

Seniority is solved seven ways instead of one: trousers, a hidden lining, a hat fold, an inherited panel, the right to mend others, a cut stitch, and an inversion.

Where I’d push next. The gathering of the seven heads wants its own plate — silk, songket, felt, oiled silk and one boat blanket in a single row. Then the in-between garments: a TKI student who transfers to Lan Fong and has to choose which side to close an elected panel on.

The names Temenggong gives its tanjak folds, and the Kora border’s twenty-one threads, are both small enough to write as lists — and long enough to carry a history of the schools in miniature.
