Part one

Seven lineages, one planet

The seven houses of the Straits are late branches of something much larger. Each grammar grew up in many places at once, wherever people faced the same kind of problem, and each became a planetary lineage with sister branches on every continent. The Straits is simply where all seven washed up on the same shore.

What follows takes that seriously. None of these works need exist in the same world or the same century. Some belong to histories where one lineage won; some belong to histories nobody wrote down. Real places and traditions are the seeds, and everything grown from them here is invention.

The Meridian Orders

Parallax and the Survey

“Every place can be described exactly, from somewhere.”

The Orders spread wherever empires needed to count what they held. They are the lineage people trust least and rely on most.

Sister branches

Part three

From the doorstep to legend

Five rungs for each lineage. The first is something you could trip over on your way home. The last is something nobody alive has seen, and every child can describe.

The Meridian Orders

Parallax and the Survey · in the Straits, Toa Keng

IThe doorstep
Benchmark doorsteps
Wherever the Orders reached

A brass bolt in the step holds a house level to the millimetre. Subsidence stops at the threshold, which is why the Orders' old towns stand dead flat on soft ground.

IIThe street and quarter
The true lamp
Baghdad, the Round City

Streetlamps whose light shows things as they measure. Clipped coins glow the wrong colour, false weights cast long shadows, and market inspectors walk the souk at dusk.

IIIThe city works
The firebreak grid
Hangzhou, a timber city of a million

Chalk lattices strung over every ward. Fires stop at the line, so the Orders' cities burned a quarter at a time and never whole.

IVThe great works
The Standard Road
Samarkand to the sea

Three thousand kilometres surveyed so exactly that the road cannot flood, subside or be lost. Caravans crossed blizzards by its milestones, which glow faintly when you stray.

VLost to legend
The Library of All Frames
Alexandria

It held every event described from every viewpoint. It never burned. It dissolved, on the night every description became true at once and the scholars inside could no longer act in any of them. What the world remembers as fire was the light of that night.

The Covenant Roads

Amanah · in the Straits, Al-Merikani

IThe doorstep
The sealed letterbox
The Swahili coast

Letters only the addressee can open. Postmen are bonded, and a lost letter is a breach.

IIThe street and quarter
The permission gate
Caravanserais from Aleppo to Kashgar

Walls that admit anyone who pledges peace, and nobody else. An army once camped outside one for a month, unable to pass a door that let in its own cooks.

IIIThe city works
The trust granary
Cairo

A city's grain held in amanah. In famine it opens to every household by name, and it cannot be looted, bought or seized, even by a sultan who tried.

IVThe great works
The Honest Roads
Aden to Calicut to the Straits

Sea routes shortened by centuries of fair dealing, so that Aden to Calicut takes one night. Cheat on the route and the ocean lengthens by a week, for you alone.

VLost to legend
Iram of the Pillars
The Empty Quarter

A city sealed in trust for heirs who were never born. It still stands, whole and lamplit, under seal. Caravans see it on the horizon some nights. Nobody can enter until the rightful receiver arrives, and the trustees are still waiting.

The Keepers of Ground

Communion and the Rooting · in the Straits, Balai Pusaka

IThe doorstep
Rooted posts
The kampung

House posts rooted into the ground by the family that raised them. The house cannot be pulled down while anyone of that line is alive.

IIThe street and quarter
The living bridge
The Khasi hills

Roots trained across ravines over generations, stronger every century. The Keepers say the oldest bridges have started, very slowly, to walk.

IIIThe city works
The mangrove wall
The coasts of the Sunda shelf

Seawalls of braided mangrove, tended by the same families for two hundred years. They are the only seawalls on Earth that grow after a storm.

IVThe great works
The forest that holds the hill
The Ethiopian highlands

Whole mountains held in place by tended church forests. When a grove is abandoned, the hill beneath it quietly begins to slide.

VLost to legend
Sundaland
The drowned plain between Borneo, Sumatra and Java

When the sea rose, the Keepers of the lowlands refused to let go of their ground. Once a century, at the lowest tide, their forest stands up out of the strait for a single night, and the old paths can be walked from island to island.

The Houses of Setting

Recomposition and the Setting · in the Straits, Lan Fang

IThe doorstep
Signboard wards
Every shophouse coast

Characters painted over a shop guard the family living above it for as long as they stay legible. Repainting a faded sign is an act of protection.

IIThe street and quarter
The talisman substation
The river towns of Lingnan

Fu arrays printed in cinnabar light a whole district. The power company is a printing press, and a blackout is a typo.

IIIThe city works
The recomposition yard
Canton and Nagasaki

After a typhoon, a port is set again from its own wreckage overnight: scrap hulls into bridges, fallen roofs into seawall.

IVThe great works
The Grand Forme
The imperial canal

A canal typeset through two mountain ranges, every lock a character. Each dynasty reset the forme, and the empire's rivers moved to follow the new text.

VLost to legend
The Typecase of Cangjie
Before the dynasties

When the first characters were set, grain rained from the sky and ghosts wept all night. The legend says Cangjie's typecase held every character that would ever be needed, and some that never should be. It was buried, and every character in no dictionary is a slug that has worked its way to the surface.

The Weaving Calendars

Reciprocity and the Weave · in the Straits, Nine Rain

IThe doorstep
Roof looms
Andean towns and river cities

Gutters strung as warp threads, a weft beam under the eaves, and the day's rain knotted by the family every evening.

IIThe street and quarter
Cooling warps
Cusco to Mérida

Heat combed into lattices over the streets at noon. Shade is sold by the hour, and a city's rich quarter is its coolest.

IIIThe city works
Water temples
Bali

Networks of temples that tell each terrace when to flood, by the calendar. There is no king, and the rice has not failed in a thousand years.

IVThe great works
The Great Warp
The Andean cordillera

Cloud systems strung along the whole mountain range as a single loom. Lake Titicaca holds the rain the world has borrowed and has yet to repay.

VLost to legend
The Account of the Suns
Mesoamerica

Four worlds ended before this one. The Calendars say each was an accounting period that closed on an unpaid debt: flood, fire, wind, hunger. The Fifth Sun is still open. Somewhere a loom is keeping our cord.

The Singing Lineages

The Third Voice and the Jeliya · in the Straits, Sané-Kora

IThe doorstep
Listening rooms
Every household

A small wood-lined room where disputes are heard by someone who is not family. Nothing said there leaves, because nothing said there is written.

IIThe street and quarter
Whispering arcades
The markets of Jenné and Timbuktu

Galleries that carry a voice only to the ear it is meant for. Deals are struck across a crowded square in perfect privacy.

IIIThe city works
The court of oral record
Kangaba, on the upper Niger

The law is sung in full every seven years, and anything not sung lapses. It is the only legal code on Earth that cannot be quietly amended.

IVThe great works
The Epic Wall
The Kyrgyz steppe

Singers held a nation's memory so completely that an invading army found the borders sung shut. Their scouts reported a country that did not seem to end.

VLost to legend
The Walls of Thebes
The Aegean

Amphion played and the stones walked into place. The Singers' version adds one line: the walls stood as long as someone sang them, and fell in the first generation that wrote the song down instead.

The Ring Assemblies

The Commons and the Joining · in the Straits, Sund

IThe doorstep
Lapped footbridges
Fjords to the Straits

Planks that share every walker's weight, so they never creak. A bridge that creaks is a bridge someone has stopped maintaining.

IIThe street and quarter
The carried house
The Philippine islands

Bayanihan: a whole village lifts a house on bamboo poles and walks it to new ground. With enough hands, the house does not feel the move.

IIIThe city works
The village roof
Shirakawa-gō

Great steep roofs rethatched in a single day by the whole village. Each roof holds exactly as long as the rota does.

IVThe great works
The Frisian dyke
The Wadden coast

A sea dyke held by a ring of every farmer on the coast. The old law was blunt: whoever will not dyke must leave.

VLost to legend
The Ring at the Rift
Þingvellir

The Althing met on the seam between two continents. The Assemblies say that for three centuries the island's whole ring held the rift closed. When the chieftains began to stand alone, the ring broke and the ground opened. The rift valley is the scar.

Part four

The copyists and the drifters

Two lineages built almost nothing on purpose and have outlasted most of what the others built.

The Copyists

In the Straits, Sekolah Petang

Woodblock almanac printers, mimeograph schools, samizdat typists, photocopy zines. Their infrastructure is the corner copy-shop, which in their world is the power station of the poor: any casting on earth, reproduced badly for a coin. Their legend is the Almanac, the one book every household on the planet owns a copy of. Nobody knows who first set it, and every copy is slightly wrong in a different way.

The Drifters

In the Straits, Rumah Arus

Pacific wayfinders, the Bajau and the Orang Laut, river boat-people everywhere. They built nothing and hold no relation; they ride what already moves. Their infrastructure is the ocean itself, read correctly. Their legend is the floating cities that never once touched land, and still circle the equator on the great currents, invisible to anyone holding a chart.

Part five · the legend under all the others

The Port That Was Carried Away

Why the seven lineages ended up in one strait.

Every lineage in the Straits tells a version of the same story, and every version claims to be the original.

Before the Straits, they say, there was a port on the same water, greater than any city since, and it was the only place on Earth where all seven sciences worked on the same thing at once. It was surveyed so exactly that its channels never silted. It was held in trust, so nothing unloaded there could be stolen. Its quays were rooted into the mangroves by families who never left. Its streets were set in type and reset every monsoon. Its roofs were looms. Its laws were sung, and never written down. And it was built without a single glued joint, so that the whole city could be taken apart.

Which is exactly what happened. The lineages quarrelled over who had carried the most. Nobody could agree, because each counted power in a different unit. So they did what the port had been designed for. They took it apart, joint by joint, and each lineage carried its share away.

· · ·

This is why no archaeologist has ever found the capital of the old maritime empire of that sea, though every chronicle says it was the richest port in the world. There is nothing to find. It was never ruined; it was demounted. The Meridian Orders took the benchmarks, and every brass bolt from Samarkand to Greenwich is one of the port's. The Covenant Roads took the seals. The Keepers took the roots, which is why the mangroves of the whole Sunda shelf are one plant. The Houses of Setting took the type. The Calendars took the looms. The Singers took the laws, which are still being sung. And the Assemblies took the joints, and have spent a thousand years teaching the world to carry things together, so that one day the port can be carried back.

The seven houses of the Straits do not agree on much. But each of them, quietly, keeps one piece in a locked room: a brass bolt, a double seal, a root in a jar of river water, a single slug of type, a length of cord, a verse no one may sing, a peg of old timber.

And every Regatta of Seven Hands ends with all seven houses building one structure together, overnight, and taking it down at dawn. The city thinks it is a game. The oldest members of every house think it is a rehearsal.
