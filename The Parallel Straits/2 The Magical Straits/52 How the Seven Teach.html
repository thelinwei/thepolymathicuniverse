The Great Schools of the Straits · on schooling, disciplines and libraries

How the Seven Teach七学

Seven ways of dividing what can be known, followed from a six-year-old’s first morning to a doctorate, and into the libraries each house built to hold it.

Toa KengAl-MerikaniBalai PusakaLan FangNine RainSané-KoraSundA day at tenBuildingsThe Crossing ExaminationSeven report cardsThe Concordat Library

Every curriculum is a cosmology. Our own sorts knowledge by object: the body to medicine, the past to history, number to mathematics. Each of the Seven sorts it by the question its house asks of reality, and those questions cut across our disciplines at strange angles. Cooking and chemistry share a classroom. Medicine splits in two. Mathematics begins with zero, or with a ledger, or with a folded sheet of paper.

Stages follow the Straits’ common pattern: primary from seven to twelve, secondary from thirteen to seventeen, tertiary after. Each house runs its own schools; the Concordat requires only that every child spend some hours each week in another house’s classroom, which is where most of the trouble starts.

Toa Keng

Parallax

“Where were you standing?”

Knowledge is sorted by distance from the knower: me, us, them, others. Every fact is taught together with the place from which it holds. A Toa Keng child learns early that an answer without a standpoint is only half an answer.

PrimaryThe Near School
HereScience of the body, PEMeasuring one’s own pace, reach, pulse and eyesight. The body as the first instrument.
NearSocial studiesFamily, street and neighbourhood, described by the child and then by the neighbour.
FarLanguages, world studiesOther peoples, always introduced by someone from among them.
BeyondMathematics, astronomyNumber, shape and stars: the things that look the same from everywhere, or seem to.

Every lesson is done twice from two places. Children swap desks each morning. Before a teacher accepts any answer, she asks where the child was standing when they found it.

The Two-Station Test at twelve: each child describes the same object from two stations and must say precisely where the descriptions disagree.

SecondaryThe Middle Rings
FramesGeometry, drawing, map projectionHow the same world looks in different coordinate systems, and the rules for moving between them.
TransformsAlgebraMathematics taught as the art of carrying a truth from one frame into another.
SurveyPhysics, geographyFieldwork with instruments. Every measurement is recorded with its station.
TestimonyHistoryThe past as competing accounts. Students must write each event three times from three witnesses.
Other TonguesLanguages, translationTranslation as a science of loss: what each crossing keeps and drops.

Classes are held in rotating rooms around a central court. Examinations give each student a fixed station and grade them on the honesty of their limits.

盲人摸象, the elephant examination, at seventeen: each candidate is given one part of something unseen and graded on how exactly they state what their part cannot tell them.

TertiaryThe College of Stations
ReferenceMetrology, statistics, logicHow standards are fixed and who fixes them.
CovariancePhysics, mathematicsWhat stays invariant when every frame changes.
CartographyGeography, planning, architectureMaps as arguments. The famous Survey of the Straits is taught here.
Reported WorldsAnthropology, journalismHow to describe others in terms they would recognise.

Doctoral work is supervised by two advisers who must hold opposed frames. The Frontier Fellowship at the Defect is the elite path.

The defence is held in the round, examiners seated on every side. The candidate stands at the centre, the 道枢, and must answer each examiner in that examiner’s frame.

The libraryshelved by 4 divisions
MeUsThemOthers
The building
Four concentric rings around a round reading room. Me is the innermost ring, nearest the reader; Others is the outer wall.
The catalogue
Each book carries several call numbers, as coordinates of distance and angle from the reader. A book on the heart is shelved in Me when it is your heart and in Them when it is a stranger’s.
The rule
Reading slips record where the reader was standing. No book may be read in only one ring before it is returned.
No shelf for
No ring holds the reader. The library shelves everything except the one who is looking.

The child who can stand everywhere and so answers from nowhere. Toa Keng teachers call it drifting and mark it down.

Al-Merikani

Amanah

“On whose behalf do you hold this?”

Knowledge is sorted by whom it is held for. Every fact arrives as a trust from someone and is owed onward to someone else. An Al-Merikani child learns that to know a thing is to become answerable for it.

PrimaryThe Keeping School
KeepingMoral education, scienceOn the first day each child is entrusted with something alive: a plant, a fish, a younger child’s reading.
Counting-forMathematicsQuantity is always quantity for someone. Sums are done in a class ledger with real consequences.
PromisingLanguage, compositionHow to make a promise, write one down and keep it. Poetry begins with vows.
HostingGeography, social studiesTrade routes and guests: who arrives, what they carry, what they are owed.

The teacher’s authority is renewed by the class each term, by a simple show of hands. Teachers take this very seriously, and so do the children.

At twelve, the child returns the thing entrusted on the first day, in whatever state it is now, and gives an account of it.

SecondaryThe Counting House
LedgerMathematics, statistics, accountingDouble entry as a philosophy: every credit is someone’s debit.
CovenantLaw, civicsContracts, oaths, and the conditions under which they dissolve.
WitnessHistorySources as testimony. Who saw it, who vouches for the one who saw it.
NavigationPhysics, astronomyTaught as ship’s captaincy: currents, stars and weather perceived on behalf of a crew.
Held BodiesBiology, healthThe body as something entrusted to its owner, and to physicians in turn.

Students run a real fund together for five years. Every lesson in mathematics eventually touches it.

The first countersignature at seventeen: a stranger entrusts the student with a real matter, and the student must answer for it a year later.

TertiaryThe Houses of Deposit
TrustsLawCovenant law, waqf, inheritance, and the renewal of office.
DepositFinance, economicsMoney as recorded trust. The College of Living Trusts at the Mandated Coast is the elite exchange.
StewardshipMedicine, nursingA patient’s body is held in trust. Surgery requires a spoken entrustment.
WitnessJournalism, historiographyThe profession of vouching.

Degrees are held in trust by the faculty and can be withdrawn for betrayal of a trust, even decades later.

Graduation is the receipt of a trust from someone the graduate has never met.

The libraryshelved by 5 divisions
InheritedLentSwornKeptOwed Forward
The building
A treasury. Reading counters like the old money-changers’ booths at the Arcade, and a vault below for what is Kept.
The catalogue
Each book lists its holder of record and every hand it passed through. Knowledge borrowed from other peoples is shelved as Lent and marked with what is owed to its source.
The rule
No book leaves without a countersignature from another reader, who becomes answerable for its return.
No shelf for
What was never entrusted to anyone. Knowledge held by people with no standing, such as servants and children, has no shelf.

The child who keeps faith with the thing and disobeys the person who gave it. The school calls it disloyalty.

Balai Pusaka

Communion

“Where does this belong?”

Knowledge is sorted by place. Tide physics, a fisherman’s pantun and the medicine of the mangrove share a shelf because they share a shore. A Balai Pusaka child learns that knowledge taken too far from its place begins to go wrong.

PrimaryThe Rooted School
Of the TreeBiology, artEach class adopts one place for six years: a banyan, a stretch of shore, a monsoon drain.
Of the WaterPhysics, geographyFlow, tide and flood, learned from that one place across six years of seasons.
Of the KampungSocial studies, historyWho lived here, who lives here, who is buried here.
PantunLanguage, literatureLiteracy through the paired quatrain, where the first couplet is always drawn from the place.

Classes are held outdoors in the adopted place. The first lesson on the first day is to say tumpang lalu before stepping onto the ground.

At twelve, each child gives a spoken account of their place to the elders, and the elders judge whether the place would recognise itself.

SecondaryThe Second Ground
Land ReadingGeography, geology, ecologyStudents move to a place unlike their first and must read it without help.
AdatCivics, lawCustomary law, and how it bends from place to place.
PusakaCrafts, material cultureThe care of heirlooms: keris, textiles, tablets. Each object is studied as a record of its place.
Voices of the PlaceLanguages, oral historyInterviews with the oldest living people of the second ground.

All subjects are taught through the comparison of two places. Examinations are held on site.

At seventeen, the student walks the bounds of the second ground at night, alone, and returns with one thing the place gave them.

TertiaryThe Faculty of Inhabited Law
CustodianshipEnvironmental science, planningA degree is three years living with one place.
Inhabited LawLawPlaces as parties to agreements. The Civic Watershed is the elite exchange.
Keeping-HousesHeritage, museum studiesThe care of pusaka and the question of whether a relic is an object at all.
Place MedicineMedicine, pharmacologyRemedies taught with the ground they grow in.

Supervisors are elders of the place, often without degrees of their own. The university pays them as faculty.

The thesis is the place’s own testimony, recorded by the graduate. Graduates are known afterwards by the name of their place.

The libraryshelved by 5 divisions
Of the ShoreOf the KampungOf the Forest EdgeOf the Deep WaterOf the Sky Above
The building
There is no central building. Knowledge is kept in small keeping-houses at its places, like wayside shrines. The only central object is a great map, the register.
The catalogue
Every item is catalogued by its place of belonging and its keeper. The model is the local gazetteer, 地方志, which gathers everything about one county into one book.
The rule
Some knowledge may not leave its place and must be read there. Scholars travel to books.
No shelf for
Knowledge of movement: migrants, sailors, anything that belongs to the passage between places.

The child whose place moves: a sea nomad, a child of the ferries, a child who has lived in six estates. The school calls it rootlessness.

Lan Fang

Recomposition

“What else can this become?”

Knowledge is sorted by verb. Every discipline is a kind of transformation. A Lan Fang child learns to read by setting type and to do mathematics by folding paper, and learns early that nothing made is finished.

PrimaryThe Workshop School
Taking ApartScienceClocks, radios, flowers, sentences.
JoiningChemistry, cookingCooking is the first chemistry. Children learn reactions at the stove.
SettingReading, writingChildren learn to read by composing words from metal type, letter by letter.
FoldingMathematicsGeometry, fractions and symmetry, learned from paper.

Children work in apprentice pairs. Everything a child makes must be unmade at the end of the term and made into something else.

At twelve, the child recomposes their first year’s work into a single new object, and must explain every part’s origin.

SecondaryThe Foundry
DissolvingChemistrySolutions, acids and decay: the art of unmaking.
HardeningMaterials, metallurgy, engineeringCasting, forging, curing.
RefiningPE, health, breathInner cultivation: the body refined in stages, breath and posture as a craft.
ComposingLiterature, music, typographyText as arrangement. Every student sets a book by hand.
GraftingBiology, botanyHow living things join, and where joins reject.
ReckoningMathematicsCombinatorics: how many things can be made from these parts.

Teaching follows the kongsi guild: masters, journeymen, apprentices. Knowledge is passed hand to hand.

At seventeen, the student recomposes one of their master’s works into a new work the master could not have made.

TertiaryThe Guild Workshops
The PressLetters, publishing, linguisticsLanguage as a type case, set and distributed.
The KitchenFood science, chemistryCooking is a full faculty.
The Clinic of MendingMedicine, surgeryThe body as a thing that can be recomposed.
The Anatomists’ HallClosedAn old faculty, sealed for a century, whose students went too far into the body.

There are no lectures. Tertiary students are placed in workshops and learn by doing the work.

The masterwork: a recomposition of your teacher’s masterwork, carrying every part of the old one.

The libraryshelved by 7 divisions
DissolvingJoiningGrowingHardeningMendingDecayingRefining
The building
A type case the size of a hall. Knowledge is kept in fragments, such as formulas, recipes, passages and diagrams, in thousands of small drawers.
The catalogue
Drawers are sorted by verb and by how often the fragment is used. The most recombined knowledge sits nearest the hand, the way the letter e sits in a compositor’s case.
The rule
There are no finished books. You set a book from fragments when you need it, and distribute it back into the drawers when you are done.
No shelf for
Anything that cannot be broken into fragments: a whole song, a life story, a face.

The child who wants to keep something as it is. Lan Fang teachers call it sentimentality.

Nine Rain

Reciprocity

“What returns?”

Knowledge is sorted by timescale of return: the breath, the day, the season, the generation, the age. Medicine splits in two, economics shelves with ecology, and history is taught as weather. A Nine Rain child learns that every act is a seed.

PrimaryThe Season School
BreathPE, healthThe body now: breathing, eating, sleeping.
DayScienceSun, tide and shadow, measured every day at the same hour.
SeasonBiology, geographyThe school year follows twenty-four seasons, each a fortnight long.
GiftEconomics, moral educationWhat we owe to parents, farmers and the sea, and how it is returned.
KnotsLiteracy, numeracyChildren record their days in knotted cords before they learn to write.

Every lesson ends with the same question: what returns? On the first day each child plants something they will harvest at twelve.

The harvest at twelve. The child eats what they planted, and gives half away.

SecondaryThe House of Returns
WeatherPhysics, meteorologyForecasting as a moral art.
TendingEcology, agricultureLong cycles of soil and forest.
ExchangeEconomicsGift, debt, interest and release.
ConsequenceHistoryThe past as chains of return: what came back, and when.
WeavingMathematics, dataPeriodicity, trigonometry, records kept in textile.
Heaven’s CourseEthicsThe debate between Xunzi and Dong Zhongshu, taught for a full year.

The timetable is set by the almanac. Some subjects only run in the wet season.

At seventeen, the student writes a letter to be opened in twenty years, predicting one return.

TertiaryThe Academy of Deferred Consequence
Breath MedicineAcute medicineEmergencies, surgery, the body now.
Generation MedicineChronic medicine, public healthWhat illness returns through families.
Long PlanningEconomics, planningThe Long Return is the elite exchange.
ForecastingClimate scienceThe monsoon as a field with memory.

Faculties are organised by timescale. A doctorate takes as long as the return it studies.

Some doctorates are awarded after the student’s death, when the predicted return arrives. A few are awarded to the student’s grandchildren.

The libraryshelved by 5 divisions
The BreathThe DayThe SeasonThe GenerationThe Age
The building
A round building laid out as a calendar. Rooms open and close with the seasons; some are sealed for decades.
The catalogue
Books are shelved by when they will be needed. The catalogue is an almanac, like the 通书, and every entry has a date of next relevance.
The rule
Every loan is repaid with a contribution: a note, a correction, a seed. A textile archive holds records woven by generations of readers.
No shelf for
What happens once and never returns. Singular events have no shelf.

The child who wants something now and for itself. The school calls it impatience.

Sané-Kora

Third Voice

“Who is missing?”

Knowledge is sorted by absence: the known, the not yet known, the lost, the suppressed, the unsayable. The lost wing is always largest. A Sané-Kora child learns to count from zero, and to notice the empty chair.

PrimaryThe Listening School
ListeningScience, musicTo what is said, and to what is left out. Birds, machines, the sea.
NamingHistory, social studiesWhose child are you? Genealogies recited back five generations.
ZeroMathematicsCounting begins with nothing. Rests in music are taught as numbers.
PraiseLanguage, compositionChildren compose praise for one another.
The MissingMoral educationWho is not in the class photograph, and why.

Every classroom has an empty chair. Lessons are call and response, learned by heart before they are written.

At twelve, the child recites their genealogy and names one ancestor nobody else in the family remembers.

SecondaryThe House of Voices
GenealogyHistoryLineages as the spine of history.
The Second SongHistoriographyEvery account paired with the account it silenced.
VoicesLanguages, oral literatureEpic, recitation and translation of the spoken.
RhythmMathematics, musicPattern, meter and interval.
OmissionStatistics, journalismMissing data as the most important data.
MourningPsychology, careHow to sit with the bereaved.

Teachers are apprenticed griots. Much of the curriculum is never written down, and examinations are spoken.

At seventeen, the student sits with a family for their seventh-month offerings and learns whom the empty seats are for.

TertiaryThe Hall of the Third Voice
LineageHistory, law of inheritanceWho descends from whom, and who was left out of the line.
Counterfactual StudiesPhilosophy, historyThe Archive of Foreclosed Histories is the elite exchange.
WitnessLaw, human rightsTestimony for those who cannot testify.
The UnsaidPsychology, poeticsWhat cannot be said, and how it acts anyway.

Each student is apprenticed to a master for the full course. There are no classes, only sittings.

The final examination: sing a patron’s praise and then, once the patron has left, its second song.

The libraryshelved by 5 divisions
The KnownThe Not Yet KnownThe LostThe SuppressedThe Unsayable
The building
The Living Stacks: much of the library is people. Readers make an appointment with a griot and sit with them. The building itself holds the Archive of the Lost.
The catalogue
The Archive of the Lost is a catalogue of books that no longer exist, with empty shelf slots labelled with their titles. Every surviving book is shelved beside what it leaves out.
The rule
No consultation of the Living Stacks without a gift. Nothing in the Unsayable may be quoted.
No shelf for
Its own omissions. The house that knows who is missing is the last to see who is missing from it.

The child who hears the second song and sings it aloud at the wrong time. The school calls it indiscretion.

Sund

Commons

“Who is carrying this?”

Knowledge is sorted by what it takes to carry: what one person can hold, what needs a crew, a village, a whole city. A second index lists what dies if nobody practises it. A Sund child learns that knowing is a kind of labour, and labour is shared.

PrimaryThe Crew School
CarryingPE, physicsMoving heavy things together: levers, balance, rhythm.
TendingScience, civicsThe school is cleaned, repaired and gardened by its pupils.
Fair SharesMathematicsDivision as the question of how to split a load fairly.
AssemblyCivicsThe class decides real matters together each week.
SagasLiteratureLong stories told in turns, each child carrying one part.

Grades are given to crews. Every child may leave their crew once a term without explanation, and the crew must reorganise without them.

At twelve, the crew builds something that none of them could carry alone, and then takes it apart.

SecondaryThe Assembly House
Crew WorkEngineering, designEvery project is built by a crew.
NetworksMathematics, ecologyGraphs, colonies, mycelia and routes.
The CommonsEconomicsShared resources, and how they fail.
AssemblyLaw, politicsThe Thing, the council, the vote.
BurdenHealth, labour historyWho has carried the city’s load, and what it cost them.
LeavingPhilosophyRites of exit: when and how a person may leave a whole.

Lessons are run by students in rotation. Teachers act as the assembly’s clerk.

At seventeen, the student formally leaves their crew for a month, and returns only if they choose.

TertiaryThe School of Composite Governance
MaintenanceEngineeringThe unglamorous science of keeping things working.
Composite GovernancePolitics, organisationWhen a group becomes a person, and when it may not.
Commons LawLaw, economicsBurden rolls and their audits.
Choral StudiesMusic, psychologyThe Choir, studied carefully and at a distance.

Degrees are awarded to crews. Individuals may leave a crew and take their share of the degree with them.

The thesis must be maintained by its crew for a full year after the defence. If it falls apart, the degree is suspended.

The libraryshelved by 5 divisions
One HandA CrewA VillageA CityDies Unpractised
The building
Open stacks, run by the readers. Well-used paths wear into the floor, and books migrate to where they are most read.
The catalogue
The catalogue is the wear. A register of practices lists knowledge that lives only in people doing it, with the number of living practitioners beside each.
The rule
Books are borrowed in pairs. Every reader owes the library one shift of shelving a month.
No shelf for
Knowledge that belongs to one person and should stay that way: a diary, a secret, a private grief.

The child who works best alone. The school calls it withholding.

The buildings

Each house’s school building is its pedagogy made solid. An architect could identify the house from the plan alone.

Toa Keng. Classrooms ring an open court with no fixed front; the teacher’s desk moves weekly. Windows are set at deliberately different heights, so no two rooms frame the same view of the city. At the centre of the court, a round platform for examinations.

Al-Merikani. A counting house. Every classroom has a counter between teacher and pupils, where things are handed across and signed for. Beneath the school, a small vault holds what each child has been entrusted with, each box labelled with two names.

Balai Pusaka. Barely a building. A shelter on stilts at each adopted place, attap-roofed and open-sided, light enough to be carried by twenty people. The central school is only a hall for elders and a room for the register map.

Lan Fang. A row of shophouses knocked through. Every classroom has a stove and a type case. The walls are panelled so that pupils can take them down, and the plan changes every year.

Nine Rain. Courtyards open to the sky, arranged around a sundial floor. Classrooms face different directions and are used in different seasons. A garden at the centre holds every seed planted by every class still enrolled.

Sané-Kora. A round hall tuned for the unamplified voice, where a whisper at one side can be heard at the other. Every room has one chair more than it has pupils. The windows are small; the school is for listening.

Sund. Built by its own pupils in demountable timber, and rebuilt by each new intake. Nobody quite remembers the original plan. The only permanent element is the assembly floor, worn smooth.

The Crossing Examination

At twelve, every child in the Straits sits the one examination the Concordat sets for all houses. The paper is a single object placed on the desk. This year it was a cracked bowl.

Each child answers twice: once in their own house’s question, and once in the question of their current crossing house.

  1. Toa Keng. From where is the bowl cracked?
  2. Al-Merikani. On whose behalf is the bowl held, and who answers for the crack?
  3. Balai Pusaka. Where does the bowl belong?
  4. Lan Fang. What else can the bowl become?
  5. Nine Rain. What returns because the bowl cracked?
  6. Sané-Kora. Who is missing from the story of this bowl?
  7. Sund. Who has been carrying the bowl?

Paired examiners mark each answer, one from each house concerned. The regulations contain one clause that almost never applies. An answer written in two houses’ forms at once, so that neither examiner can mark it alone, is stamped unclassifiable and forwarded to the Concordat registry.

Most years the registry receives none. In the protagonists’ year, it received seven.

Where the seven meet

The Concordat Library

At the centre of the city stands the one library that belongs to no house. Every book in it carries seven call numbers, one from each catalogue, and the seven numbers disagree about what the book is. Librarians are trained in a single catalogue and consult colleagues for the rest. Readers are asked to choose one catalogue at the door.

One book, shelved seven ways. A fisherman’s logbook from the harbour, 1911: tides, catches, weather, debts, and the names of the crew.

  1. Toa Keng. Us, ring two: the harbour described from inside it.
  2. Al-Merikani. Sworn: a crew’s record, vouched by the skipper’s mark, with debts still open.
  3. Balai Pusaka. Of the Deep Water: kept at the harbour keeping-house, and may not leave it.
  4. Lan Fang. Distributed into drawers: tide tables under Joining, fish prices under Reckoning.
  5. Nine Rain. The Generation: shelved for the year the same tides next return.
  6. Sané-Kora. Beside what it omits: the one crewman whose name is never written, only his share.
  7. Sund. A Crew: knowledge that took nine people to make, and one register entry for the practice of reading those tides, with two living practitioners.

The children who cannot choose a catalogue are the ones who find things. A girl walking the Toa Keng rings with a Nine Rain slip in her hand ends up in a room nobody has entered for forty years. The library has a rule that its staff never discuss: a book found through the wrong catalogue may be read, and may not be reshelved.

Crossing hoursEvery child in the Straits spends four hours a week in another house’s classroom, rotating each year. By seventeen they have sat in all seven. Most remember the crossing hours as the most confusing and most honest lessons of their schooling.
The misfits’ educationThe seven protagonists are all children who did better in their crossing hours than in their own house. Their records read as failures. Read through all seven catalogues at once, they are the only complete transcripts in the city.
Companion to the Resonance Atlas and the Ancestral Atlas of the Seven. House names follow the current register.
