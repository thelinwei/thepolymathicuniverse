Field Atlas of the Parallel Straits. Magic series, following the Armoury of the Seven Houses

The Uneven GroundHow the seven houses hold magic, and what holding does to it

The houses share one physics: magic rewrites relations locally, and World Resistance always sends the bill. What differs is how each house holds that power. Who may touch it, who carries it, who is paid, who pays. Over centuries that holding leaves marks in the ground itself, so the city's magic runs thick in some streets and thin in others, cheap for some hands and ruinous for others. This volume maps those marks, and the history that taught the houses how to make them.

Part one

The world has done this before

Every way the houses hold magic has a precedent in how our own world held dangerous or valuable knowledge. Silk, glass, porcelain, printing, vaccines, rubber, the bomb, and now the machines that learn. The patterns repeat across a thousand years with remarkable faithfulness, and each one, carried into a world where knowledge has physics, becomes stranger and more visible. Four verbs organise them: keeping, forbidding, fencing and freezing.

Keeping Holding a craft inside a body, a city or a company

Silkworms in hollow canes

Byzantium, around 552

China's monopoly on sericulture held for centuries. According to Procopius, monks carried silkworm eggs west hidden in their staffs and brought them to Justinian, and the monopoly was over.

A monopoly on a living process leaks through the smallest body that can carry it.

In the Straits: groove-cuttingsA groove can't be carried, but a seed of one can. A pinch of kampong soil, a page torn from an Al-Merikani ledger, a splinter of Sund bridge timber. Planted elsewhere, each grows a small patch of another house's cheap ground. Groove-cuttings are the city's most smuggled contraband, and a few pots of them on a balcony can make an ordinary flat into a foreign embassy of magic.

The glassmakers of Murano

Venice, from 1291

The Republic moved its furnaces to Murano. The master glassmakers were honoured, well paid, allowed to marry into the nobility, and forbidden to take their craft abroad.

The most valuable practitioners become the most confined.

In the Straits: the gilded registersThe city's greatest casters live in the best houses and hold no passports. Toa Keng lists its first-rank surveyors as instruments of the standards office; Balai Pusaka's custodians may not leave the ground their relics are rooted in. Defection is the most dramatic crime the city knows, and every house keeps a quiet file on which of its stars might go.

The porcelain prisoner

Dresden and Meissen, 1701 to 1710

Johann Friedrich Böttger promised Augustus the Strong gold. Held under guard, he worked with Tschirnhaus toward Europe's first hard-paste porcelain, and the secret was kept behind the walls at Meissen.

The patron pays for gold, receives something else, and keeps the maker to keep it.

In the Straits: the accidental yieldLan Fang shareholders fund castings for ore. Sometimes the kongsi's hands find something else instead: a new relation, a stranger metal, a way of setting type in water. Under the articles, the discovery belongs to the company. The hand who found it is promoted, housed, and never allowed to work for anyone else again.

Forbidding Naming what may not be known

The Index of Forbidden Books

Rome, 1559 to 1966

For four centuries the Church published its list of prohibited titles. Copies circulated anyway, and for the curious the list itself became a reading guide.

A list of what not to read is a map of what matters.

In the Straits: the Register of Prohibited RelationsToa Keng's register is the most studied document in the city's cellars. Every banned casting gains an aura and a price. Cellar-children learn the register by heart before they can cast anything on it, which means the office has, without meaning to, written the syllabus of the underground.

Cover-names of the alchemists

Europe, medieval to seventeenth century

Alchemists wrote in Decknamen, cover-names such as the green lion or the king's bath, readable only to initiates. Copied and recopied, the recipes drifted.

Knowledge hidden in language mutates as it travels.

In the Straits: street variantsForbidden spells passed hand to hand change with every copying. Some come out weaker, some stranger, a few more powerful than the original. These street variants have no entry in any register, so no house's dispel is written for them. The underground has its own evolution, and some of its species can't be killed because nobody has named them.

The press

Mainz, from around 1450

Movable type made copies cheap. Within a century the institutions that governed reading had lost control of what people read.

A cheap copying technology unsettles every priesthood at once.

In the Straits: Lan Fang's typeLan Fang's magic is typesetting: a relation set once can be printed and cast by many hands. That makes it the city's press. Houses that hold magic through scarcity fear Lan Fang's type more than any rival's weapon, and the standards office keeps an inspector in every Lan Fang print-hall.

Fencing Taking from some and gating the rest

Onesimus and the smallpox

Boston, 1721

An enslaved West African man, Onesimus, described inoculation against smallpox to Cotton Mather, who championed it during the epidemic. The practice came from Africa; the city's credit went elsewhere.

The knowledge that saves a city often comes from the people least permitted to hold it.

In the Straits: the unlicensed cureWhen the city is in real danger, the working answer is often held by someone with no writ: a Petang student, a hawker's grandmother, a crosser. Every house must then choose between accepting a cure it cannot license, licensing it after the fact under its own name, or letting the crisis run.

Seeds at Kew, gardens here

1876 and 1888

Henry Wickham shipped rubber seeds out of Brazil to Kew. Seedlings went east, and at the Singapore Botanic Gardens Henry Ridley developed tapping methods and pressed planters to take up the crop.

Knowledge is gathered in one place, grown in a garden in another, and profits in a third.

In the Straits: the Garden of RelationsToa Keng keeps a glasshouse of other people's magic: cuttings of every house's grooves, grown under measured conditions, catalogued and licensed back to the city. It is the most mixed ground in the Straits and the least owned by anyone whose ancestors made it.

The examination halls

China, 605 to 1905

The imperial examinations opened office to anyone who could pass. Passing usually took decades of tutoring that only families with means could fund.

An open door with a very long corridor.

In the Straits: the open examinationToa Keng's licensing examination admits anyone. A cellar talent with no tutor gets a desk, a paper and no real chance. Lan Fang's Hakka memory knows this corridor well, and the kongsi's night-tutoring halls exist partly to shorten it for their own.

Enclosure

England, sixteenth to nineteenth century

Common fields were fenced and titled, act by act, parish by parish, until most of the land once shared was private.

Shared ground becomes owned ground one lawful hedge at a time.

In the Straits: fenced groovesSund's commons are grooved by all and owned by none. Each private ward built on them fences a piece, and fenced groove answers only to those inside the fence. The city's commons shrink by a courtyard a year, and nobody has ever broken a law doing it.

Freezing Holding an uneven map still and calling it stability

Born secret

United States, 1946

The Atomic Energy Act classified certain nuclear information from the moment of its creation, whoever created it. The principle came to be called born secret.

Knowledge owned before anyone finds it.

In the Straits: born registeredToa Keng's founding claim: any relation of measure, discovered by anyone, anywhere, is registered at the moment of discovery. A child who finds one in a cellar has trespassed on property nobody has seen yet.

The grand bargain

Non-Proliferation Treaty, 1968

States that already had the weapon kept it; the rest renounced it in return for access to peaceful uses and a promise of eventual disarmament.

A treaty that freezes an uneven map.

In the Straits: the Concord of LegendariesEach house holds one legendary artifact and has sworn never to forge another. The struck-off schools and the crossers, who arrived after the Concord, may not forge one at all. Every legend in the Armoury is also a deterrent, and the peace of the city rests on seven weapons nobody has used in living memory.

Dual use

Cold War export controls, 1949 onward

Machine tools, computers and chemicals that served both factories and weapons were placed under control lists, and the lists never stopped growing.

The most useful knowledge is the hardest to govern, because it is also everyday.

In the Straits: the same rainNine Rain's weather is irrigation and siege at once. The casting that could clean Basin Seven could also poison it. No house can control the dangerous magics without also controlling the ones people need to live.

Governing the substrate

The present

As machine learning grew harder to govern as ideas, attention turned to the ground it grows on. The EU AI Act presumes systemic risk above a threshold of training compute; export controls on advanced chips began in 2022. Alongside runs an open argument: release models openly so capability and scrutiny spread widely, or hold them closed so a few accountable hands can control their use.

When the knowledge can't be held, hold the ground it grows on.

In the Straits: governing groovesSpells leak; grooved ground doesn't. The houses learn to govern soil instead of formulae, and the real struggle moves to zoning, land title and demolition. The same argument runs between Sund, whose commons lie open to any caster, and Toa Keng, whose ground answers only to the licensed. And somewhere in the city is the rumour of an instrument that can teach casting to whoever holds it, which would shorten every corridor overnight.

Part two

Five ways holding shapes the magic

Governance in the Straits is physical. How a house holds magic changes what that magic can do, what it costs, and where its bill lands. Five mechanisms carry the effect from institution to ground.

The grooveThe ground remembers who worked it

A relation rewritten again and again in the same manner wears a groove, and World Resistance softens along it for anyone trained in that manner. A century of survey has made the gridded core cheap for measure-magic and stubborn for everything else. Balai Pusaka rooting runs deep under the old kampongs. The quays have been covenanted so often that a promise made there half binds itself. A caster walking across the city feels the resistance change underfoot like a change of soil.

So territory is magic. Whoever shapes a district shapes what can be cast there for generations, and clearance is erasure. When a kampong comes down, its groove goes with it and the old magic has nowhere to land. Reclaimed land, freshly made, carries no groove at all: every tradition works there at full price, which is why the unregistered practise on it.

The shapeThe institution's form becomes the power's form

Concentrated houses produce spectacular magic carried by a few: Toa Keng's examined surveyors, Balai Pusaka's custodians with relics that hold a relation for three hundred years. Distributed houses produce magic thin in any one person and vast in total. A Sund assembly-casting spreads load across three hundred bodies, each carrying a sliver, and moves a bridge no archmage could shift. Nine Rain spreads it across time instead, a village borrowing from the season and repaying over decades.

Each shape fails in its own way. Concentrated magic is brittle: lose the archmage and the ward falls. Distributed magic is slow to direct and fails with its quorum. When too few come to the assembly, the bridge simply stays where it is that year.

The doorEvery house has one, and a shadow outside it

Each tradition has its own figure for talent that arrives unsanctioned, and each house's answer produces a different kind of outsider. Toa Keng records it as contamination and sends hunters into the cellars. Sané-Kora warns that a voice with no lineage has nothing to hold its nyama, and harms its own speaker. Lan Fang offers a share, or wages. Nine Rain says the child was born on the wrong day-sign, then watches whether the season disagrees. Sund says the assembly is open. Al-Merikani asks only whether the child keeps promises. Balai Pusaka has the most unsettling answer: the relic chooses, and when a keris chooses a hawker's daughter over a prince, the court must accept it or break its own law.

Open houses make many modest casters and a city that is hard to steer. Closed houses make a few great ones and a large, untrained underground.

The billProfit is exporting the load

World Resistance always sends the bill, but it need not reach the caster. The cleverest institutions route load elsewhere, the way a factory routes waste downstream. Toa Keng's wards over the counting-houses discharge into the drains of the districts below, so the rich live under a clean sky while the lower kampongs inherit headaches, rot and bad luck. Lan Fang pays dividends in magic to shareholders and leaves its tailings in the ground and in the bodies of its hands. Al-Merikani covenant-credit lets a merchant cast today and have the load fall due on a debtor years later, possibly in another port.

Against these stand the magics that may not be sold: waqf endowments held forever for public use, pusaka that cannot change hands, Nine Rain's insistence that what is taken returns on its day. The market magics and the unsellable magics have never been reconciled, and Basin Seven is where their bills pool.

The reserveWhat each house refuses to map

The mind-mage in the Empire's prelude teaches that another person's heart is not your territory. Every house in the Straits keeps some relation it treats as sovereign in that sense. For Sané-Kora it is the voices of the dead. For Balai Pusaka it is the will of an object. For Sund it is the commons itself, which may be used by all and enclosed by none. Toa Keng's founding instinct is that nothing lies beyond measure, so its history is a long pressure against every other house's reserves, and its greatest works and its worst harms come from the same gesture.

Part four

House by house

For each house: how it holds magic, what that holding does to the magic, who stands at its door and who stands outside it, where its bill goes, which history it echoes, and the temptation that could break it.

Toa Keng

What is measured may be permitted.

Holds magic as
Examination, licence and register. Magic belongs to the standard, and casters are its instruments.
What that does
Castings that are exact, repeatable and portable anywhere a standard reaches. Its groove is the grid: cheapest in straight streets and surveyed lots, faltering in ground no one has measured.
The door
The examination, open to everyone and passed mostly by the tutored.
The shadow
The unlicensed talent, recorded as contamination. Toa Keng's cellar-hunters are the most feared officials in the lower city.
The bill
Wards over the counting-houses discharge downhill through the drains. Licence fees make it the richest house per caster.
Echoes
Born secret; the garden at Kew; the examination halls.
Temptation
Redefinition: rewriting the unit instead of the thing. And the Garden of Relations, which grows every house's magic under glass and owns the cuttings.

Al-Merikani

A promise kept is a road.

Holds magic as
Covenant, credit and endowment. Standing is the record of promises kept.
What that does
Magic that crosses distance and time. A covenant sealed on the quays binds in a port across the sea. Its groove runs along the river, where two centuries of contracts have made promises nearly self-enforcing.
The door
Anyone whose word holds. Newcomers arrive with no record, and so with very little power.
The shadow
The defaulter. A broken covenant leaves a visible mark, and a marked hand can't seal anything until the debt is cleared.
The bill
Deferred. Load falls due later, on whoever holds the obligation. Against this stands the waqf: castings endowed forever for public use, which no one may sell. It is the house's conscience and its oldest fortune.
Echoes
Endowments that outlive their founders; letters of credit across the Indian Ocean.
Temptation
Bundling deferred load and trading the bundles, until nobody in the city knows whose body owes what.

Balai Pusaka

We keep what keeps us.

Holds magic as
Custodianship. Relics are held by the court, but the relic chooses its bearer.
What that does
The deepest and slowest magic in the city. A pusaka can hold a relation for centuries. Its groove lies under the old kampongs, deeper than any other, and it cannot be moved.
The door
No one can apply. The court prepares heirs; the relic may refuse them.
The shadow
The unchosen heir, raised for a power that never came. And the chosen commoner the court would rather not see.
The bill
Carried across generations by the lineage, and released each year when the relics are ritually bathed. The river runs heavy that week.
Echoes
Murano's confined masters; treasures that can't be sold.
Temptation
Selling ground. When a kampong is cleared the groove dies, so a court that sells its land sells its own magic by the lot.

Lan Fang

Set it once, print it a thousand times.

Holds magic as
Shares in the kongsi. Members vote, draw dividends and hire hands.
What that does
Reproducible magic. A relation set in type can be printed and cast by many, which makes Lan Fang the city's press and its mass caster in ore and earth. Its groove is Lan Fang, dense and rectangular, like a forme of type.
The door
Buy a share, or work for someone who has. Open in principle, uneven in practice.
The shadow
The print-hands, who cast printed relations they don't own and carry the load of them.
The bill
Dividends rise to the shareholders; tailings sink into the ground and into the hands.
Echoes
The press at Mainz; Böttger at Meissen; the kongsi of west Borneo.
Temptation
Floating the kongsi: selling shares to outsiders, other houses included. And printing forbidden relations cheaply, because someone will pay.

Nine Rain

What is taken returns on its day.

Holds magic as
A loan from the season, kept by the calendar-keepers and woven into the year.
What that does
Timing, weather and foresight. Nine Rain magic is overwhelming at the right moment and nearly nothing at the wrong one. Its groove lies across the gardens and catchments, and it moves with the year.
The door
The day-sign of one's birth, though the season can overrule it. Some are called in their fortieth year.
The shadow
The wrong-born who cast out of season. Their workings come back later, with interest.
The bill
Returned to source. Every loan is repaid to the land, and a family can inherit a grandmother's borrowing.
Echoes
Dual use: the same rain for irrigation and siege.
Temptation
Borrowing against seasons not yet come, mortgaging the rains of a future the borrower won't live to see.

Sané-Kora

The word remembers who spoke it.

Holds magic as
Lineage. Voices, genealogies and praise pass within families of speakers.
What that does
Memory, address and the dead. A spoken relation carries every voice behind it. Its groove runs through names more than ground, so it works best wherever names are still said aloud, and those listening places are scattered all over the city.
The door
Lineage. Outsiders may learn the words; the nyama doesn't answer them.
The shadow
Voices without lineage, whose released nyama has nowhere to go and turns on the speaker.
The bill
Held by the speaker's line. Patrons pay griots for praise, and praise binds load and power to the name praised.
Echoes
Oral traditions kept by hereditary masters of the word; the Index inverted, since nothing written can be banned.
Temptation
Selling praise. A griot for hire can make a tyrant's name heavy enough to rule by.

Sund

Many hands, one joint.

Holds magic as
Commons and assembly. Everyone who comes carries a sliver.
What that does
Structural feats impossible for one person: bridges moved, hulls launched, roofs raised in a night. Its groove lies in the yards and on the bridges, worn smooth by many hands.
The door
Open. Anyone may come to the assembly and carry.
The shadow
The free rider, who uses the bridge and never comes to lift it.
The bill
Spread so thin it barely registers. But a joint cast too often wears out and stops answering, and an overgrazed commons can die.
Echoes
Enclosure; the case for open release.
Temptation
Letting a private ward fence one courtyard of the commons, and then the next.

The two struck off

Sekolah Petang

Come after work.

Holds magic as
Practice alone: no licence, no lineage, no shares. The evening school teaches whoever arrives at dusk.
What that does
The most mixed and least grooved magic in the city. Petang casters borrow every house's groove and own none, so their work runs a little everywhere and strongly nowhere, and they are the best in the city at the unmarked reclaimed shore.
The door
Nightfall.
The bill
No one routes Petang's load anywhere, so its students pay it themselves, and often carry other houses' exported load besides.
Echoes
Onesimus; every reader the press created.

Rumah Arus

Everything flows somewhere.

Held magic as
Flow and water right, in the years before the house was struck from the rolls.
What became of it
Its groove lies drowned beneath Basin Seven. Water with a dead groove belongs to no tradition, which makes it the easiest place in the city to send a bill. Every house's exported load runs downhill into the one house nobody speaks for.

Part five

Three tensions, nine positions

No house sits at one end of every argument. The same house can be generous at the door and ruthless with the bill, or closed to newcomers and scrupulous about paying its own way. Placed on three axes, the city looks less like a hierarchy and more like weather.

Where power sits

Balai Pusaka looks institutional until a relic chooses. Then power leaps to a single person the court never picked, and it is the least governable moment in the city.

Toa Keng's door is wide and its corridor is long. On paper it is the most inclusive house in the city.

The house most open to newcomers pays the most for its magic. Petang's students carry their own load and a share of everyone else's.

Part six

The ground in motion

Uneven ground is a story engine. Each of these begins with one house's way of holding magic and ends up in everyone's streets.

The wrong hand

Balai Pusaka, Sekolah Petang

At the annual bathing of the relics, an old keris rises from the water toward a Petang student who came to watch. The court has seven days to accept her, disown the relic, or discover that a pusaka refused is a groove unmade.

The float

Lan Fang, Toa Keng

The kongsi votes to sell shares to outsiders. The buyers are quiet, numerous and well documented, and by the end of the season the standards office controls the press it has inspected for a century.

Downhill

Toa Keng, the Water Authority

A new ward over the counting-houses makes the core the cleanest ground in the Straits. Below it, children in the lower kampongs sleep badly and wake bruised. Josefina's engineers trace the drains, and every line leads to Basin Seven.

The quorum

Sund, every house

The bridge must be lifted before the monsoon. Too few come to the assembly. The free riders turn out to be the other six houses, who have crossed it daily for eighty years and never once carried.

The mortgage

Nine Rain

Forty years ago a Nine Rain elder borrowed a monsoon to end a drought. Now, in the middle of the water crisis, the season comes to collect, and the only people with the calendar to read the debt are her grandchildren.

The street variant

Sekolah Petang, the cellars

A mutated forbidden relation spreads through the cellars. No register lists it and no dispel is written for it. It also seems to be the only thing that makes Basin Seven's water clean, and every house must decide whether to accept a cure it can't license.

The teacher

All nine

An instrument appears that can teach casting to whoever holds it. Every door and corridor in the city is suddenly irrelevant. Six houses want it destroyed, two want it owned, and Sund wants it placed on the bridge where anyone can touch it.

The Concord cracks

The legendaries

A crosser forges an eighth legendary artifact, outside the Concord and bound by nothing. The seven houses have kept the peace for a lifetime by never using what they hold. Now they must decide whether one new weapon makes theirs worth using.

Coda

Nine permissions, one ground that keeps score

In the Empire's lecture hall there is one permission, and it comes from the throne. The Straits has seven, and two more that the houses would rather forget. None of them agrees about who may cast, who carries, who profits or who pays, and every disagreement leaves a mark in the soil.

That is where the magic becomes exciting. A caster in this city is always standing on someone's history. The same spell is cheap on one street and ruinous on the next, lawful at noon in the quays and contraband at dusk on the reclaimed shore. Magic here is terrain, weather and inheritance at once: uneven, contested, and alive enough to surprise the people who think they hold it.
