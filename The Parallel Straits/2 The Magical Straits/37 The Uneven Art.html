01 · The argument in the lecture hall

Who is allowed to touch the world?

The question is older than the Empire, and it cannot be answered merely by identifying the most talented person in the room.

The imperial teacher says magic requires discipline. A student hears: you must wait for the Empire to permit you. Both may be responding to real dangers. Both may also have something to gain.

The argument for authority

Consider the failed harbour spell. A gifted individual altered a current she did not understand. Vessels ran aground for weeks. The school that inherited the wreckage spent forty years repairing its consequences. Its teachers remember the dead whenever someone calls oversight a matter of pride.

“Ability and command of consequences are two different gifts.”

The argument for the individual

Consider the child who can hear a building fail before it does. She has no sponsorship, and the examinations recognise none of what she senses. She is told to wait. On the night the ceiling falls, she acts anyway. The next morning, officials ask how she learned a restricted practice.

“What are your rules for, if the people who need them cannot get through the door?”

The most dangerous sentence in the lecture is not “magic requires responsibility.” It is the next sentence: “therefore it belongs to us.”

Even that sentence deserves a history. Some institutions arose after genuine catastrophe. Others began as protective guilds and became hereditary fortunes. A school can protect a discovery from a predatory state; the same school can keep a useful method out of the hands of people who need it. A self-taught practitioner can free a neighbourhood from dependence; they can also walk away when their beautiful experiment fails.

What this chapter changes

Magic is a common physics, but it is never encountered under common conditions. Aptitude, time, instruments, trusted relationships, land, archives, money and public credibility shape which possibilities become practical. The seven schools are not seven moral camps. They are seven magnificent bodies of knowledge, each with people who teach, protect, bargain, hoard, copy, maintain, speculate and rebel.

02 · The terrain of access

The world gives magic.
Society gives advantage.

One physics. Unequal proximity to the relations, tools and institutions that make it usable.

A gifted person may be powerless without the means to practise. A modest practitioner may wield enormous force through an archive, a workforce, a trusted road or a well-kept place.

The gifted outsider

Power without a platform

She can sense a buried watercourse but owns no land, has no mentor and cannot prove to planners that it exists.

The ordinary graduate

A platform without brilliance

He can operate a harbour ward because generations of instruments, manuals and workers hold its complexity for him.

The invisible keeper

Knowledge without status

She repairs the ward each night and knows the warning sounds that its celebrated designers have forgotten.

03 · Institutions with interiors

Seven schools.
More than seven sides.

Each school's greatest contribution also gives it something valuable to guard. Its students do not inherit a unanimous politics.
I · TKI

The translators

Parallax · descriptions, references, interoperability

TKI opens magical work across languages and schools. It can make a foreign method understandable to people its originators never expected to teach. That also makes the institution a powerful broker: to be translated by TKI is often to become legible to the whole Commonwealth.

A scholarship tutor publishes accessible primers; an archive director withholds dangerous equivalences; a commercial bureau charges for the only approved translation between two major systems. Each calls their work responsible.

The dispute inside

If mistranslation can cause catastrophe, how open should a common language of magic be? And who gains when TKI gets to decide what another tradition means?

II · Al-Merikani

The trustees

Amanah · permission, witness, obligation

Al-Merikani protects strangers by making promises operative. Its wards may shelter people the state would reject; its trusted roads can make distant ports neighbours. But the power to draft terms is itself a fortune. A covenant that looks equal on parchment may be offered to someone who cannot afford to refuse.

Its public sanctuary and its private underwriting house may be funded by the same endowment. The practitioners do not always agree about what that money asks of them.

The dispute inside

Can an obligation remain freely chosen when one party owns the only safe passage? When does a promise become a market in other people's risk?

III · Sri Temenggong

The custodians

Communion · place, continuity, tending

Sri Temenggong knows that a city cannot replace every river, grave, tree and route with a clean map. Its practitioners can defend a living site against destruction. Yet an old relationship to a place can become a claim to decide who belongs there now.

A family with centuries of service, a migrant caretaker who has tended the site for thirty years, and people relocated from it may all hold real, incompatible relationships with the same ground.

The dispute inside

Does authority come from ancestry, maintenance, memory, present care—or the place's own response? Who speaks when those answers conflict?

IV · Lan Fang

The makers

Recomposition · notation, transformation, manufacture

Lan Fang puts once-rare abilities into devices that can be copied and repaired. A working family's first spell may come from its workshop rather than a mage. Its brilliance also turns methods into products, and products into questions of authorship, labour and control.

One lecturer releases designs openly. Another protects a patent to fund further research. A third teaches students to dismantle every patented array and see whose older work has been typeset inside it.

The dispute inside

Who deserves credit when an industrial spell incorporates generations of unnamed craft? Can wide access justify an invention's profitable enclosure?

V · Nine Rain

The accountants of consequence

Reciprocity · cycles, exchange, delayed return

Nine Rain notices costs that others declare nonexistent: heat shifted to a distant ward, water borrowed from another season, resistance deferred beyond an election. Its forecasts can avert famine. They can also make scarcity profitable to whoever sees it first.

Some practitioners insist forecasts must be public. Others know that publishing a prediction changes planting, trade and the future being predicted. A confidential warning may save lives—or save a patron's holdings first.

The dispute inside

When is guarded knowledge a way to prevent panic? When is it simply an information advantage sold to the highest bidder?

VI · Kora

The living archives

Third Voice · testimony, names, absence

Kora can keep an account alive after official records are erased. Much of its knowledge resides in people, performances and relationships. This protects it from seizure; it can also make access depend on a master's favour, a patron's taste or the years a student can afford to spend in apprenticeship.

An elder may refuse to record a song so it cannot be extracted. A younger singer may ask how anyone outside the house will learn it. Both fear losing the tradition, in different ways.

The dispute inside

Who is entrusted to carry a story? What happens when the voice being paid to perform it is not the voice that lived it?

VII · Sund

The builders of the commons

Commons · distributed agency, load, maintenance

Sund turns ordinary coordination into extraordinary capacity. Its neighbourhood wards and shared works can persist where the feats of solitary masters fail. A Sund building's magic resides partly in the people who keep it working: repairers, cleaners, gardeners, residents and stewards.

That is a profound redistribution of power. It is also tempting language for an employer who wants unpaid work to sound like civic pride. A neighbourhood may be celebrated for carrying a public system while its most tired members bear most of the resistance.

The dispute inside

Who may speak for “everyone”? Who can leave a collective work without being shamed? Can a commons pay and credit its keepers well enough to remain a commons?

Beyond the seven. The Copyists reproduce rough versions of costly spells; the Drifters navigate relations the schools are too settled to see. Both are untidy. Cheap copies fail differently from authorised ones. Mobility may offer freedom while costing a community the protection that comes with recognised place and records.
04 · A city changes

The little spell
that became everyone's business.

A fictional episode to test the world's tensions. No one makes a single decision called “the future of magic.”

Lan Fang announces the Hearth Pin: a small, inexpensive array that stabilises simple heat and preservation spells. Its advertisements show a vendor saving unsold food and a family cooling a room through the night.

Month one

Possibility spreads

Demand exceeds the workshops' expectations. Families who have never hired a mage perform useful magic at home. Young practitioners learn by opening the Pin and changing it. A Copyist prints a crude repair sheet that circulates faster than the official manual.

Month four

Reliability has a geography

The Pin works beautifully in old districts threaded with maintained networks. In newer housing on reclaimed ground, it flickers. Wealthier owners buy reserves to smooth the failures; others leave a charged Pin unattended overnight. TKI's maps reveal that the product's promised “universal access” depends on inherited infrastructure.

Month seven

A discovery has ancestors

Sri Temenggong finds that a key component is gathered from ground its keepers have tended for generations. Lan Fang's engineers did not know the relation made the component effective. The source community now asks who made the invention possible. The factory asks what happens to thousands of households if production stops.

The first wet season

Someone has been carrying the cost

Nine Rain finds a slight but widespread delay in monsoon drainage. Sund crews report exhaustion near public stabilisers that absorb household fluctuations. Al-Merikani insurers cover only unmodified Pins, excluding the repairs that make them affordable. Kora records experiences from users whose failures never reached a formal report.

Year two

No return to the previous world

A ban would take useful magic away from people who now depend on it. Unrestricted sales would leave costs with people who cannot choose to avoid them. Different districts improvise different settlements: public repair shops, community reserves, paid maintenance rotas, licensed designs, copied designs, regional limits and illegal imports. Some work. Some become new monopolies.

The Pin changes the world because people can use it. It changes the world again because the means of using it were never equally shared.

05 · Voices in the argument

No single good side.

Give every position a concrete experience that made it plausible. Let people change their minds when the world answers back.

The dispute need not resolve into Empire versus freedom. It can travel between people whose interests overlap for one season and split the next.

The imperial teacher

“Someone must be answerable.”

She has seen the cost of untrained power and knows the state can mobilise repair on a scale no individual can. She also cannot easily see how its examinations mistake familiarity for competence.

The unsponsored student

“I was already practising.”

He learned to steady his neighbour's breathing long before the academy offered a place. Official instruction could help him. Admission would also require him to accept the academy's description of what he does.

The school patron

“Research must be funded.”

Her wealth built laboratories and scholarships. She expects exclusive rights in return. Some of the discoveries would not exist without her; some of the people who made them cannot afford the resulting devices.

The maintenance worker

“Ask who stayed after the unveiling.”

She does not seek a heroic title. She wants a say in schedules, an account of the load she carries, and wages that reflect the fact that the enchantment stops when her crew stops.

The Copyist

“An imperfect copy may still save someone.”

He knows his instructions can fail. He also knows the official version is priced beyond reach. Every revised edition contains corrections made by readers the schools never admitted.

The old master

“Once published, I cannot take it back.”

She guards a technique because she knows what powerful buyers did with the last one. Her student wonders whether protection has become another name for their dependence.

06 · Story engines

Where the world
becomes dramatic.

These are unsettled questions for scenes, characters and institutions, rather than laws to add to a rulebook.

Admission

A school recruits a remarkable street practitioner. Is it offering a path, claiming a discovery, or doing both? What must the student give up to become teachable in its grammar?

Inheritance

An heir has legal title to a relic; its oldest caretaker knows how to wake it; a displaced community remembers why it was sealed. Which relationship will the relic recognise?

Disaster

An unapproved spell stops a flood and worsens a distant drought. Can its caster be celebrated and held accountable? Who knew enough to warn them and stayed silent?

Profit

A company makes affordable magic truly widespread. Its customers become dependent on parts only it can supply. What would it take to refuse a useful monopoly?

Education

A teacher makes students practise by maintaining ordinary works before performing dazzling ones. Is that profound pedagogy, or a convenient way to fill the school's repair rota?

The seven together

All seven schools must operate one planetary work. Each can see a failure the others miss; each needs resources the others control. What kind of trust can survive that negotiation?

The seven protagonists need not begin as spokespeople for seven philosophies. Each can carry a personal loyalty and a grievance against their own school.

That would make Concordance harder and more meaningful. They must discover which relationships have been hidden behind the institutions' official accounts, and decide what to carry together while keeping their differences. The Straits matters because these older planetary grammars meet there under unusually dense conditions, and keep arguing.

Open proposition · The Uneven Art
Magic is everywhere.
Access to it has a history.

Its wonders emerge from people, places, accumulated knowledge and shared labour. So do its concentrations of power. Every attempt to open magic changes it; every attempt to preserve it changes who may enter.
