The Parallel Straits

Old Brine

老卤

The first night

The envelope was the wrong white. Nela noticed it at the bus stop, under the sodium lamp, turning it over in her hands. The stationer near the college sold only the bright kind, bleached for weddings, and she had written Madam Chia’s name on it in blue ink because that was the only pen in her bag. Twenty dollars inside, folded once. She did not know whether that was too much for a woman she had spoken to perhaps forty times in her life, every one of them across a tray of pickles.

Block 7 stood at the lowest point of the Basin, where the ground ran down to meet the canal and the canal had held no water since March. The tentage filled the void deck from pillar to pillar, blue and white stripes, the kind the Board rented out by the week. Someone had strung fluorescent tubes along the poles. They hummed at a pitch she felt in her back teeth.

By the lift lobby, taped over the notice about water hours, was a newer notice under the Board’s seal. Block 7 had been selected for renewal. Residents would be informed of relocation terms in due course. The tape had begun to curl at the corners, as if the paper were trying to lift itself off the wall.

She signed the condolence book beneath a long column of names written in the same two hands, the family filling in for people who had phoned. She pushed the envelope into the box. A woman of about fifty, with a square of sackcloth pinned over her T-shirt, took Nela’s hand in both of hers and did not ask who she was.

“Thank you for coming, girl. Ma would be happy. Eat something. Got peanuts, got kueh.”

In the portrait above the altar, Madam Chia was perhaps sixty, her hair set for the photographer, wearing a kebaya the colour of young limes. Nela had never seen her like that. She remembered a hairnet and a stained apron and the flat of a wooden spoon lifting the lid of the big jar at the corner of the stall. You want extra? Take. Your mother so thin.

The jar was on the altar now. It stood among the oranges and the joss sticks and the bowl of rice with its chopsticks planted upright: squat glass, red plastic lid, two-thirds full of cucumber and carrot and pineapple in a turmeric-yellow brine, with crushed peanuts and sesame floating on top. Nela had seen it a thousand times without ever looking at it.

“She said the jar must come,” the daughter said, following her eyes. Her name was Bee Lian; it was on the wreath stands, from your loving daughter. “Before she went, she told us, bring the jar down. We don’t know why. Old people, you know.”

Nela took a chair at the back, by the table where three uncles were playing cards and a boy was asleep across two seats with his mouth open. The priests in yellow began again, cymbals and a drum and a long chant that rose and fell like traffic.

That was when it came. She knew it the way you know your own handwriting on an envelope you have forgotten sending.

Since she was nine, when Nela prayed properly, with the words shaped and meant, a warmth would open under her breastbone, like swallowing soup too fast, and then whatever she had asked for would happen. Small things, mostly. A bus that came when she was late. A fever that broke. A question on an examination paper that she had revised the night before. The college had a file on her now, and a probation, and a Sané-Kora elder who called her the girl who gets answered with an expression Nela could not read.

Under the tent the warmth was everywhere. It belonged to nobody. It lay at ankle height like the steam off a wet road, and it was hers. She was sure of it with her whole body. Her answered prayers were loose in the void deck, and they had begun to go wrong.

The joss paper in the metal bin caught with a green flame and went out. One of the uncles swore softly and pressed his thumbs into his eyes. A second uncle kept shuffling the same deck, and the cards kept clumping, sticking to each other as if damp. From the back row, over the incense, Nela could smell the achar on the altar. It was sharper than it should have been, as though a week had passed inside the jar in an hour.

Amadou arrived at eleven, in a pressed shirt with his house’s thread at the collar, and sat down beside her without saying hello.

“They send one of us to every wake in the Basin during the seventh month,” he said. “To listen for who answers.”

“And what do you hear?”

He turned his head so his left ear faced the priests. He did that all the time now. He had asked her to take notes for him in lectures, and told her it was because his handwriting was bad.

“The cymbals,” he said. “The words go to mush.”

At the next table a man sat by himself playing patience. He wore a long-sleeved white shirt, ironed with a crease down each sleeve, far too neat for the hour. When the priest sang the dead woman’s name in the chant, the man murmured, “Yes,” without looking up. Later a little girl called across the tent, “Ma!” for her own mother, and the man said, “Yes,” again, quietly, to his cards.

Amadou did not hear him. Nela did. Each time the man answered, the air near his table thinned a little, the way a room thins when someone opens a window. And each time he looked better, like a man who had eaten.

She left at two, and on the bus she tried to pray for sleep. Nothing opened under her breastbone. The warmth had stayed behind in the void deck with the jar.

The second night

They put her to folding gold. Bee Lian set down a stack of joss paper and showed her the fold, once and twice and the ends tucked to make an ingot, and two neighbours from the ninth floor worked across the table, fast and silent, their ingots piling up in a basin like bread rolls.

“Forty-one years she sold achar,” Bee Lian said. “Same brine. Never throw away. Every week she pour a bit in, top up, add salt, add vinegar, add whatever. She call it the mother. You know Hokkien people with their soya sauce pot? Like that. Old brine.”

Nela creased a gold sheet with her thumbnail. “People came to her,” she said. It came out as a statement, and she was not sure where it had come from.

“Wah, all the time. Not to buy. To talk. Somebody’s son in trouble, somebody’s shop cannot make it, somebody’s husband sick. Ma would listen and listen and then she say, leave it with me. Leave what? I never ask.” Bee Lian folded. “And she was always tired. Whole life, tired. The doctor say nothing wrong with her.”

One of the neighbours said, without looking up, “She carry a lot.”

“I’m also tired,” Bee Lian said. “Tomorrow after the funeral I go back to work, six o’clock. My son, survey trainee with Toa Keng, he cannot take leave. After this, the Board will move us anyway.” She laughed a little. “Ma hold this block together forty years. Now they want to take it down, just when she let go.”

At four the water tanker came into the car park with its horn going, and everybody stopped folding and went down with pails.

When they came back, Bee Lian took something out of her bag and put it on the table between the gold ingots. It was a school exercise book with a green cover gone soft at the edges.

“Found it in the rice bin,” she said. “Names. Maybe you are inside. You are the achar girl, right? The one Ma always talk about. Nela.”

Nela opened it. The first pages were in pencil, the letters big and careful like a child’s. A name, a line, a few words. Mr Goh, shop, leave with me. Rosnah’s boy, court case, leave with me. Dozens of them, dated by the lunar month.

Then the handwriting changed. Some entries were in red ink, in a neat, slanted, clerical hand, the hand of someone who wrote forms for a living. Each red entry was short. A name, an age, a matter, and one word: Assigned. Beneath every red line, in Madam Chia’s pencil, there was a reply, as if she were answering a letter.

Nela found herself on the fourteenth page.

Nela, 9. Mother, fever. Assigned.
OK. Can.

She turned the pages and found herself again, and again. Nela, 11. Scholarship. Nela, 14. Father, hospital. Nela, 17. Exam. Eleven years of it in red ink, and under each line the pencil saying yes. OK. Can. OK. Can do. OK.

“Ma cannot write so neat,” Bee Lian said. “Ma only went to school three years. I don’t know who is this red one.”

On the last page, in pencil, pressed so hard that it had come through to the cover:

When I go, let everything go back. Nobody carry after me.

Nela closed the book. Her hands had gone cold on the gold paper. She understood what was loose in the void deck now, and why it was settling on the family. A whole neighbourhood’s miracles had been held by one tired woman in a hairnet, and now they were returning to their owners, as she had asked. Some of them had nowhere to land, so they were landing on whoever was nearest and most tired. Bee Lian had been rubbing her temples for two days. The boy asleep across the chairs had a nosebleed in the morning. The uncles could not shuffle.

Her mother had been nine years well.

Someone set a paper cup of tea down beside her elbow. It was the man in the white shirt. Up close he smelled of starch and of nothing else, the way a new building smells of nothing.

“You look like you’re carrying something,” he said.

“No,” said Nela.

“Yes,” he answered, gently, as if she had called his name, and went back to his table.

The third night

The funeral would be in the morning. By midnight most of the cousins had gone home to shower and sleep. Bee Lian lay across two chairs with her arm over her eyes, still frowning. Her son had come in his survey jacket at nine, burnt a bundle of joss sticks, eaten standing up, and gone back to his night shift. The uncles had given up on cards. The tubes flickered. Somewhere upstairs a tap had been left open during the water hours and was coughing brown air.

Across the car park, the getai stage played to its rows of red plastic chairs. The singer, in sequins, sang an old Hokkien song to the front row, which was empty, because the front row is always kept empty in the seventh month. Nobody living would sit there for any money. The living audience stood at the back with their umbrellas, though it was not raining. The rain had not come right all year.

In the void deck the joss paper would not catch at all now. The lid of the achar jar on the altar had begun to dome.

Nela came back from washing her face and found the man in the white shirt sitting beside Amadou, leaning to his bad ear, talking low. Amadou’s face was the face of someone who has come up from underwater and breathed.

“I can hear him,” Amadou said when she sat down. His voice was too loud, the voice of a person testing a room. “Every word. Nela, I can hear him.”

“What did he say?”

“He asked if I want to keep hearing.”

“And what did you say?”

Amadou watched the priest’s mouth move and did not answer. The man in the white shirt had already turned toward the altar, toward the portrait in its lime-green kebaya.

“Ma,” someone sobbed in her sleep, one of the neighbours dozing by the gold.

“Yes,” said the man.

Nela stood up and went to the altar.

She had never prayed at an altar before. She had prayed on buses, in exam halls, once in the corridor of a hospital with her forehead against a vending machine. She did not know the right way to hold the joss sticks, so she did not hold any. She looked at the jar. The brine inside was darker than it had been on the first night, the colour of strong tea, and small bubbles rose through it and gathered under the lid like breath held.

She knew the rules as well as anyone at the college. The world leans back against everything held true. Someone pays. When Nela prayed and was answered, the bill went somewhere. For eleven years it had gone to a woman who wrote OK, can under a stranger’s red ink. If she prayed now, it would be answered, because her prayers were always answered, and the bill would go somewhere again. She did not know where. She did not know whose book it would be written in, or in whose hand.

She prayed anyway.

Give it back to me. Mine. Only mine. Nobody else carry.

The warmth opened under her breastbone and she waited.

The fluorescent tubes steadied. Their hum dropped half a tone and became almost pleasant. On her two chairs, Bee Lian stopped frowning and turned onto her side like a child. The uncle who had been pressing his eyes lowered his hands and looked around the tent, surprised, as if someone had spoken his name. In the metal bin, the gold ingots caught all at once with an ordinary orange flame and went up in a sheet of light, and the smoke rose straight out between the pillars into the dark.

And something arrived in Nela, or seemed to. A heaviness at the back of the knees, in the shoulders, behind the eyes. It felt like a full pail being carried up nine floors when the lift is down. It felt like eleven years.

Or it felt like three nights without sleep, and grief for a woman she had barely known. She could not tell. She stood at the altar and tried to tell, and could not. Somewhere in the city, perhaps, a neat slanted hand had just written a new name in red. Perhaps not. Perhaps the answer had come from the jar itself, which had carried forty-one years of everybody’s everything and was still, obstinately, souring.

When she turned around, the chair beside Amadou was empty. The white-shirt man’s cards lay on his table in their neat columns of patience, the game unfinished, one king face up.

At six Bee Lian woke, rubbed her face, and looked years younger and very embarrassed to have slept.

“Aiyo, I sleep so long,” she said. Then she looked at Nela, standing where she had stood all night, and at the burnt-out bin, and at the altar. “Ma said whoever sit the last night, that one take the jar.”

“I can’t take it,” Nela said. “It’s your mother’s.”

“You take. Nobody here eat achar anymore. Kelvin say too salty. And they are going to knock down the block.” Bee Lian lifted the jar from among the oranges with both hands, the way you lift a baby, and put it in Nela’s arms. It was heavier than it looked, and warm from the candles. “Every week, top up a bit. Salt, vinegar, whatever you got. Don’t throw away the old one. The old one is the mother.”

Amadou walked her to the bus stop. The canal bed was grey in the first light and a pair of mynahs were arguing on the railing.

“I can hear the birds,” he said.

She waited for him to tell her what he had answered, and he did not, and she did not ask. The bus came when she needed it to, as buses always had for her, and she did not know anymore what that cost or who paid.

She sat at the back with the jar in her lap. Through the glass, the brine moved with the bus, yellow and dark, forty-one years of it, never thrown away. She would have to learn what to put in. The weight was still there in her shoulders, or it was the jar, or it was the morning.

The lid ticked softly as the brine breathed. To Nela it sounded like someone, very quietly, saying yes.
