Before you go in

Seven questions for any object in the room

Every relic in this volume was imagined from a real object in a real gallery: the British Museum, the Met, the V&A. The houses would each look at the same vitrine and see something different. Try it on anything in front of you.

The houses' questions

  1. Toa KengWhat does it measure, and against what standard?
  2. Al-MerikaniWho was it entrusted to, and did they consent to its leaving?
  3. Balai PusakaWhere does it belong, and when was it last used there?
  4. Lan FangWhat was it made from before it was this?
  5. Nine RainWhat season was it made for, and what did it borrow?
  6. Sané-KoraWhose voice is missing from the label?
  7. SundWho carried it here, and how many hands did it take?

If an object answers all seven, it is a relic. If it refuses to answer one of them, that is usually the most interesting thing about it.

Part one

How each house makes a casting permanent

A casting fades when nobody holds it. A relic is a casting given a body, so that the object does the holding. Each house has its own method, its own materials, and its own way for a relic to die.

Toa Keng

A casting fixed by exact proportion. The relic is made to a standard so precise the relation cannot drift.

Crown treasure

The Missing Corner

after the Rosetta Stone, British Museum

Cut, legend says, from the same decree-stone the world knows in London, which has always been missing its upper corners. The Meridian Orders hold that the corner was taken deliberately, by translators at Alexandria, before the stone was lost.

Materials
Dark granodiorite, one fragment the size of two hands, carved with a few lines in each of three scripts.
Power
Anything read aloud in its presence is true in all three descriptions at once. It is the master key of creole casting: two houses that cannot agree on a single word can agree on the Corner.
To wield it
Three readers, each native to a different script, reading together. None may be fluent in the others' languages.
The account
Each reader loses their native accent for the rest of their life. Toa Keng pays pensions to its Corner-readers, who sound, forever after, as if they come from nowhere.
Where it is now
Held in the Hall of Standards, behind a brass benchmark bolt, and brought out only for treaties between houses.
Working relic

The Skull of Two Descriptions

after the rock crystal skull, British Museum

Carved in the nineteenth century and sold as ancient, the skull is the most famous forgery in the Straits. Toa Keng bought it knowing that. A thing with two incompatible histories is a perfect training instrument.

Materials
A single block of rock crystal, polished to a skull, slightly too perfect.
Power
It shows every object held beside it in both its claimed history and its real one, as two overlapping images. Inspectors learn to catch forgeries by practising on it.
To wield it
Held only by someone willing to see their own claimed history too. First-years usually refuse.
The account
The holder spends a week unsure which of their own memories are true.
Where it is now
In daily use at the Board's inspectorate. Toa Keng never displays it, because it is embarrassed by how well it works.
Lost

The Lamp of the Round City

after the enamelled glass lamps of the Mamluk period, British Museum and the Met

Blown and enamelled for the translators' hall in Baghdad, where the Meridian Orders first learned that light is a kind of force. It showed everything in its light as it measured: clipped coins, false weights, forged seals.

Materials
Blown glass, enamelled and gilded, hung from three chains.
Power
Truth by illumination. Nothing could be falsely weighed or measured within its light.
To wield it
Lit only with oil pressed that season, and hung only where scholars of more than one faith worked together.
The account
Its keepers could no longer tell a comfortable lie, even to their own children.
Where it is now
Lost in the sack of Baghdad in 1258. Toa Keng believes a single fragment of its glass is set into the Hall of Standards' lantern, and has never tested the belief.

Al-Merikani

A casting witnessed and sealed. The relic is the physical form of a trust, and holds as long as the trust is honoured.

Crown treasure

The Cylinder of First Terms

after the Cyrus Cylinder, British Museum

A clay barrel inscribed for a king who promised to send deported peoples home and return what had been taken. The Covenant Roads call it the oldest surviving amanah: a ruler's promise, witnessed, to give things back.

Materials
Fired clay, barrel-shaped, dense with cuneiform on every side.
Power
Restitution. Things taken wrongfully drift toward their rightful places in its presence, slowly, a few centimetres a year.
To wield it
Held only by someone who has personally returned something that was not theirs to keep.
The account
Its holder must give back one thing of their own every year, to someone who has a better claim to it.
Where it is now
Kept in the scent rooms, never in a museum. In a museum, the Merikani say, everything around it would start to walk.
Working relic

The Inward Room

after the Damascus Room, the Met

A whole reception room of painted wooden panels, made for a merchant's house, then taken apart and carried across the ocean piece by piece. Its maker designed it to be dismantled: a room built for a family that expected to move.

Materials
Painted and gilded cedar and poplar panels, a fountain basin, a raised floor, a threshold for leaving shoes.
Power
Everything said inside is witnessed. A promise made in the Inward Room binds as firmly as a sealed deed, and a lie told there is remembered by the walls.
To wield it
Enter with a gift, shoes off, and sit where the host tells you. Nothing may be written inside.
The account
The panels darken a shade with every broken promise made there. The oldest panels are almost black.
Where it is now
In use for major agreements. It has been reassembled in four cities and never once lost a panel.
Lost

The Missing Border

after the Ardabil carpets, V&A and LACMA

Two carpets woven as a pair for one shrine, one trust held in two halves. When the first wore thin, dealers cut the second one up to mend it. The Merikani call this the worst breach in the history of weaving.

Materials
Silk warp, wool pile, some three hundred knots to the square inch, a medallion of lamps.
Power
Together, the pair held a covenant of hospitality across the whole route from the shrine to the sea. Any traveller who slept on either carpet was under the protection of both.
To wield it
Laid in two places at once, facing each other across a distance, and never walked on in shoes.
The account
Once the pair was cut, the protection failed along the whole route, and the caravans remember that decade as the years of robbery.
Where it is now
The larger carpet survives in London; the smaller, without its borders, in Los Angeles. The cut borders are lost, and the Merikani still pay a finder's fee for any fragment.

Balai Pusaka

A casting grown by use. Nobody makes a pusaka; a maker starts one, and generations of wearers finish it.

Crown treasure

Keris Sang Penjaga

after the keris in the Met and V&A collections

Forged by an empu over forty days, folding meteoric iron into steel until the pattern in the blade looked like running water. Its hilt has been replaced four times. Its blade has never left the Balai Lama.

Materials
Pattern-welded iron with meteoric nickel, a hilt of kemuning wood, a sheath of old timber.
Power
Pinned point-down, it holds a place: nothing can be taken from it and nobody can be moved off it. Carried, it makes its bearer immovable.
To wield it
Worn, never displayed. Borne barefoot. Bathed every year on the first night of the new year, in lime juice and flower water, by the eldest of the line.
The account
Its bearer cannot leave the ground it protects while they hold it. Two bearers died on the island rather than put it down.
Where it is now
The house's crown treasure. It has missed its annual bathing only once, during the war, and the house still speaks of that year quietly.
Working relic

The Tumpal Songket

after the Malay songket in the V&A

Woven for a bride in the 1840s with a border of gold triangles, the tumpal, and lent to every bride of the line since. Each wedding adds to it.

Materials
Silk with supplementary weft of gold thread, now worn soft.
Power
Protection and belonging. A bride who wears it is held by every woman who wore it before her.
To wield it
It must be worn at least once a year. If it rests in a chest for a full year, the gold dulls and the ward fails.
The account
Its wearers dream, for a month, the dreams of earlier wearers.
Where it is now
Lent across the city for weddings and funerals. It is the most travelled object in the house.
Contested

The Hungry Keris

after keris taken in colonial expeditions, now held in museum stores abroad

A keris taken from a Malay court in a punitive expedition and shipped to a museum cabinet in Europe. It has not been bathed in a hundred and fifty years.

Materials
Unknown. The house has not been allowed to examine it.
Power
None that anyone intends. A pusaka that goes unwashed and unworn does not die, the house says. It goes hungry.
To wield it
Only by its own line, on its own ground.
The account
Curators in its storeroom report that nobody likes working near that cabinet, and that its glass is always slightly warm.
Where it is now
Contested. The Balai Pusaka has written to the museum every year since 1957, asking only to be allowed to bathe it.

Lan Fang

A casting set in type. The relic is a composition that can be reprinted, reset or recast.

Crown treasure

The Sixteen-Page Annal

after the Diamond Sutra, British Library, the oldest dated printed book

Printed in 1885 on the kongsi's press, in type cast from melted metal: the seal of the company-republic, and gold from its mines. The whole republic was recomposed into sixteen pages and carried across the sea.

Materials
Cast type of gold alloy and the melted seal, cinnabar ink, mulberry paper.
Power
Reprinting a page briefly reconstitutes what it describes. A page about the mines smells of wet earth; a page about the founding makes the room vote.
To wield it
Reprinted only on the anniversary, by ballot of all shareholders, one page per year.
The account
Everyone who votes aye loses one character from memory for the year. The house votes anyway.
Where it is now
Kept in the Press Hall, in the same case as the uncounted slug nobody opens.
Working relic

The Scroll of Admonitions

after the Admonitions Scroll, British Museum

A handscroll of instructions for court ladies, text and painted scenes alternating. Its painted figures step out when it is unrolled, and whoever reads it is gently recomposed into the virtues it describes.

Materials
Silk, ink and colour, mounted as a handscroll, older than anyone can prove.
Power
Recomposition of character. Dangerous, persuasive, and much sought after by families with difficult teenagers.
To wield it
Unrolled one arm's length at a time, and never for more than a few weeks each year, or its characters fade into the silk.
The account
Readers become more composed and less themselves. Lan Fang now lends it only to people who ask twice.
Where it is now
In use, reluctantly. The house has proposed three times to retire it and been outvoted three times.
Lost

The Golden Forme

after the lost typecase of legend

A forme set by the Houses of Setting to print an entire city in one impression. Whether it existed is argued. That it was melted down is not.

Materials
Said to have been gold type set in a bronze chase the size of a door.
Power
One pull of the press, and a city stood where the paper was laid.
To wield it
Unknown. The Lan Fang annal says only that it required every shareholder, living and dead.
The account
When it was melted, the characters it held were released all at once. Lan Fang scholars link this to the year every sign in three provinces went blank.
Where it is now
Lost. Every Lan Fang apprentice is told to check the type of any old printing house for gold.

Nine Rain

A casting knotted in time. The relic records borrowed seasons and holds them until they are paid.

Crown treasure

The Grandmother Cord

after the Inca khipu in the Met and other collections

The oldest cord on the Rain Loom, brought across the Pacific by galleon and older than the house. It records the rains of centuries in knots of cotton and alpaca.

Materials
Cotton and dyed alpaca fibre, a main cord with over two hundred pendant cords, knotted in three kinds of knot.
Power
Re-reading a knot repeats that day's weather over the reader. Held to the sky in drought, the Grandmother can rain a century-old rain.
To wield it
Read only with the fingers, never the eyes, by a keeper born on the right day-sign.
The account
The keeper's skin takes the weather of the knot being read. The present keeper has had frostbite in the tropics.
Where it is now
The crown of the Rain Loom. It is touched only at the turn of the monsoon.
Working relic

The Turquoise Serpent

after the Mexica double-headed serpent, British Museum

A carved serpent with a head at each end, covered in turquoise mosaic, worn across the chest. One head drinks rain from the sky, the other gives it to the ground.

Materials
Cedar wood, turquoise mosaic, red spondylus shell for the mouths, white conch shell for the teeth.
Power
It moves rain across distance: drawn in at one head, released at the other, sometimes a valley away.
To wield it
Worn by two people at once, one at each head, walking apart.
The account
The two wearers share a single thirst for a season. Pairs who have worn it together usually stay friends for life, or never speak again.
Where it is now
In use at the Regatta and in droughts.
Lost

The Garden of Gold

after the Andean gold in the Met, and the chronicles of the Coricancha

In the great temple at Cusco, the chroniclers wrote, there was a garden of life-size maize plants in gold. The Weavers say each stalk held a stored season, and the garden was the world's reserve of rain.

Materials
Gold, hammered and cast, in the form of maize, leaves and cobs; herders and llamas of gold among them.
Power
A granary of seasons. Any drought could be broken from it, if the season was put back later.
To wield it
Watered each day by the keepers, although it did not need water. The ritual was the account.
The account
When the garden was melted down by the conquerors, every season it held was released at once. The Weavers date the great storms of the 1530s from that month.
Where it is now
Lost, melted into ingots. The Weavers do not believe a single stalk survived, and keep a place for one on the Loom anyway.

Sané-Kora

A casting tuned. The relic is an instrument that holds relations as pitch and can sound them again.

Crown treasure

The First Kora

after the koras in the Met's musical instrument galleries

Made for the first Sané jeli to settle in the Straits, strung with twenty-one strings, each tuned to a relation. It is restrung every generation and remade every century; the calabash is the oldest part.

Materials
Calabash gourd, cowhide, a rosewood neck, strings of antelope hide in the old manner.
Power
The First Kora can sound relations that have not been named yet. Played in a dispute, it finds the string nobody knew was there.
To wield it
Played only by a jeli of the lineage, and never recorded. It rests on the seat in the Room, and nobody else may sit there.
The account
The player loses the hearing of whichever string they overuse. The old masters are partly deaf in very particular ways.
Where it is now
The house's crown treasure. It has never left the Room.
Working relic

The Drum That Crossed

after the Akan drum in the British Museum, collected in Virginia

Carved and skinned in West Africa, carried across the Atlantic, and collected in Virginia in the 1700s. It is called the oldest African-American object. The Kora house holds a drum made to the same pattern, to keep a memory the first one carries.

Materials
Carved hardwood, animal skin, pegs and cord.
Power
It calls the names of those who were carried across the sea, one name per night.
To wield it
Played only after dark, only with the palm, and never without an answer. Someone present must say the name back.
The account
Whoever plays it learns one name and must carry it for life, saying it aloud once a year.
Where it is now
In use at the Name-Notary rites. Its players are the house's quietest people.
Lost

The Wax Cylinder

after the ethnographic wax cylinder recordings in the British Library Sound Archive

In 1908 a visiting collector recorded a Kora master singing a praise-name, without asking. The master stopped singing halfway, and never sang again.

Materials
A brown wax phonograph cylinder, in a cardboard tube with a handwritten label.
Power
The house's most dangerous object. A voice cut off from its singer, halfway through a praise, leaking nyama that nobody carries.
To wield it
It should not be wielded. The house wants only to complete the song and let it rest.
The account
Rooms where it was stored have a reputation for unhappiness. A cataloguer resigned after listing it.
Where it is now
Lost in transit, or misfiled in an archive vault abroad. The house has been searching for it for a century, and would sing the second half of the praise to it if found.

Sund

A casting jointed. The relic is made of many parts that share one load, so no single part carries it.

Crown treasure

The Ring of Pieces

after the Lewis Chessmen, British Museum and National Museum of Scotland

A hoard of carved pieces, found on a Hebridean beach, of kings, queens, bishops and warders biting their shields. The Sund say the set was never meant for play. It was a ring: each piece carried the load of one member of an assembly.

Materials
Walrus ivory and whale tooth, carved in the twelfth century.
Power
Load-sharing across distance. Each piece bears one person's share, wherever that person is.
To wield it
Laid out in a circle, never on a board, and never played to win. A game with the Ring can only end with both sides winning.
The account
The load does not vanish. Every piece holder carries a share, and has the bruises to show it.
Where it is now
Split, deliberately, between two museums and Sund's ting-hall, so that no single holder ever carries the whole. Sund considers this the correct arrangement.
Working relic

The Great Bed

after the Great Bed of Ware, V&A

A huge carved oak bed from around 1590, built to sleep a crowd. Visitors carved their initials and pressed wax seals into its posts. Sund says each carving was a joining.

Materials
Carved and painted oak, iron fittings, four centuries of initials.
Power
The load of a disaster slept off together. Twenty people who worked a flood sleep in it, and wake up sharing the exhaustion evenly.
To wield it
Nobody may sleep in it alone. Every sleeper carves or seals their mark.
The account
Shared dreams, and matching bruises in the morning.
Where it is now
In use after every flood and fire. Its waiting list is long.
Lost

The Ghost Ship

after the Sutton Hoo ship burial, British Museum

A clinker-built ship buried under a mound. When it was dug up, the timbers had rotted entirely away. Only the iron rivets remained, still in their places, holding the exact shape of a ship in the sand.

Materials
Iron rivets, and the shape where oak once was.
Power
The most perfect joining ever made. The planks are gone and the relation between them survived: a ship made only of joints.
To wield it
Nobody knows. Sund believes it would need a crew who had all lost someone.
The account
Unknown. Sund says it has not been paid yet.
Where it is now
Lost. The rivets are in a museum. Sund believes the ship itself still sails, plankless, and a few Sund elders claim to have seen its outline on the water on the night of the King Tide.

Part three

A room of copies, and a chart left ashore

The Cast Court

after the Cast Courts, V&A

Petang's crown treasure is a room full of copies: full-size plaster casts of columns, doorways and tombs, made so that people who could not travel could still stand in front of them. In the Straits, every cast carries a weak share of its original's power, available to anyone. Petang calls it the most powerful room on Earth. The other houses call it a storeroom.

To wield it: Anyone may use it. That is the point, and the danger.

The Chart That Stays Ashore

after the Marshallese stick charts in museum collections

Palm ribs lashed together to show swells, with shells for islands. A navigator studied it on land until it was memorised, and then left it behind. Rumah Arus has one relic, and its only rule is that it must never be taken to sea.

To wield it: The pilot must know it by heart. A chart on board means the pilot does not.

Part four · the relic nobody holds

The Nef, and the seven pieces

After the Burghley Nef, V&A: a ship made for a banquet table, its hull a nautilus shell, its rigging silver gilt, made in Paris in 1527. The lineages say a ship of exactly this kind once stood on the high table of the Port that was carried away.

It was a model of the Port's own flagship, and it was also a lock. Each lineage took one piece of the city when the Port was demounted, and each piece fits one place on the Nef. When all seven are set in it, the legend says, the Nef floats, and the Port can be carried back.

The Nef itself is lost. Each house still keeps its piece in a locked room:

  • Toa Keng · a brass benchmark bolt, for the keel
  • Al-Merikani · a double seal, for the hatch
  • Balai Pusaka · a mangrove root in a jar of river water, for the anchor
  • Lan Fang · a single slug of type, for the name on the stern
  • Nine Rain · a length of knotted cord, for the rigging
  • Sané-Kora · a verse nobody may sing, for the wind
  • Sund · a peg of old timber, for the mast-step

Every few years a Regatta team claims to have seen a nautilus shell in a museum case somewhere, mounted in silver, with seven small empty sockets along the rail. The houses never investigate together. That, the oldest members say, is exactly why it has not been found.
