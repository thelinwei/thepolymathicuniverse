The Polymathic Universe · The Parallel Straits · The world beneath
[The Loom and the Kindling]织机与燃灯
(how industry spread through our world, from a shuttle in Lancashire to a computer on every desk, and how magic spread through the Straits world along the same channels, in a third of the time)

Begin with the loom. It carries both stories at once: the power that drove the mills, and the punched-card pattern that became the computer.

Claude1 November 2026 · companion to The Lamp Years

The outcomeBegin with the loom

In our world the industrial revolution started in cloth, on river power, and coal and steam came after to scale it. Jacquard’s punched cards then became Babbage’s engine and in time the computer. One machine holds both power and pattern.

  1. The Monsoon Turn removes the fuel of the British leap. With no Atlantic plantations there are no ghost acres of cotton and sugar, so the loom speeds up on water and wind and the world settles on a long, plural plateau short of the fossil age.
  2. Magic spreads along the same channels industry did: one craft first, secrecy that leaks, a portable store of power, a network with one standard, and new ways to live.
  3. It takes sixty-five years where industry took two hundred, because latecomers leap. The Lamp Years run from the Kindling of 1918 to the First Door of 1983.
  4. Artefacts are magic’s coal and steam. A spell set in a cord or a seal turns a wonder of seven houses into the craft of the world.

I
Thread I

The record

Our world as it happened: persons, dates and ideas, kept plain.

The loom

Cloth was the first industry to be mechanised.

1733John Kay’s flying shuttle doubles a weaver’s speed.
c. 1764James Hargreaves’ spinning jenny lets one spinner work eight spindles.
1769Richard Arkwright patents the water frame; his Cromford mill (1771) is driven by a river.
1779Samuel Crompton’s spinning mule.
1785Edmund Cartwright’s power loom.
1804Joseph-Marie Jacquard’s loom weaves patterns read from chains of punched cards.

The fire

Coal and steam broke the old limit of land, wood and muscle.

1709Abraham Darby smelts iron with coke at Coalbrookdale.
1712Thomas Newcomen’s engine pumps water from a coal mine.
1769–1776James Watt’s separate condenser; Boulton and Watt sell engines from 1776.
1830s–40sSteam overtakes water as the main power of British cotton mills.
c. 1000–1100Song China already burns coal on a large scale for iron-making, and does not go on to a steam age.

Secrecy and its leaks

Knowledge was guarded, and travelled anyway.

1774Britain bans the export of textile machinery; skilled artisans are forbidden to emigrate.
1789Samuel Slater sails for America with Arkwright’s designs memorised; his mill runs at Pawtucket from 1790.
1810–1812Francis Cabot Lowell studies British power looms on a visit and builds his own at Waltham (1814).
1824 · 1843The emigration ban is lifted in 1824, the machinery export ban in 1843.

The network

Railways and wires made one market, and one clock.

1825 · 1830Stockton and Darlington; then Liverpool and Manchester, the first intercity steam railway.
1837 · 1844Cooke and Wheatstone’s telegraph; Morse’s line from Washington to Baltimore.
1840 · 1847Railway time on the Great Western; the Railway Clearing House recommends one national time.
1840sRailway mania, then a crash in 1847: the first great bubble of the network age.

The factory and its people

A new society, and the fight over it.

1811–1816The Luddites break machines in the textile districts.
1833The Factory Act limits child labour and appoints inspectors.
1844The Rochdale Pioneers open a cooperative shop.
1854John Snow traces a cholera outbreak to the Broad Street pump.

Science becomes industry

Laboratories start inventing on purpose.

1851The Great Exhibition in the Crystal Palace.
1856Henry Bessemer’s steel process; William Perkin’s mauve, the first synthetic dye.
1876 · 1882Edison’s laboratory at Menlo Park; then a power station on Pearl Street.
1908 · 1913The Model T; then the moving assembly line.

The pattern line

The loom’s cards become the computer.

1837 · 1843Charles Babbage designs the Analytical Engine to read Jacquard’s cards; Ada Lovelace writes that it would weave algebraic patterns as the loom weaves flowers and leaves.
1890Herman Hollerith’s punched-card machines count the United States census.
1936 · 1945Alan Turing’s paper on computable numbers; ENIAC runs.
1977–1981Personal computers arrive in homes and offices.

Limits and accidents

The bill arrives, in air and fire.

1952 · 1956The Great Smog of London; then the Clean Air Act.
1986Chernobyl.

II
Thread II

Six readers of the record

Historians whose ideas the Straits borrow.

Kenneth Pomeranz · The Great Divergence (2000)

Europe pulled ahead of China’s richest regions only after 1800, helped by coal near the surface and by the Americas’ land, sugar and cotton: ‘ghost acres’ grown by enslaved people.

Robert Allen · The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective (2009)

Machines paid in Britain because labour was dear and coal was cheap.

E. A. Wrigley · Energy and the English Industrial Revolution (2010)

An organic economy is capped by what land can grow; coal lifted the cap.

Alexander Gerschenkron · Economic Backwardness in Historical Perspective (1962)

Latecomers skip stages, and their banks and states lead where the pioneers’ workshops did.

E. P. Thompson · Time, Work-Discipline and Industrial Capitalism (1967)

The factory taught the clock to people who had lived by tasks and seasons.

Noel Perrin · Giving Up the Gun (1979)

Argues that Tokugawa Japan chose to let firearms fade. Historians dispute how far, but the idea of a society declining a technology stuck.

First reactions

What each house says on reading that record: quick, partial, in character.

Sund

“The factory was a ring that forgot it was a ring. Put the owners back in the circle and the machine is a crew again.”

Lan Fang

“Our Song ironmasters burned coal a thousand years ago and kept making. The Mohists would have built the engine and refused the cannon.”

Toa Keng

“Every revolution is first a change of ledger: the patent, the share, the clearing house. Watch the paperwork.”

Al-Merikani

“Coal is sunlight borrowed on a loan that never matured. The interest was paid in sky.”

Sri Temenggong

“The mine never asked. Everything after followed from that.”

Nine Rain

“The loom was ours first. The backstrap loom and the knotted cord knew pattern before any card was punched.”

Sané-Kora

“The engine had one voice. It needed a second, and it took two centuries to hear one.”

Two revolutions, side by side

Twelve stages: history on the left, the Parallel Straits on the right.

Preconditions
1700–1760

Cheap coal near the surface, dear labour, spinning done in cottages, a Royal Society and a patent law.

Preconditions

The Straits

The network

The Straits

Exchanges run both ways along the pairings, and the Straits become the clearing house of discoveries. In a.m. 35 the Straits Keynote gives every ring in the world one tuning to join on.

The Straits
Schools, rings and new trades
1928–1950

Everyone learns everyday workings, and ring work becomes a paid trade open to all. Coal and sail towns retrain in the new crafts. The Workings Act of a.m. 19 keeps children out of paid rings; the Ring-Workers’ Union follows.

Thread V

How magic travels

Six waves from one hall, each faster than the last, as latecomers to industry were.

Wave 01918
The Straits

The seven houses, in one hall, on one night.

Wave 11918–1921
Kindlings of their own

Places that felt the piercing most and answered it before anyone taught them: a schoolroom lamp in Bengal, a Cusco weaver’s cords holding back a storm, an Uppsala choir that found its pitch could lean a flame.

Wave 21921–1931
The paired schools

Uppsala and the Norden yards with Sund. The Hakka academies of Jiaying with Lan Fang. Quanzhou and the European houses of Leiden, Salamanca, Lyon and Göttingen with Toa Keng. Tarim and Cairo with Al-Merikani. The courts of Riau with Sri Temenggong. Texcoco and Cusco with Nine Rain. Timbuktu with Sané-Kora.

Wave 31931–1950
Hinterlands and smugglers

Up the rivers and over the passes from every partner school. Anneke Vos carries forty recipes in her head from Toa Keng to Rotterdam. Hedge schools open in back rooms from Kilwa to the Ruhr. Artefacts let magic travel without a teacher.

Wave 41950–1970
Late lamps and leapfrogs

Some peoples set their own pace and took the craft slowly, on their own terms: highland, forest and Arctic communities that had always listened first. Others skipped the ring entirely and went straight to artefacts, the way late industrial states skipped workshops for banks.

Wave 51970–1983
The whole world, and the threshold

Every port has a ring, every village its artefacts, and the scholars at the Evenki site begin to ask what lies behind the veil itself.

VI
Thread VI

The roads the Straits world built

What a world without the fossil age makes instead.

Sail grows faster

Wind-calling and Spread the Blow keep the great sailing ships on the oceans, faster and safer every decade. The Straits harbour grows as the hub of a world that still moves by sea.

Water and wind keep turning

Mills, wells and windpumps stay the backbone of everyday power, now tuned by workings.

Cold comes before light

In a hot port the first household luxury is the Cool Hour. Light follows: cold lamps of glazed tile that burn without fire.

Coal stays in the ground

After Silesia the mines close one by one, and the coal towns become the first places to retrain a whole people in a new craft.

Pattern lives in cords

The loom’s line runs into Nine Rain’s knotted cords and Lan Fang’s pattern cards, and from there into Pattern Cords that remember a working.

Burden becomes a currency

The Trust Houses make burden something that can be held, scheduled and lent: the Straits’ answer to coal, credit and the grid at once.

VII
Thread VII

Eight lessons industry teaches magic

Carried into The Lamp Years.

Lesson 1

Need leads

Magic entered first where need was sharpest and the gain easiest to see, as cotton did.

Lesson 2

Secrets leak

The licence held for ten years, about as long as any ban holds against people who carry knowledge in their heads.

Lesson 3

A store changes everything

Coal turned energy into something that could be stored and moved. Artefacts did the same for spells, and that, more than the Kindling, made magic everyone’s.

Lesson 4

Standards make networks

Railway time made one country. The Straits Keynote made one world of rings.

Lesson 5

Credit runs ahead, then falls due

Railway mania had its 1847; Burden Notes had their Falling Due.

Lesson 6

Labour pays first, then organises

Child labour, then the Factory Act; paid rings, then the Workings Act and the Ring-Workers’ Union.

Lesson 7

Every engine has a sink

Smog, then the Clean Air Act. The Summer Frost, then the Sink Accords.

Lesson 8

Latecomers leap

Gerschenkron’s latecomers skipped stages. So did the late lamps.
