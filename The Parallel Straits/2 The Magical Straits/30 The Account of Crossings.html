What every grammar cannot see, what it costs a body to pierce two of them, where the crossers really come from, and the one thing no single mind is allowed to attempt.

Seven grammars laid side by side. A crosser is anyone who has gone straight down through more than one of them.

Part one

Who is good at what, honestly

The earlier atlases showed that every house can reach almost every power. That is true, and it makes for a world without drama. This chart says how each house reaches each power: natively, fluently, only at a heavy cost, or not at all.

Every house has at least one blind spot, and most have several powers they can only buy at a price. That is what makes crossers necessary, rivalries real, and every mixed team in the Straits worth watching.

Part two

What each grammar cannot see

A grammar is a way of seeing, and every way of seeing has an edge. These are the edges, and who stands just past each one.

Toa Keng

Anything that cannot be measured.

Grief, loyalty and the dead have no decimal places. A surveyor standing in a house of mourning sees a room of the correct dimensions.

Kora and Balai Pusaka can make a Toa Keng inspector look foolish in any case involving feeling or inheritance.

Al-Merikani

Anything that cannot be a party.

The sea signs nothing, the sky keeps no promise, and a person unconscious cannot consent. A trustee in a storm is only a passenger.

Nine Rain treats Merikani weather covenants as polite fiction, and is right.

Balai Pusaka

Anything new, and anywhere else.

A thing with no history offers nothing to commune with, and a practitioner away from her ground is ordinary.

Lan Fong constructs and Toa Keng benchmarks are the two things a Balai Pusaka elder cannot read.

Lan Fong

Anything that must stay as it is.

Every Lan Fong act is an edit. The house cannot preserve, and it cannot summon, because a spirit has no characters to set.

Balai Pusaka and Sané-Kora both keep what Lan Fong would improve, and neither forgives the improvement.

Nine Rain

Anything urgent.

The weave waits for the right day. In an emergency, a Nine Rain master can only tell you, very precisely, when help would have been cheap.

Sund and Lan Fong own every fast crisis; Nine Rain owns every slow one.

Sané-Kora

Anything with no voice.

Stone, iron, fire and machines have no account to hear. A Kora master can calm a riot and cannot move a pebble.

Lan Fong and Toa Keng rule the material world the Kora ear cannot reach.

Sund

Anything done alone, or in secret.

A ring cannot keep a secret and cannot act fast; it has to agree first. A single Sund caster is the weakest person in the room.

Every house that works alone, in private or at speed has the advantage over Sund until the load gets too heavy.

Part three

The twenty-one crossings, with their hands and their accounts

The Book of Crossings names the twenty-one dyadic orders and what each one discovered. Here is what each looks like when it casts, and what it costs. A crosser pays in both houses’ currencies at once, and the two prices do not add up. They compound.

Toa Keng × Al-Merikani

Juru-Wasīṭ

Covenant Translator

The hand
A chalk line snapped through a sealed palm. The line hangs in the air written in two scripts at once.
The account
Cold fingers and a hot lining together. Translators shiver and sweat in the same minute, and the ports call them the fevers.

Toa Keng × Balai Pusaka

Juru Selat

Strait Adept

The hand
A barefoot surveyor drawing lines along coasts that no longer exist, walking the old shore straight through modern buildings.
The account
They lose the present map. Old Juru Selat get lost between their house and the market and have to be led home by children.

Toa Keng × Lan Fong

通变师

Master of Penetrating Transformation

The hand
A chalk grid filled with cinnabar characters. Their spells look like tables, and read like proofs.
The account
The brass taste and the forgetting together. They lose numbers before characters, and the greatest of them count on their fingers.

Toa Keng × Nine Rain

Horizon-Keeper

Many-Sky Adept

The hand
The haze of possible futures woven into strands, and several futures held in the hands at once like threads.
The account
Insomnia, and the inability to commit. Horizon-Keepers are late to everything, because every future they arrive in is only one of several.

Toa Keng × Sané-Kora

The Fourth Listener

Erased-Viewpoint Seer

The hand
A single lamp and a single clap. Where the missing shadow and the missing echo coincide, a removed person is reconstructed.
The account
Slowly, they begin to lose their own. An old Fourth Listener casts one faint shadow and no echo at all.

Toa Keng × Sund

Lögbrú

Law-Bridge

The hand
Chalk lines strung from clasped forearm to clasped forearm. A ring agrees on a frame for one hour without agreeing on anything else.
The account
Bruises in exact geometric grids. The ring can read the meeting's minutes on each other's shoulders.

Al-Merikani × Balai Pusaka

Penjaga Amanah

Keeper of the Trust

The hand
A palm over the lining while standing barefoot. A title deed goes cold in the hand of an owner who has not kept the land.
The account
They carry the weight of land and trust together, and so can neither leave home nor own anything themselves.

Al-Merikani × Lan Fong

契符师

Covenant-Talisman Master

The hand
Contracts set in type and sealed in wax: talismans that execute their own conditions.
The account
A red stain that smells of oud. Every breach of every contract they ever wrote burns their fingertips, wherever it happens.

Al-Merikani × Nine Rain

Ayni Underwriter

Intergenerational Risk-Keeper

The hand
Knots tied in sealing wax. Weather debts insured across generations, with premiums paid in rain.
The account
They carry other people's arrears. Their shoulder bands grow heavier every year until they can barely lift their arms.

Al-Merikani × Sané-Kora

Name-Notary

Witness of Continuity

The hand
A name sung and then pressed with the double seal. The person named cannot be erased, possessed or replaced.
The account
They can never forget a name, and nobody can ever forget theirs. Name-Notaries do not sleep well, and cannot travel incognito.

Al-Merikani × Sund

Quorum Arbiter

Witness of Collective Legitimacy

The hand
Standing inside a ring with a hand on the lining. A consenting ring warms it; a coerced one burns.
The account
They feel every unwilling participant as a burn. An arbiter at a great public work comes home scarred.

Balai Pusaka × Lan Fong

Pusaka Engineer

Engineer of Maturing Things

The hand
Tools set once in type and then worn by a family for generations until they gather tuah. Machines that grow wiser with use.
The account
The house's two prices at once: bound to the ground, and forgetting. Their tools end up remembering them better than they remember themselves.

Balai Pusaka × Nine Rain

Penjaga Aliran

Keeper of the Flow

The hand
Barefoot in a river, knotting the current. A place is restored as a moving pattern, never a frozen past.
The account
They age with the seasons. A Penjaga Aliran looks visibly younger in the rains and older in the drought.

Balai Pusaka × Sané-Kora

Saksi Tanah

Witness of the Land

The hand
Kneeling, one palm on the soil, singing low. The ground's testimony becomes audible and admissible.
The account
They hear every site they stand on. Cities are unbearable, so most of them live in forests and come to court by boat.

Balai Pusaka × Sund

Penjaga Bersama

Shared Keeper

The hand
A barefoot ring on kampung soil. A place grows sturdier with every hand that maintains it.
The account
Their roots spread to everyone in the ring, so a whole neighbourhood finds it hard to move away.

Lan Fong × Nine Rain

Cycle Engineer

Living-System Master

The hand
Characters knotted into cord. Spells that revise themselves every season, in answer to how the world responded.
The account
Their spells outlive their intentions and keep changing. A Cycle Engineer must proofread every casting she ever made, once a year, for life.

Lan Fong × Sané-Kora

Namewright

Engineer of Personhood

The hand
A construct's name set in type and then sung. The result can say I, and remembers being made.
The account
Every construct takes a character from its maker's memory. The great Namewrights ended their lives unable to write their own names.

Lan Fong × Sund

Commons Engineer

Civic Systems Mage

The hand
A ballot and a ring together. The users of a machine become working parts of it.
The account
Bruises shaped like characters. Families of Commons Engineers can read a public works budget on their grandmothers' shoulders.

Nine Rain × Sané-Kora

Keeper of the Missing Season

Ecological-Cultural Seer

The hand
Listening for the rain that did not come, and for the silence where a bird should have answered.
The account
Grief for things that never happened. They mourn harvests nobody else knows were lost.

Nine Rain × Sund

Ayni Steward

Civilisational Reciprocity Keeper

The hand
A ring holding the warp of a monsoon, sharing a season across a whole population.
The account
The whole ring's thirst. Entire districts find themselves drinking water at the same hour.

Sané-Kora × Sund

Chorus-Lawspeaker

Plurality Binder

The hand
A ring singing in deliberate dissonance, every contradiction held. Decisions are reached without anyone agreeing.
The account
The ring's ears ring in a chord. Old lawspeakers can tell who was in their assembly by the interval.

Part four

What piercing feels like

Everyone in the Straits agrees that studying two houses does not make a crosser. Nobody agrees what does. The crossers themselves describe the same four stages.

The double bill
The student casts in one grammar and pays in the other’s currency. A Toa Keng surveyor’s lining goes warm; a Sund joiner’s ears ring. It is terrifying, and most students stop here and choose one house for good.
The mistranslation
For a season, nothing works. Each grammar reads the other’s casting as an error and cancels it. Crossers call this the deaf year. Houses call it failure, and many withdraw their scholarships.
The floor
One day the student reaches the relation underneath both grammars and finds it has no name in either. This is the piercing. The student usually says nothing about it for months, because there is nobody to tell.
The disowning
Both parent houses notice the new operation, and both object. No house wants a practitioner who can prove its grammar is a surface. Every crosser in the Book was disowned at least once, and most were readmitted only after a crisis made them indispensable.

There is a residue test for crossers, and investigators use it at every scene. A single grammar leaves one kind of trace. A crosser leaves two traces that do not agree: chalk dust that smells of oud, a missing echo exactly where a double shadow falls. When the traces disagree, the Board sends for someone senior.

Part five

Where crossers really come from

The official histories say crossers are trained at the great houses. Look at the Book’s biographies and a different pattern appears: most of the famous ones were never properly inside any house at all.

Sekolah Petang · the stencil nursery

Petang students copy castings from every house onto duplicator sheets, badly, without the grammar that came with them. A stencil does not know which house it belongs to. So Petang children grow up casting across all seven before anyone tells them that is supposed to be impossible, and a startling number of Straitwalkers first cast from a purple smudge. The houses call Petang magic crude. The Book of Crossings calls it the nursery.

Rumah Arus · the school under the schools

Arus holds no relation of its own; it reads what is already moving. Which means an Arus pilot learns, from childhood, to feel the current beneath every grammar rather than the grammar itself. Of the twenty or so Deepwalkers in recorded history, the Book lists four as Arus-trained, and suspects several more. When the school was struck off, the city lost its only formal training for the thing every house most fears.

Part six

Concordance

The Book of Crossings says that Concordance is not the highest rank, but a different category, and then declines to explain. This is the explanation.

Concordance is a relation that several minds from several grammars can hold together and that no single mind can hold at all. Its account belongs to no caster. It is owed by the Concordance itself.

That last clause is why the houses fear it. An account that belongs to nobody does not disappear; it persists, and a relation held long enough with its own account begins to behave like a person. Al-Merikani learned this with its old contracts, which is where the Straits’ djinn come from. A Concordance is the same thing at the scale of a city.

There are three generally accepted Concordances.

The Port
legendary
The lost capital that all seven lineages built together and then carried away. If the legends are right, the Port was never merely a city. It was a Concordance with its own account, and it is still owed. That would explain why every house keeps one piece of it in a locked room, and why none of them will let the others hold theirs.
The Night of Two Tides
Year 307
Forty-seven districts, pumps and pylons and neighbourhood wards, held together as one system. The Book credits the Commons Engineers. The survivors credit something that answered when the districts called, which none of them could describe afterwards, and which has not answered since.
The Unregistered Return
1917
The one resurrection in the Straits, performed by all seven houses in one night for a woman nobody will name. It worked. What came back was continuous in every way the Continuity Case would later require. The account for it is the snarled cord in Nine Rain’s sealed room, and it has never been reconciled. The houses do not discuss who, or what, is still paying.

The prohibition, and the glued joint

Scholars insist that a Deepwalker is not Concordant, and most students think this is a point of taxonomy. It is a warning. A Deepwalker, one mind that sees beneath four or more grammars, is the one kind of person who could try to hold a Concordance alone, and the attempt has only one outcome: a relation held by a single mind with no one to share its account can never be let go of again.

Sund has a word for a joint that can never be taken apart. The glued joint somewhere in the city that no one at Sund will locate is not a piece of bad carpentry. It is the one time a Deepwalker tried.

Part seven

A calendar for the crossings

The Book of Crossings dates its crises from a Year 0 it never names. The lineages count from the same event, and they call it the Reckoning of the Carrying: the night the Port was taken apart.

The outside world remembers that night as the great raid of 1025, when a fleet from the Coromandel sacked the old maritime empire and its capital was never found again. The lineages know better. The raid came, and the Port was already gone.

Part eight · a first piercing

The Stencil Press

Sekolah Petang, the evening shift, a Tuesday.

Nur Hidayah was fourteen and on duplicator duty, which meant cranking the drum of the old spirit duplicator in the back room while the evening shift did arithmetic next door. The purple ink got into everything. She had stopped noticing it on her hands.

The sheet on the drum was a cantrip copied from a copy of a copy: a Toa Keng line for keeping a table level, which the canteen used under its wobbly legs. Someone had written the Kora word for listen in the margin, because the canteen auntie was going deaf and the girls wrote her notes on whatever paper was lying about.

Hidayah cranked the drum and the sheet came out warm, and the back room went silent.

It was not quiet. It was silent in the exact shape of a person. The echo of the drum came back from every wall except one corner by the filing cabinet, where it simply did not return, and on the floor under the lamp, in that same corner, there was a second shadow with nobody to cast it.

She knew what that meant. Every child in the Straits knows the residue table the way other children know their times tables. A double shadow is Toa Keng. A missing echo is Kora. The two together in the same place mean someone has been taken out of the room.

· · ·

It took the Board three weeks to establish who. A night-school teacher who had run the back room in the 1970s had been quietly erased from the school’s records after a dispute with an inspector: removed from the rolls, the photographs and the memory of everyone who had worked with her. Hidayah’s stencil had found her outline. Nobody had asked it to.

Two houses wrote to the school in the same week. Toa Keng offered a scholarship, on condition that Hidayah never again cast with a Kora word in the margin. Sané-Kora offered a place, on condition that she give up the chalk line. Both letters described her as promising and both described what she had done as an error.

Hidayah read them in the back room, with purple hands. Then she put both letters on the drum face down, cranked out forty copies of the canteen table-leveller for the evening shift, with listen in the margin, and went next door to do her arithmetic.

The teacher’s name has since been restored to the school’s honours board. It was the first thing Hidayah proofread every evening that year.
