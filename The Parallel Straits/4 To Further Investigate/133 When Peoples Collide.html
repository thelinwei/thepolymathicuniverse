Part I / The names
01 / THE FIRST ENCOUNTER

An unexpected doorway into Inner Asia

It began with a seemingly modest question: What can you tell me about Kyrgyz people? A people of mountain pastures, portable homes, horse games and enormous oral epics entered the conversation. There were the seasonal movements between lowland and jailoo, the woven intelligence of felt, and the manaschy who carried an epic through voice and memory.

The Manas trilogy recounts the unification of scattered tribes across three generations. Its storytellers perform with changing rhythm, tone and gesture, and, according to the tradition documented by UNESCO, may understand their vocation as arriving in a dream. The story's transmission is a social activity: a living person holds and renews a shared past. [1]

There is already a useful paradox here. The Kyrgyz may appear to an outside historian as a people who migrated and transformed over centuries. Yet a tradition centred on unification repeatedly teaches its audiences how to imagine themselves as one people.

In the margin · Something that would return later

The endurance of the Manas tradition offers one possible mechanism for the survival of collective identity: stories can keep relationships imaginable across shifts in territory, government and population. This is an interpretation, rather than proof that the epic preserved an unchanged ethnic identity over two millennia.

But the questioner had been thinking about Chinese history. One unfamiliar people opened a much larger puzzle.

“Kitan, tangut, Mongols, manchus etc etc. did these people have different identities? Or did some lineages change their names over time?”
From the conversation

It was the kind of question produced by a familiar historical narrative: first the Xiongnu, then the Xianbei, the Turks, the Khitan, the Jurchens, the Mongols, the Manchus. Names enter the historical stage, fight Chinese dynasties, sometimes conquer them, and later seem to disappear. It resembles a relay race in which each northern people hands its place to the next.

But the relay race is a trick of the narrative. These peoples were not lined up waiting for their turn. They lived across overlapping lands and eras. Some spoke related languages; others did not. Some were dynastic houses; others were broad coalitions. Some were descendants of earlier groups, while others adopted, absorbed or displaced them.

02 / THE VIEW FROM THE FRONTIER

The peoples beyond the wall

The striking realization was that “the northern nomads” described a relationship to the Chinese imperial frontier, rather than a single ancestral people. It grouped together forest peoples, mobile pastoralists, agricultural communities, traders, military coalitions and imperial administrators.

First, look at the land. The great belt of grasslands runs from the western Eurasian plains through Kazakhstan toward Mongolia and northeastern Asia. Rivers, mountain passes, forest margins and oasis corridors connect it to still other worlds. The green has no allegiance to the borders drawn across it today.

The map offers a different starting point from a Chinese dynastic chronology. The names Xiongnu, Göktürk, Uyghur or Mongol arose within an interlinked region that cut across what we now call countries. The old Yenisei Kyrgyz world lay far north of the modern Kyrgyz heartland in the Tian Shan; their story cannot be contained by Kyrgyzstan's modern outline. The steppe helped connect these histories, but it never made its inhabitants a single people.

And by around 1100 CE, the northern Chinese world contained several rival states at once: the Khitan-led Liao; the Tangut-led Western Xia; the Song; and Jurchen groups on the verge of establishing the Jin. These were concurrent political actors, each with different languages, institutions and histories.

The most revealing comparison was linguistic. Khitan was closely related to the Mongolic world, commonly described as para-Mongolic. Jurchen and later Manchu were Tungusic. Tangut was Sino-Tibetan. Turkic-speaking peoples included the Göktürks, Uyghurs and Kyrgyz. The linguistic affiliation of the Xiongnu remains disputed, in part because the evidence is fragmentary. [2]

The distinction matters: a political succession does not establish linguistic descent, and linguistic kinship does not establish membership in the same polity.

03 / FROM FAMILY TREE TO WEB

A tree that became a web

The request was to draw a map of ancestry and divergence. At first, a family tree seemed the natural answer. Then the categories began to separate.

The Khitan example is especially arresting. After the Jurchens overthrew the Liao, a Khitan ruling group moved west and founded the Qara Khitai, or Western Liao, in Central Asia. Their political tradition had migrated hundreds of kilometres into another cultural environment. [3]

The Jurchen-to-Manchu story was different. In 1635, Hong Taiji formally imposed the designation Manchu on his community, an identity already being moulded through political centralisation and the banner system. The change involved deliberate institutional and symbolic work. [4]

And the rise of the Göktürks after the defeat of the Rouran, followed later by Uyghur and Kyrgyz victories, described changes in power among overlapping steppe populations, not a straightforward biological genealogy.

The names in a chronicle are stable nouns. The societies behind them were verbs.
Part II / The evidence
04 / THE DEAD ENTER THE DISCUSSION

The genomes enter

Then ancient DNA made the old picture harder to sustain. The remains of people buried many centuries ago began to reveal the complex population histories beneath familiar imperial names.

2020 · 214 ancient individuals

A 6,000-year genetic study of the eastern steppe found that the Xiongnu formed through mixing among previously differentiated populations and migrants from surrounding regions. The later Mongol-period populations had a different ancestry profile. [5]

2022 · Avars across Eurasia

Genome research connected elite Avar-period burials in Europe's Carpathian Basin to Northeast Asian steppe populations, with particularly close comparisons to a Rouran-period genome. A political claim of migration could now be examined through biological evidence. [6]

2023 · Inside Xiongnu cemeteries

Researchers examined 18 people from two frontier burial communities and found pronounced genetic variation, sometimes within extended families. In those samples, ancestry profiles also differed by burial status. [7]

2024 · A Xianbei-led emperor

The genome of Yuwen Yong of Northern Zhou showed both Northeast Asian-related and Yellow River farmer-related ancestry. One ruler's body contained evidence consistent with generations of marriage across political and regional lines. [8]

2025 · Xiongnu and European Huns

Shared long DNA segments linked some Xiongnu-era elite individuals to later people in the Carpathian Basin, supporting direct ancestry for certain lineages. The wider Hun-period population remained heterogeneous. [9]

The most important revelation is not a single newly discovered ancestor. It is the separation of three processes historians had long been tempted to equate: genes can travel; languages can spread; identities can be adopted. They intersect, but rarely describe one simple motion.

Research margin · What ancient DNA actually establishes

Genetic studies compare sampled individuals with reference populations using statistical models. An ancestry proportion is an inference under those models, not a percentage of membership in a historical ethnicity. A few elite graves may be unrepresentative of the population they governed. Shared ancestry can establish biological connections while leaving questions about language, self-identification, religion and politics unanswered.

These studies put a remarkable kind of pressure on written histories. Where an imperial chronicle might present the Xiongnu as a single political subject, excavated graves reveal individuals with different ancestral histories living within that one imperial system.

05 / THE FIRST NAME RETURNS

Back to the Kyrgyz

The Kyrgyz had started the inquiry, and genetics returned us to them. Modern Kyrgyz communities inhabit the Tian Shan and adjoining regions, while historical references to the Yenisei Kyrgyz locate important earlier traditions much farther north. The relationship between ancient and modern communities involves migration, mixture and debated processes of ethnogenesis.

A 2025 genomic study of Kyrgyz populations in Xinjiang and Kyrgyzstan modelled several ancestry components and inferred multiple phases of mixture across the last two millennia. The precise percentages belonged to its samples and modelling choices; the larger point was a population history shaped by repeated contacts across Inner Asia. [10]

Yet the name endures.

The contrast is striking. The Xiongnu imperial identity eventually ceased to designate a living state. The Khitan name survived in transformed forms such as Cathay, even as its political world dissolved. The Mongols remained Mongols after their empire fragmented. The name Manchu was intentionally consolidated in the seventeenth century. And the Kyrgyz carried an old name into very different historical circumstances.

What exactly survives when a people survives? A bloodline? A common language? An institution? A story? A name others continue to use? Or some changing configuration of all of them?

This was the moment the conversation ceased to be only a historical inquiry.

Part III / The reaction
06 / THE IDEA ARRIVES

“This reminds me of molecule collisions.”

“Peoples as molecules. Sometimes new ones emerge, sometimes they reduce. Certain peoples aggregate to form powerful polities. They multiple and collide with others. Sometimes they encounter a threat or catalyst and transform.”
The user's observation, as written in the conversation

This was the intellectual pivot. The original problem had been genealogical: who descended from whom? The new problem was kinetic: under what conditions do populations combine, separate, reorganise, and produce unexpected political formations?

A family tree tracks descent. A reaction diagram makes room for encounters. It gives us attraction and repulsion, instability and durability, combinations that never existed before, and the possibility that what appears to disappear has instead changed form.

A clan might merge into a confederation. A defeated ruling family might relocate and build another state. A conquered population might remain in place while adopting a new identity. A dynasty might fragment into rival successor polities. A shared enemy might bind old rivals together.

Our first response pursued the analogy through bonds and catalysts. But then the discussion found a more unsettling possibility: perhaps the most dramatic historical transformation does not require a dramatically different population. Perhaps the arrangement itself can change everything.

That took the conversation from reactions between substances to the states of matter.

The real discovery was a shift from “Who were they?” to “What made them become what they became?”
07 / A CHANGE OF STATE

The same people, another state of matter

Water, ice and steam contain the same H₂O molecules, yet behave differently because of how those molecules are organised and interact. Could something comparable happen to human societies? This was the unexpected analogy that our first conversation brought forward: phase transitions.

Imagine a population whose households, languages and inherited relationships remain broadly continuous. In one period its alliances are scattered and local. In another, those alliances form a wide-ranging confederation. Later, authority may become concentrated through administrators, military commands, taxation and court institutions. The population need not be replaced for its collective capacities to change dramatically.

“A society can undergo a radical transformation without experiencing an equally radical change in its population.”
From our earlier exchange

Think of the Mongolian Plateau before and after Temüjin's rise: military affiliation, obligations and the ability to coordinate action were transformed through institutional reorganisation. Then think of the later Mongol successor polities. An empire could fragment without every family, language or practice disappearing with its political centre.

There is a further variation that chemistry suggests: graphite and diamond consist of carbon atoms with different arrangements and properties. The analogy alerts us to the importance of structure. It also has limits: societies possess overlapping loyalties, unequal power and deliberate choices; there is no universal social equivalent of temperature at which a tribe becomes a state.

Nor must these states follow one another. A centralised monarchy can coexist with loosely federated communities; one institution may tighten its bonds while another loosens them. The point is the potential for qualitatively different forms of collective organisation among substantially continuous populations.

The historical implication

The composition of a society and the structure of its relationships are different variables. Political transformation can be swift, while demographic change is slow; equally, a state may keep its name while its population changes. This distinction prepared the ground for Gemini's more elaborate chemistry.

08 / A SECOND INTELLIGENCE ENTERS

Gemini puts the metaphor in a laboratory

Then six screenshots arrived from Gemini. Another intelligence had taken the same observation and built a much more detailed chemical vocabulary around it: polymerisation, activation energy, catalysts, enzymes, depolymerisation, displacement, allotropic transformation and conservation of mass.

Of all these additions, polymerisation was the most immediately useful. It explained how the dispersed social networks we had been discussing might become capable of acting as one enormous political formation.

How dispersed communities can become an empire

Gemini began with the economics of pastoral movement. Herds need grazing land; grass changes with the seasons; keeping every household and animal in one place can be costly. Camps and local communities therefore move across broad pasturelands. Yet distance need not mean isolation: marriage, trade, kinship, patronage and periodic assemblies maintain relationships across that space.

In its analogy, small social units become monomers: mobile constituents capable of linking into a larger structure. Under particular conditions—an external threat, military victories, promises of booty, a prestigious ruling house or shared sacred legitimacy—those links multiply. The result is a polymer: a confederation capable of mustering resources and projecting power far beyond any single constituent community. Gemini described a khan as supplying the necessary “binding energy.”

The terms illuminate the process, but the scale of the units matters. A household, a patrilineal lineage and a large political community are different entities. The Borjigin were a lineage, while the Kereits, Naimans and Tatars were substantial political groups in Temüjin's world. “Monomer” cannot mean all of them at once.

The real transformation happens inside the polymer

Temüjin provides a particularly revealing historical case. His achievement involved more than aligning existing clans behind one leader. The Mongol polity reorganised warriors and households into military-administrative units of tens, hundreds, thousands and ten-thousands. Command could cut across older allegiances, and leaders were appointed through a new imperial hierarchy. The resulting structure partially supplanted earlier tribal affiliations, even as families and their descendants continued to exist. [13]

In other words, the constituents did more than stick together. Their relationships were transformed by belonging to the larger structure. The polymer itself changed the properties of the monomers. This is precisely where your earlier intuition about phase transitions and Gemini's notion of polymerisation meet.

And what happens when the new structure fractures? Gemini proposed depolymerisation. It invoked the Mongol Empire's emergence into the Yuan, Golden Horde, Ilkhanate and Chagatai Khanate as an example. The historical process was contested and gradual, involving dynastic succession, regional administration and inter-Mongol warfare. These successor states retained relationships and institutional inheritances, rather than simply reverting to the original tribes. [14]

Activation energy, shocks and political catalysts

The next part of Gemini's answer explored what changes the likelihood or speed of aggregation. It presented a powerful agrarian neighbour as a catalyst: the resources and potential threats of a unified China might encourage steppe communities to negotiate, raid or unite on a comparable scale. It compared droughts and disastrous winters (dzud) to abrupt environmental pressure, and charismatic leaders such as Modu Chanyu, Bumin Qaghan, Temüjin and Nurhaci to enzymes that redirect a reaction.

These are evocative comparisons, rather than literal chemical mechanisms. Harsh winters can generate migration, cooperation or conflict, depending on the circumstances. And a successful leader is not a detached catalyst: leaders recruit supporters, distribute resources, reshape institutions and become entangled in the political order they help create. This is why the mirror-empires theory, which we would encounter next, proved so consequential.

When a polity disappears, what remains?

Gemini also supplied the idea of conservation of mass. Chinese historical narratives may cease to mention a polity under its former name; that does not mean its inhabitants evaporated. After the Liao fell, Khitan populations followed different trajectories: some continued under new rulers, others became connected to the Qara Khitai in Central Asia, and many were gradually incorporated into other societies. The Kyrgyz, meanwhile, carried an old name across long periods of change in territory and ancestry.

The metaphor is especially provocative when it distinguishes political disappearance from demographic disappearance. It becomes misleading if treated as a conservation law. Populations die, grow, migrate and intermarry; names can survive when many ancestral lines change. The more defensible claim is that the end of one collective identity does not imply the extinction of all the people who once lived under it.

And the reaction types—synthesis, decomposition, displacement and structural reorganisation—had already appeared in our first diagram. Gemini gave them a more systematic language. Our discussion, in turn, asked how far such equations can explain history before they begin disguising its complexities.

The comparison did something more useful than simply accepting or rejecting Gemini's equations. It forced us to ask which bonds connect people, which pressures transform those bonds, and which parts of a society survive a change in its political form.

09 / THE SYSTEM REACTS TO ITSELF

The empires looking into a mirror

Gemini's references to Thomas Barfield and Peter Turchin introduced a deeper historical connection. Barfield argued that pastoral empires on China's frontier often developed in intimate economic and political relationship with the Chinese imperial state. He called them shadow empires. Their ability to extract wealth through diplomacy, trade, tribute and raiding affected their cohesion. [11]

Turchin's mirror-empires model proposed a stronger feedback dynamic: competition across a pastoral–agrarian frontier could push both powers to increase their size and military capacity. He used the term autocatalytic for the reciprocal escalation. [12]

Here the chemical metaphor gains a new dimension. Instead of China being an external catalyst working upon inert steppe peoples, both neighbours alter one another's conditions. Each may become both reactant and catalyst in the other's transformation.

Ecology complicates the picture further. Pasture, water, severe winters and animal health shape the possibilities of mobile pastoralism. Climate shocks may produce migration, scarcity and warfare, but their consequences vary with institutions, contingency and human response. A colder year is not a direct equation for an invasion.

A productive intellectual disagreement

Barfield's shadow-empires argument and Turchin's mirror-empires model are explanatory theories, not settled descriptions of every Asian empire. They are most useful when treated as competing or complementary hypotheses that can be tested against particular historical cases.

Part IV / The element that remembers
10 / WHAT CHEMISTRY CANNOT CONTAIN

The element that remembers

At the heart of this entire exchange is a difference between matter and people. A molecule does not interpret its own bonds. A person can. Humans can declare loyalty, renounce an inherited identity, invent a new name, learn a language, build institutions or tell a story about the origins of their community.

The Manchu case offers a clear instance of political naming: a state shaped a collective category and sought to make it durable. The Kyrgyz Manas tradition offers another kind of persistence, in which an epic is repeatedly narrated and inherited. The Khitan migration westward shows political structures travelling into a new environment. These are different mechanisms of identity-making.

Identity is thus both a result of historical events and a force acting upon later events. A remembered betrayal may alter an alliance. A revered founder can lend authority to a new ruler. An institution may preserve cooperation after the leader who established it has died.

Read the same history through four lenses
Who moved, married, had children, and survived? Ancient DNA can illuminate parts of this, especially where historical records are thin.
Which languages were spoken, learned, spread, or abandoned? A language may pass between groups without a wholesale population replacement.
Who claimed authority, mobilised resources, made alliances, and founded institutions? Dynasties can emerge or disappear faster than populations.
Which names, epics, loyalties and origin stories remained compelling? These can survive political ruptures and shape the next round of collective decisions.

In the previous exchange, this generated the phrase self-modifying chemistry. A compelling metaphor, if held lightly: societies can alter the rules by which their members relate, and can collectively reinterpret what those relationships mean.

There may be no single conserved ethnic essence beneath every historical transformation. Yet continuities persist: in families, in languages, in places, in objects, in legal systems, and in stories told to children.

A political formation may disintegrate. Its institutions, descendants and memories may have entirely different afterlives.

11 / RICH SOIL

What this opens for PMU

The original curiosity was historical, but the conceptual harvest reaches much further. The Polymathic Universe is already concerned with schools, exchange, treaties, languages of knowledge, social authority and the responsibilities that accompany unusual powers. Here is a new way to ask how a polity comes into being.

Perhaps its decisive strength is neither territory nor a common ancestry, but the density of relationships between people who remain different. What bonds are strong enough to endure a succession crisis? What institutions reduce the effort of cooperation? What happens when an unexpected discovery suddenly makes previously distant communities interdependent?

Magic, in this setting, could amplify some bonds while making others more precarious. A shared spell might require agreement across multiple traditions. An exchange institution might begin as a temporary alliance and acquire a life of its own. A catastrophe might force rivals into a common undertaking; the resulting institution could survive long after the catastrophe has been forgotten.

And a political name might endure while its membership continually changes. That is particularly intriguing in a world where knowledge travels through teachers, apprentices, travellers, envoys, performers and schools.

Uncommitted possibilities · For later PMU exploration

Bonding: What makes communities willing to share rare knowledge?

Activation: What discovery or crisis allows a new federation to form?

Memory: Can a ritual preserve an alliance after every original participant has died?

Mutation: When does a school, guild or diplomatic network grow into a new political identity?

Afterlife: What survives when the institution itself collapses?

There is no reason to force any of these immediately into the established world. Their usefulness is that they change the questions we will ask while making it.

12 / STILL UNFINISHED

The questions left alive

The conversation ended with another possible encounter: Ibn Khaldun and his notion of ʿasabiyyah, or social solidarity. Centuries before contemporary complexity theory, he explored how group cohesion contributes to dynastic power and how that cohesion can later weaken. It is a promising future companion to Barfield, Turchin and the steppe histories, though that comparison still deserves its own careful investigation.

And other questions remain more unsettling. Why do some names remain alive for thousands of years? Can an empire develop its own identity independently of the peoples who compose it? How quickly can a social bond be created, and how long can its memory last? Can knowledge-sharing produce a polity without military conquest? When does aggregation become domination rather than cooperation?

The molecular analogy has given us a grammar, not yet a theory. To become a theory it would need clearer units, variables, mechanisms and cases that might disprove it. But as a research instrument, it has already done something valuable: it has allowed apparently unrelated histories to speak to one another.

A question about the Kyrgyz led to the Xiongnu. The Xiongnu led to genomes. Genomes led to collisions. Collisions led to a chemistry of power. And power led back to the stubborn mystery of human memory.

We began by looking for ancestors. We found processes that produce new possibilities.

There is more to investigate. That is precisely why this ground is fertile.

When a name disappears from the historical record, what exactly has vanished?

Three distinct histories

Genetic descent tracks relatives and ancestral populations.

Political formation tracks authority and institutions.

Identity tracks how communities recognise and narrate themselves.
