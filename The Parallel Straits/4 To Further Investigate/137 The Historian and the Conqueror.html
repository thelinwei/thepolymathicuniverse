An inquiry into the chemistry of peoples

The Historian
and the Conqueror

A journalistic research journey through Damascus, the Muqaddimah and the chemistry of peoples.

From the Kyrgyz to Damascus, and from one extraordinary encounter to a science of human association.
Journalistic research documentation · 16 November 2026

The conqueror had been asking about him. The judges were arguing over surrender. And somewhere beyond Damascus's walls, the old historian's account of how empires acquire power was about to become a matter of personal survival.

01 / The city without its sultan

The army departed in the night.

In late 1400 the Mamluk sultan al-Nāṣir Faraj moved north from Egypt toward Damascus. Timur's campaigns had already scarred cities across western Asia. A second army stood beyond Damascus: one belonging to a conqueror whose authority reached across much of Central Asia, Iran and beyond.

Ibn Khaldun had been summoned into the expedition while out of judicial office. His autobiography describes his reluctance, followed by his agreement when the sultan's marshal insisted. Once in Damascus, the armies watched each other for weeks, sometimes fighting. On 7 January 1401 Faraj and the leading commanders left at night, fearing conspiracy in Egypt. Much of the retinue and many officials remained behind. ↗ Eyewitness ↗ Mongols

The scene

The bewilderment began the following morning. Judges assembled at the ʿĀdiliyya madrasa, where Ibn Khaldun was staying. They hoped for a pledge of security from Timur. The citadel commander objected. Religious and civic notables nonetheless went out to negotiate; they returned with promises and the expectation that Damascus would open its gates. Some inhabitants disputed the bargain.

Ibn Khaldun had heard that Timur wanted to meet him. By dawn he had also heard enough of the dispute to fear for his life.

The details matter. The scholar was neither simply summoned under guard nor dispatched on a cleanly authorised peace mission. According to his own account, he asked the judges for permission to leave; they initially refused. They eventually lowered him over the wall. There he found Timur's deputy, Shah Malik, who offered him a mount and an escort. ↗ Eyewitness

Interpretive illustration of the crossing. The account specifies descent from the wall, but not its exact physical mechanism; no basket is required by the testimony.
02 / Inside the tent

Food, translation, and questions.

Timur was reclining on one elbow, distributing dishes to groups of Mongol soldiers seated before the tent. The historian greeted him, made a sign of humility, and kissed the extended hand. Timur indicated a place to sit. Then he summoned a Hanafi jurist from Khwarazm, ʿAbd al-Jabbār ibn al-Nuʿmān, to interpret. ↗ Eyewitness

The historical scene was a meeting of several intellectual and political geographies. A Tunis-born Maliki scholar, a Turco-Mongol ruler from Transoxiana, a Khwarazmian interpreter, Syrian judges, Chaghatay commanders, and Egyptian political interests all occupied the same space.

Timur began by asking about Ibn Khaldun's homeland and career. Their conversation moved through the Maghreb: Tangier, Ceuta, Fez, Sijilmasa, the Strait of Gibraltar, the boundaries of settled territory and desert. Ibn Khaldun described the varying meanings of near, middle and far Maghreb. The emperor wanted something more exhaustive.

He wanted the region described so thoroughly that he might understand its distant geography as if he had seen it himself.

In the days that followed, Ibn Khaldun produced a substantial account of its rivers, mountains, settlements and territories. Timur instructed his secretary to translate it into Mongolian. A historian had become a provider of operational geographical knowledge to a conquering power. We cannot establish from this request alone whether Timur planned a western campaign, but the information certainly had political value. ↗ Eyewitness ↗ Lawrence

One domestic detail grounds the entire scene: a dish called rishta was offered. Ibn Khaldun ate it and reported that his evident enjoyment pleased the host. The hospitality of a ruler and the danger of his army occupied the same room.

03 / The science of flattery

He says he has awaited Timur for forty years.

Another judge had been captured and held for ransom. Ibn Khaldun, fearing that his own welcome might suddenly vanish, decided to speak in a way that would win Timur's favour. He tells us this plainly in his account. The rhetoric that followed was deliberate diplomacy. ↗ Eyewitness

He recounted conversations from decades earlier with an astrologer and others in North Africa who had spoken of a world-changing ruler appearing from the northeast. He mentioned predictions associated with a conjunction of planets and the expectations of religious thinkers. He then connected this to his own account of political power.

In Ibn Khaldun's argument, ʿaṣabiyyah—the solidarity of a group—made collective sovereignty possible. Great concentrations of support allowed rulers to project power over great distances. In Timur's military following, the historian professed to see a solidarity unparalleled among earlier kingdoms.

Reading the evidence

His autobiographical account explicitly connects his praise to fear and a desire to win favour. It establishes that he used astrology and group-solidarity theory in the conversation; it does not establish that he regarded every prediction as literal or that his praise offers a detached judgement of Timur.

The daring move is intellectually revealing. Ibn Khaldun makes the conqueror the apparent living vindication of his theory. He offers an explanation of why Timur is powerful—and, by giving it, attempts to influence how Timur treats him.

04 / A disagreement over kings

Who gets to inherit the past?

Timur was no passive recipient of admiration. Their talk turned to the rank and lineage of ancient kings: Alexander, Caesar, Khusraw, Nebuchadnezzar. When Ibn Khaldun placed Nebuchadnezzar among them, Timur questioned the classification. They argued about whether he belonged to Babylonian or early Persian history. Timur then asserted an ancestral connection, on the maternal side, with the legendary Persian king Manushihr. ↗ Eyewitness

Ibn Khaldun appealed to al-Ṭabarī, the respected historian of the early Islamic centuries. Timur did not accept that authority as decisive. He proposed bringing Arab and Persian histories for comparison and debate.

This is a fascinating confrontation between two ways of authorising historical truth. The historian appealed to a revered chronicler. The conqueror advanced competing traditions and offered to assemble texts. Neither man's position was simply innocent of politics: the classification of ancient kings could also reinforce contemporary claims to sovereign legitimacy.

Timur also stressed that he ruled as a deputy of a Chinggisid khan. In post-Mongol Central Asia, a conqueror without direct descent from Genghis Khan could wield effective control while invoking a titular khan to authorise it. The arrangement reminds us that power and legitimacy need not originate from the same place. ↗ Eyewitness ↗ Mongols

A correction of scale

Timur was a Turco-Mongol ruler operating within a Chinggisid political inheritance; calling him simply “a Mongol” obscures that complex inheritance. Ibn Khaldun's text itself frequently calls the troops “Tatars” or “Mongols,” reflecting the vocabulary and classification of his time.

05 / The city changes hands

An assurance is granted. A city burns.

The historical record now becomes difficult. As Timur advanced toward the city, Ibn Khaldun witnessed builders and engineers discussing siege works and water channels around the citadel. Within days, large siege engines had been deployed; the defenders sought terms. Timur also heard a claim to the Abbasid caliphate from a man who appealed to the hereditary authority of that office.

He called jurists and judges to examine the claim. Ibn Khaldun was among them. The scholar traced the competing legal positions concerning the caliphate and the historical passage of Abbasid authority from Baghdad to Cairo. Timur dismissed the claimant after hearing the legal arguments. Military sovereignty had briefly staged a forum of juridical legitimacy. ↗ Eyewitness

But meanwhile the conqueror seized wealth and permitted his soldiers to plunder Damascus. In Ibn Khaldun's account, discarded furnishings were burned, the fire spread into buildings, and the Great Mosque was terribly damaged. He condemned the destruction in unmistakable terms. The account is strong evidence for what he reported seeing or learning; its exact sequence and scale remain subjects for historical comparison with other chronicles. ↗ Eyewitness ↗ Mongols

The contrast

He would later secure written protection for particular classes of people: Qur'an teachers, secretaries, administrators and other officials who had served the Egyptian sultan.

These grants did not constitute a successful protection of the city as a whole. The historian obtained a limited diplomatic concession amid the broader calamity.

What makes the episode so hard to dismiss is its moral dissonance. The same ruler might receive a Qur'an with ceremonial respect, debate the legality of the caliphate, demonstrate curiosity about distant landscapes, and preside over the devastation of a major city.

Learning, curiosity and cruelty could coexist. A love of argument offers no guarantee of humane government.

06 / The rules of a court

A Qur'an, a poem, some sweets, and a signed protection.

A companion familiar with the practices of Timur's court had advised Ibn Khaldun to bring a present. The scholar chose a fine Qur'an, a prayer rug, a copy of al-Būṣīrī's Burda poem, and four boxes of confectionery from Cairo. These belong to a later audience, not his initial entry into the tent. ↗ Eyewitness

His report gives the gestures extraordinary precision. Timur raised the Qur'an to his head. He kissed the rug, asked about the poem and shared the sweets among the gathering. Ibn Khaldun had observed the gift practices of the court closely enough to participate with some skill.

He then requested written security for people he named. Timur instructed his secretary to produce an order, and Shah Malik affixed the ruler's seal. The document mattered because an oral promise could be transformed into something others might physically carry and invoke.

There is a deeper institutional issue here. Ibn Khaldun sought security for people whose skills could be valuable to a conqueror: teachers and administrators. His argument joined humanitarian concern to the pragmatic needs of government. This was a diplomatic calculation within the terms the ruler might accept.

For our ongoing inquiry

A ritual offering, a diplomatic audience, a written obligation and the prestige of expertise: this single scene contains several mechanisms by which relationships are established, enacted and enforced. The same instruments can be used in generous or coercive circumstances.

07 / The smallest debt

And then, the mule.

As the encounter was drawing to a close, Timur asked whether Ibn Khaldun possessed a good mule. Would he sell it? The historian declined the commercial framing: one such as he, he replied, should offer such a thing as a gesture of homage to one such as Timur. The conqueror answered that he meant to compensate him generously. The mule was taken away, and Ibn Khaldun never saw it again. ↗ Eyewitness

The incident appears almost comic after the scale of events. But it contains a precise negotiation of political hierarchy. A sale might imply equality of bargaining parties; a gift expresses rank and patronage. Timur insists on the possibility of generous repayment. Each person chooses a role in the exchange.

On his way back to Cairo, Ibn Khaldun and companions were robbed by a band of tribesmen. After a difficult journey through Ṣafad and Gaza, he returned to Egypt in March 1401. Later, an embassy brought money on Timur's behalf as payment for the mule. Ibn Khaldun would not accept it before consulting the Egyptian government, for fear that receiving funds from a hostile sovereign might be politically compromising. The amount delivered, he says, was incomplete. ↗ Eyewitness

Even when the empire had moved on, the obligation created by the mule remained.

It is an almost perfect miniature of diplomacy: an exchange that acquires meanings far larger than its monetary value.

08 / Information in both directions

Two texts leave Damascus.

One went toward Timur: a geographical account of North Africa, commissioned by the conqueror and ordered into Mongolian.

The other went west after Ibn Khaldun returned to Egypt: a letter to a ruler of the Maghreb recounting the encounter and explaining Timur's rise within the post-Chinggisid world. The letter included observations about the breadth of the conquering army, Timur's intelligence, and the violence of his campaigns. The identification of the particular Maghrebi ruler addressed is debated. ↗ Eyewitness

A scholar had been pressed into producing knowledge for a ruler who threatened his world. He then helped transmit knowledge about that ruler in the opposite direction.

Did he write the second account to warn North Africa? Did he hope to redress the imbalance created by the first? Some scholars have suggested as much, but the text alone cannot establish his internal motive. What it establishes is that knowledge itself became an object of political exchange. ↗ Lawrence ↗ Fischel

09 / After the meeting

The historian returned to his desk. The city remained.

Ibn Khaldun lived another five years, dying in Cairo in 1406. Timur died in 1405. Their encounter survives chiefly through the historian's own narrative, later scholarly translations, and references in other accounts of the era. ↗ Fischel ↗ Aramco

The autobiography is an extraordinary document, but it is also a composed account by an experienced political actor. It records his fear and diplomacy; it casts his conduct in defensible terms; it preserves remarkable conversations whose exact words passed through an interpreter and memory.

We have no equal, independent transcript from Timur's side. We should read it as testimony of rare richness and a document with its own authorial perspective.

What can the source establish securely?

That Ibn Khaldun says he descended the wall, met Timur, discussed the Maghreb, sovereignty and historical classification, wrote a geographic account, witnessed the siege and destruction, requested protections, gifted objects, gave up a mule and returned to Cairo. His broad account is supported by scholarly treatments of the encounter.

What remains interpretation?

How far Timur's geographical questions reflected military plans; how sincere the two men's mutual regard was; whether Ibn Khaldun sought to balance information supplied to Timur by later correspondence; the complete extent and effectiveness of local amnesty promises; and the exact wording of conversations conducted through an interpreter.

A few corrections worth preserving

35 days refers to the period Ibn Khaldun reported staying with Timur, not a verified count of meetings. The gifts were presented at a later audience. The written protections were limited. The famous descent from the wall is established; the popular detail of a basket is not specified by the translated account. The interlocutors were not speaking directly without mediation.

· · ·
Before Damascus

To see what he carried into that tent, go back seven decades, to Tunis. At seventeen he lived through a catastrophe that emptied cities and took his parents. Much later, after palace intrigues, captivity, diplomacy and exile, he would ask a question of immense ambition: could history become a science of the conditions under which societies exist?

10 / The world changes

The plague takes his parents.

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Khaldun was born in Tunis in 1332, into a learned family whose history included generations in al-Andalus. He grew up within a tradition of Arabic language learning, law, Qur'anic studies, mathematics and philosophy. In 1348–49, the Black Death swept through North Africa. Among those who died were his parents and several of his teachers. ↗ Alatas ↗ Aramco

The plague did more than remove people he loved. It altered the social world in which he had expected to live. Urban institutions lost personnel. Families and networks disappeared. The political environment shifted.

There is a haunting parallel with your initial question about northern peoples who seem to vanish from the historical record: the appearance of discontinuity can reflect many kinds of transformation—death, migration, adoption of new affiliations, loss of institutions, and changes in the names by which others classify a people.

Ibn Khaldun's most influential work would eventually ask historians to account for precisely this problem: the conditions of a society change, so the plausibility of historical reports changes with them.

11 / An education in power

He served rulers, abandoned courts, and returned to them.

His early adulthood unfolded across a shifting political landscape: Tunis, Fez, Granada, Béjaïa and other centres of the Maghreb. He was secretary, negotiator, administrator and political adviser, sometimes favoured and sometimes suspected. He experienced imprisonment in Fez and negotiated with rulers and communities on competing sides of regional struggles. In 1364 he went as an envoy to the Castilian court in Seville, ancestral home of his family. ↗ Aramco ↗ Alatas

The experience taught him that sovereign power was often less stable than its ceremonial language implied. Rulers needed military followers, fiscal resources, allies and credible claims to legitimacy. A change in one part of this arrangement could overturn another.

After years of political movement, he withdrew in the mid-1370s to a stronghold at Qalʿat Ibn Salāma, in present-day Algeria. There he began composing the great historical work Kitāb al-ʿIbar, including the book that later became famous independently as the Muqaddimah. He completed an initial version around 1377–78, then continued revising his work. ↗ Alatas ↗ Princeton

When he moved to Cairo in 1382, he found a great urban centre of scholarship, legal institutions, and international exchange. His career as a Maliki judge and teacher brought prestige and fresh political difficulties. He also suffered a terrible family loss when a ship carrying loved ones to Egypt was wrecked. This was the man who, nearly seventy, went over the wall at Damascus—not a detached academic, but a historian whose methods had been tested by government, family tragedy, court diplomacy and danger. ↗ Aramco

12 / The new science

The historian must examine what is possible.

For Ibn Khaldun, a chronicle could accumulate carefully transmitted reports yet still mislead. A witness might exaggerate; a transmitter might misunderstand; a later historian might repeat a claim that no longer fits the conditions of the society being described. The solution was to study the structures that make an event plausible.

He called his subject ʿilm al-ʿumrān (علم العمران), the science of human social organisation, settlement and civilisation. The Arabic ʿumrān has a richer range than any single English word; scholars variously emphasise habitation, development, social organisation and civilisation. ↗ SEP ↗ Alatas ↗ Colonial

Our simplified depiction of his critical method—not an algorithm Ibn Khaldun literally drew.

Take the famous example of a chronicler reporting an immense ancient army. Could it be fed? Could the region have supported so many households? Would transport, distance, taxation and production permit the claim? If the structural conditions are wrong, transmitted testimony alone cannot rescue the story.

In one of history's most productive intellectual reversals, a thinker trained to respect authoritative texts insisted that the world described by the texts also had to be understood. He inherited, argued with and extended established Islamic traditions of historical writing and philosophical inquiry. His innovation was powerful precisely because it was built within a living intellectual world. ↗ SEP ↗ Colonial ↗ Alatas

13 / The binding force

ʿAṣabiyyah is more than blood.

In accounts of Ibn Khaldun, ʿaṣabiyyah is often rendered as “tribal solidarity.” That catches part of the concept. It can also mislead, because the political phenomenon he studies includes allies, clients, religious movements and relations of common loyalty that reach beyond a single biological lineage.

Kinship creates obligations, but the sense of shared descent is itself a social and political fact. Beliefs about belonging affect who cooperates with whom, who risks violence for whom, and which leaders are able to mobilise a group. ↗ Darling ↗ SEP ↗ Alatas

Different networks can acquire ties through alliance, marriage, common institutions, religious commitments or coercion. The diagram is a conceptual translation of our discussion.

This is where your “peoples as molecules” comparison becomes especially productive. The number of people matters, but so do the ties. Two populations of similar size can possess radically different capacities for collective action because their obligations and methods of coordination differ.

Yet the analogy also reveals a limit. Human beings interpret their bonds. A lineage can remember its ancestry selectively; a new ruler can rearrange military units; a teacher can cultivate loyalty across languages; a community can revise the terms of an institution. The participants can change their own rules of connection.

A people is connected by its relationships—and by what its members understand those relationships to mean.

Solidarity is also not intrinsically benevolent. It can sustain hospitality and mutual aid; it can equally support exclusive privileges, conquest and collective aggression. The strength of an organisation and the justice of its actions must be examined separately.

14 / The two environments

The camp and the city produce different kinds of strength.

Ibn Khaldun distinguished between badāwa and ḥaḍāra, terms commonly translated as pastoral or desert society and sedentary or urban civilisation. These categories referred to differing modes of social organisation—not two permanently separate biological peoples. Their meanings have been flattened by translation, and the literature increasingly emphasises the limits of rendering them simply “nomadic” and “civilised.” ↗ Oxford ↗ Colonial ↗ Alatas

He observed that communities accustomed to material scarcity, local self-defence and reciprocal obligations might maintain the strong cohesion needed to challenge wealthier rulers. Urban centres, meanwhile, could specialise labour, cultivate crafts, sustain formal scholarship and produce elaborate institutions. Those capacities were enormous sources of cultural achievement—and also created dependence upon taxation, government and complex systems of provision.

I.

Pastoral / rural association

Local cooperation, direct obligations, mobility, practical adaptation, and forms of cohesion tied closely to kinship and alliance.

II.

Urban / settled association

Occupational specialisation, markets, administrative institutions, schools, crafts, public buildings and concentrated surplus.

Both kinds of organisation depend upon cooperation. Neither maps cleanly onto a single ethnicity. Cities can house mixed populations; mobile pastoral communities can sustain long-distance exchange and sophisticated political institutions.

His concern was that the shift from a founding group's intimate solidarity to the luxuries and hierarchies of a royal court could undo the very bonds that secured a dynasty's power. That was a theory about the consequences of institutional change, not a declaration that the city lacked knowledge or achievement.

15 / The dynastic paradox

Success changes the successful.

One of Ibn Khaldun's sharpest observations is that a ruler can undermine the original allies who helped establish his rule. Conquest creates access to wealth. Wealth funds standing forces, court dependents and administrators. The ruler may then seek exclusive control of authority and reduce the influence of his founding partners. Those partners lose status and power; the ruler grows more dependent upon people whose position comes from his favour. ↗ Excerpt ↗ Darling

A simplified mechanism, not a universal destiny. Real dynasties can renew institutions, incorporate new groups, or persist far longer than a stylised cycle suggests.

He also tied political stability to money. Soldiers and administration must be supported. Governments may respond to mounting expenditure through heavier extraction, which can damage productive activity and reduce the revenue on which the dynasty relies. The structure of rule is therefore connected to an economic feedback loop. ↗ Excerpt

The familiar summary that dynasties “last three generations” should be read as a stylised pattern, not a clock. Its value lies in identifying possible mechanisms—intergenerational change, concentration of rule, weakening alliances, escalating costs—not in predicting an expiry date for every kingdom.

There is an open question here: could newly created civic institutions replenish solidarity, replacing a ruling clan's weakened ties with wider forms of cooperation? Could a prosperous city enlarge, rather than erode, certain kinds of collective belonging? Those are challenges to develop beyond his original examples.

16 / Why the city matters

His civilisation has crafts, markets—and knowledge.

Ibn Khaldun's account was concerned with much more than dynastic battles. The Muqaddimah examines sustenance, crafts, earning, urban growth, teaching and the sciences. His order of exposition is revealing: he moves from types of society and political power to cities and occupations, and then to education and scholarly disciplines. ↗ Princeton ↗ SEP ↗ Education

A farmer, smith, carpenter and baker depend on one another even if they have never met. An urban population can sustain skilled crafts because its members exchange labour and produce surplus. Formal learning becomes increasingly possible when the organisation of daily life makes time, specialists, institutions and materials available.

This is an important complement to the story of conquest. Social life can be held together by mutual dependence as well as by tribal loyalty or military command. Some forms of knowledge are embodied in craft practice; others depend on written study and professional teaching. In the economy of a city, both can become specialised and transmitted across generations. ↗ SEP ↗ Umran

For our discussion of institutions, this matters enormously. A school, guild, profession, trade network or ritual gathering can bind people through shared practices. The bonds are different from those of an army, but they are real and sometimes remarkably durable.

17 / Reading him critically

The modern Ibn Khaldun is also a product of translation.

His reputation as a “founder of sociology” is understandable, but it can distort his project by placing him inside a category invented centuries later. He was a scholar working with Arabic historiography, Islamic law, philosophy, theology and political practice. Later readers found striking anticipations of sociology and economics in his writing; that retrospective resemblance must not erase his intellectual setting. ↗ Colonial ↗ SEP

His treatment of climate and peoples has also provoked considerable debate. A recent study argues that colonial-era translators recast his environmental and historical categories into more rigid racial terminology than the Arabic justified. This is a scholarly interpretation worth investigating, not a settled absolution of every hierarchy or troubling claim in his work. The central methodological lesson is to distinguish the original conceptual vocabulary from modern labels added later. ↗ Colonial

One must also resist reading a single elegant explanation backward into every historical society. Groups frequently cooperate without a shared ancestry; highly centralised institutions can remain resilient; urban identities can mobilise extraordinary sacrifice. The model earns its value when it sharpens questions, not when it forecloses them.

Is ʿaṣabiyyah exactly “social cohesion”?

“Social cohesion,” “group feeling” and “solidarity” are useful translations but each shifts emphasis. His discussions of descent, alliance, leadership and political power give the concept a particular historical shape. It overlaps modern social-network language without mapping perfectly onto measurable network density.

Is badāwa simply “nomadism”?

No single English gloss captures all its uses. The rural/tribal and urban/settled opposition is analytical and historically situated, not a racial division. Different scholars prefer “rural,” “pastoral,” “desert” or “tribal” depending on context. That choice can profoundly affect how modern readers interpret the theory.

Is the dynastic cycle inevitable?

No. It is a historical theory of recurring tendencies, built primarily from cases Ibn Khaldun knew. Intergenerational institutions, changes in military financing, new forms of legitimacy and civic solidarity can interrupt the schematic sequence.

18 / A people that survives its empire

The Mongols reveal a delay between political power and identity.

The thread that brought us here began with the Kyrgyz, the names in Chinese dynastic histories, and your image of peoples colliding like molecules. We found that ancestry, language, ruling identity and political affiliation can follow different historical trajectories.

Consider a suggestive comparison. Historian Morris Rossabi argued that the conquests of the thirteenth century laid foundations for Mongol identity while the imperial structure itself remained divided; a more encompassing identity developed through later cultural memory in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. This is a specific historical interpretation, not a universal account of ethnic development. ↗ Rossabi

A.

Political time

Alliances, battles, imperial succession, conquest, fiscal demands, treaties.

B.

Cultural time

Stories, language change, lineages, rituals, institutions, and the remembered meaning of a past empire.

The two timescales need not coincide. A polity might form before a broad identity does. An identity might persist after the polity dissolves. A shared story could later influence the formation of new political institutions.

For the Kyrgyz, the epic of Manas offers another kind of cultural memory: narratives of unification carried through oral performance. This makes it useful to ask whether a cultural practice can repeatedly renew relationships among people who no longer share a single governmental structure. We should study the historical uses and transformations of the epic rather than simply presume that every performance produces solidarity.

19 / The experiment opens

What if cohesion could be cultivated without conquest?

Here we leave Ibn Khaldun's historical propositions and enter our own speculative inquiry.

In our conversation, you imagined peoples as molecules: some aggregate, some disperse, some recombine; a threat or a catalyst changes the reaction. Gemini elaborated that metaphor through polymerisation, decomposition, displacement and phase transitions. Ibn Khaldun adds an essential variable: the strength and quality of the relationships that allow a group to coordinate.

But a society need not be organised around an imperial centre. A translation network, confederation of schools, transregional artistic practice, shared obligation to maintain an ecology, or recurring ritual assembly might generate connectivity without unifying all of its participants under one sovereign.

A purely speculative distributed institutional network, inspired by the seven-school setting of PMU. The nodes and connections are illustrative, not assignments of institutional functions.

PMU's Congress at year zero and Covenant twenty-five years later pose a beautiful test. A one-time meeting can initiate agreements. A second meeting, after generations of students, teachers and envoys have succeeded one another, tests whether obligations survive the turnover of individuals. The periodic return becomes an institution that can renew ties between otherwise independent centres.

Think of the differences between coercive cohesion, in which a ruler compels participation; reciprocal cohesion, in which collaborators benefit from continuing exchange; and ritual cohesion, in which people make membership and obligation visible again and again. These are proposed analytical distinctions for our worldbuilding, not categories attributed to Ibn Khaldun.

The research question

Could a distributed society become powerful through repeated relationship-making, shared knowledge and mutually recognised obligations—without reproducing the concentrated sovereignty and courtly estrangement described in the dynastic theory?

Ibn Khaldun would surely ask how such ties are enforced; who has the resources to sustain them; how collective decisions are reached; how unjust obligations are contested; and what happens when the strongest school begins extracting more than it returns. Those are useful questions precisely because they keep a beautiful system from becoming an effortless utopia.

20 / Return to the encounter

The scientist and the survivor were the same man.

Return to Damascus for a moment. A scholar approaches an imperial court carrying books, legal expertise, geographic knowledge, ritual gifts and an extraordinary claim about the causes of power. The man receiving him possesses armies, wealth, genealogical legitimacy, scribes, translators and the authority to issue protection.

Nothing in the scene is reducible to a contest between sword and pen. The scholar's knowledge can serve the conqueror; the conqueror's institutions can authorise the scholar's request; the scholar's words can influence decisions; the city's destruction can proceed regardless.

Relationships are created through food, flattery, language, titles, seals, obligations and memory. Many of them are unequal. Some are protective. Others are dangerous.

And years later, the historian's account carries those relationships to us.

The world he wanted to explain was the very world in which he had to negotiate his next breath.

That is why I think we should preserve Ibn Khaldun as a full historical and intellectual encounter. His work invites us to seek mechanisms. His life reminds us that every mechanism passes through actual human beings.

Sources, method & open questions

The Damascus encounter is anchored above all in the autobiographical account (al-Taʿrīf) as translated by Mohammed Ballan, compared with Fischel's earlier scholarly translation and later research. Claims framed as Ibn Khaldun's reported experience are not treated as an omniscient camera, and short reconstructed scenes preserve recorded details without inventing dialogue.

The ideas are read through the Muqaddimah, modern scholarship and excerpts, keeping Ibn Khaldun's own concepts distinct from the network and chemical analogies proposed here. For a fuller engagement with his writing, see Franz Rosenthal's three-volume translation, reissued unabridged in 2026.

Could repeated artistic, scholarly and diplomatic exchanges generate bonds across sovereign boundaries? And can a society build solidarity robust enough to survive changes in rulers without becoming rigid?

Footnote · For the Straits

Ibn Khaldun in the Polymathic Universe.

One date makes him belong to both worlds. Ibn Khaldun died in Cairo in March 1406; the Monsoon Turn came twenty years later, in 1426. Everything in this essay, from the plague and the courts to the wall, the tent and the mule, happened in the Straits' world exactly as it happened in ours. PMU inherits him whole. What changes after 1426 is who carries his book, in which language, and what they build with it.

Six seeds

i.

The book that arrived in Arabic

In our world, most modern readers met the Muqaddimah through nineteenth-century European translations, and the colonial-archive study in section 17 argues that those translators hardened his categories into racial ones. In PMU, European colonialism stayed small, so the book travels the older way: by monsoon, in Arabic, in the cargo and memory of the Andalusi, Maghrebi and Indic scholars whose lineages gather in Al-Merikani. The Straits meet ʿaṣabiyyah, badāwa and ʿumrān in their own vocabulary first.

Al-Merikani's clerks take ʿumrān as their word for what the house's ledger records: the standing of a relationship between ports. They describe the strength of a ledger entry as its ʿaṣabiyyah. The Straits' greatest equity is relationship, so the Straits' oldest textbook on relationship becomes a set text.

ii.

The Turn had a reader

Ibn Khaldun's student in Cairo, the historian al-Maqrizi, lived until 1442, across the Monsoon Turn. His chronicles record the Chinese junks that reached Jeddah in 1432 on Zheng He's last voyage. Zheng He himself descended from a Bukharan governor of Yunnan under the Yuan, which makes a quiet rhyme with the Khwarazmian jurist who interpreted in Timur's tent.

The seed: a copy of the Muqaddimah sits in the consortium's council at the Turn. Its members read the first European fleets in the Indian Ocean in Khaldunian terms: a hungry, tightly bound people from the margins of the settled world, sailing for rich ports. They answer with a cohesion of their own, a consortium of ports. The Monsoon Turn becomes the first policy drafted by readers of Ibn Khaldun.

iii.

The estuary: desert and city, translated to water

His great pair, badāwa and ḥaḍāra, came from desert and city. The Straits translate it to the sea. The Orang Laut, the sea peoples whose loyalty upheld Malay ports for centuries, are the Straits' badāwa: mobile, reciprocal, fiercely bound. The port is its ḥaḍāra.

Taman Budi Pusaka's everyday name, Muara, means estuary, the place where river and sea meet. The house can hold that meeting as a founding idea: a port stays strong for as long as it keeps drawing solidarity from the sea people who never settled in it.

iv.

The 120-year clock

Ibn Khaldun reckoned a dynasty's usual life at three generations of about forty years each, roughly 120 years. Count from the Kindling in 1918 and the clock reads 2038, twelve years past PMU's present. Al-Merikani scholars have watched that date for a century. The signs they track are his: founding partners pushed to the edge, a centre that grows exclusive, costs that climb. To them, the resentment of schools held outside the Covenant reads like the second stage of his cycle.

The world already holds their answer. The Congress–Covenant cycle returns every twenty-five years and re-makes the bond for each new generation of teachers and students before the old bond can thin. In this reading the Covenant is the Straits' reply to the Muqaddimah: solidarity renewed on a schedule. 2038 becomes the year everyone watches, with Ibn Khaldun's book open on the table.

v.

The tent, reversed

At the Congress, the Straits sit where Timur sat. Delegations arrive bearing gifts, the Straits ask them to describe their homelands, and they read the praise they receive as information. What a supplicant chooses to praise shows what he fears and what he wants. They learned this from the one account in which a supplicant told us plainly why he praised. The bid papers at the auction are each delegation's version of Ibn Khaldun's speech before Timur, and the Straits read them the way he would have.

vi.

Paying for the mule

A Straits idiom: to pay for the mule is to honour a debt late, through a changed government, and not quite in full, yet to honour it. When a school whose polity has changed since the Congress returns with only part of its sealed gift, people say it has paid for the mule, and it is welcomed. Al-Merikani's ledger keeps a column for such entries: obligations that outlive the regimes that made them.

1.

Reads the weave

In any room he sees who would risk what for whom. To him, cohesion is visible, like threads drawn between people.

2.

Counts the grain

He hears any report and asks what would feed it, carry it and pay for it. Rumours of strange magic from distant ports land on his desk first.

3.

The courteous tongue

He praises the powerful in true sentences shaped to what they want to hear. Flattery is his diplomatic instrument, and he knows it.

4.

Strict bench, supple tent

He was appointed to and removed from the judge's seat many times. He is unbending in law and endlessly adaptive in negotiation.

5.

Built from weather

He lost his parents to plague and his family to a shipwreck, so he writes about the world's upheavals as someone who has lived through them.

6.

Appetite and grace

He eats the rishta with pleasure. He gives up his mule without complaint, and he still keeps the account.

Why Mozi. Mozi (墨子) stopped a war before it began. When the state of Chu prepared to attack Song with Gongshu Ban's new siege ladders, Mozi walked ten days and nights to the Chu court. There he untied his belt to stand for a city wall and used wooden slips as siege engines (解带为城，以牒为械). Gongshu Ban attacked nine ways, and Mozi held nine times. When Gongshu Ban hinted that he still knew one way to win, Mozi answered that his three hundred students already stood on Song's walls with his defences. Chu called off the war. On the way home it rained as Mozi passed through Song, and the gatekeeper of the state he had just saved would not let him in to shelter.

Ibn Khaldun watched Timur's engineers prepare the siege of Damascus. Mozi out-argued a siege engineer at a table. On their way home, both were left unthanked, one robbed on the road and one shut out in the rain. Each is the other's missing half: the historian who reads how a people binds, and the defender who shows a court the outcome of a war without its dead.

Storyline moment · Over the wall at Veyr

Veyr holds the surviving Straits exchange teachers and students. The seven houses are building their answer. Before it is released, they send two men to Veyr's war camp: the historian and the defender.

They go down Veyr's harbour wall at night, as one of them has done before. Veyr's commander receives them in a pavilion while he hands out dishes to his officers. He asks the historian to describe the Straits, its seven houses, its harbours and its people, so completely that he might see it as if he had been there. Ibn Khaldun does. He names every house and every bond between them, with such precision that the commander understands what the account is telling him: the Straits move as one body.

Then Mozi asks for a table. He unties his belt and lays it down as Veyr's harbour wall. Veyr's engineers set out their machines and spell-works in wooden slips. They attack nine ways, and Mozi holds nine times. When the chief engineer says he knows one more way, which is to remove the defender, Mozi tells him the defence is already standing on the walls of every Straits port, taught to three hundred students.

Half of Veyr's council stands down that night. The historian asks for one thing in writing: protection for the named teachers and students held in Veyr. The commander's seal goes onto the order, and the captives walk out with them. The war that follows is shorter for it.

As they leave, the commander admires the historian's mule and asks to buy it. Years later, a payment for the mule arrives at the Straits from Veyr's new government. It is incomplete. The historian accepts it only after consulting the seven houses.

In Damascus he explained the bonds of power to a conqueror. In the Straits he helps a people make theirs on purpose.
