The proposition · three traditions

Three ways of knowing, one warming world

This thread grew out of the Three Tables experiments in the Polymathic Learner, where one problem was put to Greek, Vedic and Chinese mathematicians and each answered in its own native way. Doubling the Square and Squaring the Circle drew out three kinds of answer, and three verbs to carry them.

to boundthe Greek table

Archimedes traps the circle between a polygon inside and a polygon outside. The answer is an interval, proved from both sides.

to balancethe Vedic table

The Śulba cord-makers turn a square into a circle of equal area, and the circle back into the square. The answer is a rule that runs in both directions.

to cut finerthe Chinese table

Liu Hui cuts the polygon finer and finer: 割之弥细，所失弥少. The answer is a procedure that can always be refined.

Here the three tables leave mathematics and sit down before a warming planet. The thread runs in three volumes, from the real science, to the deep past, to a future with all of modern physics in hand.

I · How they find out
The olive line climbs the hills. The Lyceum proves by contradiction that the warming is theirs: the nights warm faster than the days. The rains arrive under the wrong star. The ledger of fires shows the sky receiving more than the forests return. Plum blossoms open earlier in ten thousand gazetteers. Tabulated county by county, the trend can no longer hide.
II · How they struggle
Every city waits for its neighbour. A proved bound, carved at Delphi, becomes the first treaty of the air. A dakṣiṇā, a fee, on every fire, payable in trees. A rule that runs both ways, from fire to forest and back. An emperor’s edict of self-blame, then Yu’s old rule: channel the fires, don’t dam them. Charcoal goes back into the fields.
III · Their great work
The Parasol of Syracuse. A cloud of bronze-foil discs at the balance point between Earth and Sun, shading one part in fifty of the light. The White Falcon. An altar of white-glazed brick with wings across a desert, rebuilt every year, and ships that whiten the clouds with sea-spray. 补天 · Mending the Sky. Rockets loft five-coloured stone dust, patch by patch, measured and adjusted. The sunsets turn five colours.
IV · The same rains, by magic
The Wind Stations. A league of island towers bends the seasonal winds back toward the failing fields, and the seas learn to share one wind. The Rain Circle. Singers, water-workers and grain-keepers tune the monsoon across whole catchments, and learn that every crop keeps its own time. The Seasonal Calendar. Ten thousand valleys report to one academy, which schedules the rains, and then learns to read the reports it never asked for.
The rules of the game

Three worlds that never meet

In each sky below, one civilisation goes on alone. It finds its own fuel, burns its own fires, and meets its own changing climate. No ship, scholar or rumour crosses between them. What each one notices, argues over and builds comes only from its own tools of thought, the same tools we watched at work in the Three Tables experiments.

Each world also has to take one hard step before anything else can happen. A bad season has to become a pattern, and a pattern has to become a history.

01A storm ruins a harvest.an event
02The rains fail again, and again.a pattern
03People compare their grandparents’ seasons with their own.a changing baseline
04They see one connected sky and reach for it.a shared system

That step needs three things: records kept far apart in place and time, a memory long enough to tell the exception from the return, and someone willing to count. Part One shows that each tradition already held all three. Part Two follows each world through its own discovery, struggle and great work. Part Three runs the same rains again, in worlds where magic works.

Every story is invented. Every seed it grows from is real: a text, an institution, an observation that actually existed. They are gathered at the back with their sources, so you can tell where history ends and the dream begins.

Part OneThe record GPT

What each archive already held

Long before anyone could model a climate, each tradition was already watching the sky, measuring the rain and remaking land and water. These are the real traces each invented world grows from.

The Greek archive

Physicians, weather-watchers, philosophers and drainage engineers.

  • Late 5th c. BCE · Hippocratic corpusAirs, Waters, Places

    The physician arriving in a new city is told to study its seasons, prevailing winds, water, terrain and ways of life, and to read its illnesses in that setting. Medicine begins with the environment.1

  • 4th–3rd c. BCE · TheophrastusOn Weather Signs

    Birds, animals, the shapes of clouds, the look of the mountains and the risings of stars, gathered into a systematic book of warnings.2

  • 4th c. BCE · PlatoCritias 111b–d

    An older Attica of deep soil and wide woods, set against the bare hills of Plato’s own day, “the bones of a wasted body”. Land seen changing over the long run.3

  • Bronze Age · BoeotiaThe drainage of Lake Kopais

    Mycenaean canals and embankments that drained a lake to make farmland: an inhabited landscape remade by engineering.4

  • 5th c. BCE, told later · Diogenes LaertiusEmpedocles the wind-stayer

    A later life of Empedocles calls him kōlusanemas, the one who stops the winds, and tells of skins stretched along the hills to hold back the gales that blighted the crops. A philosopher remembered for changing the wind itself.5

The Vedic and Indic archive

Hymns to the rain, and the statecraft and astronomy that grew after the Vedas.

  • Ṛgveda 5.83 and 1.32Parjanya and the released waters

    Hymns to Parjanya, the roaring rainstorm that quickens the plants, and to Indra, who breaks Vṛtra and sets the held waters free. Water, obstruction, fertility and release, worked out in poetry and cosmology.67

  • Arthaśāstra 2.24The Director of Agriculture

    The statecraft manual gives rainfall amounts by region, how the rains fall across the season, and how officials should plan the crops from them. It also describes a vessel for measuring rain.89

  • c. 150 CE · JunagadhRudradāman’s inscription

    A great storm breaches the embankment of Sudarśana Lake; the king has it rebuilt, and the inscription remembers the earlier reigns that built and improved it. Disaster, public works and a ruler’s standing carved into one rock.10

  • 6th c. CE · VarāhamihiraBṛhat Saṃhitā

    Long chapters on foretelling the rains, where close observation of clouds, winds and seasons sits beside the reading of the stars, all part of one science.11

The Chinese archive

Royal questions, rivers led to the fields, and a fossil that remembered another climate.

  • c. 1250–1050 BCE · ShangThe oracle bones

    Royal diviners ask, again and again, whether it will rain, whether drought will come, whether the harvest will be good. Rain enters a practice of inquiry and inscription.1213

  • c. 256 BCE · SichuanDujiangyan

    The Min River is split and led to the Chengdu plain for irrigation and flood control, with the river’s own shape and flow doing the work. Yu’s way of channelling, built in stone and bamboo, and still running.14

  • Zhou onwardThe Mandate of Heaven

    Floods and droughts read against a ruler’s standing. The answer to drought joins public works, granary relief and prayer for rain.1516

  • 11th c. CE · Shen KuoThe bamboo at Yanzhou

    Fossil bamboo turns up in the dry north, where no bamboo grows. Shen Kuo infers that the climate there was once wetter: a different environmental past, read from the ground.17

Each archive already held the long memory, the far-apart records and the people willing to count.
Part TwoThree skies Claude

Now the invented histories. In each sky one civilisation burns its own fires, finds out, struggles, and builds the great work its way of knowing predicts.

Sky I
The Greek sky
to bound
δός μοι πᾶ στῶ καὶ τὰν γᾶν κινάσω.“Give me a place to stand, and I will move the earth.” · Archimedes, as reported by Pappus
I · The discovery

The olive line climbs the hills

In this sky, Hero of Alexandria’s spinning steam-toy never stays a toy. Within a few generations of the Library at Alexandria, the aeolipile pumps the mines of Laurion, and the forges of Athens burn black stone dug from the hills of Thrace.

The first people to notice anything are farmers. Olive trees that once stopped at the foot of the hills start climbing them. Snow on Parnassus melts a month early. The Etesian winds that carry the grain ships from Egypt come late and weak, and grain prices in the Piraeus begin to jump.

Greeks had kept parapegmata for centuries, stone calendars with a peg for each day, noting which star rose with which weather. In the city of Larissa, an invented keeper we will call Philinna does something nobody had bothered to do. She lays three centuries of pegged weather side by side and counts. The frosts are fewer, and the first figs come earlier. seed

The question goes to the Lyceum, and the Lyceum answers the only way it knows: with a proof. Suppose the Sun has grown hotter. Then the days should have warmed most, since the Sun shines only by day. But Philinna’s pegs say the nights have warmed faster than the days. So the Sun is not the cause, and something is holding the heat in after dark. The only new thing in the air of Greece is the smoke of its own forges.

They never found the cause directly. They proved what it could not be until only one thing was left.
II · The struggle

The commons of the air

Proving it was the easy part. Aristotle had already put the difficulty in one line, centuries earlier: what belongs to the most people receives the least care. seed The air above Corinth is the same air as above Athens. If Athens banks its forges and Corinth doesn’t, Athens is poorer and the sky is barely cooler.

The Assembly argues for years. Sophists, paid to win, take every side. The gods warm the world, they say, or the warming is good for vines, or Athens should go first and let the barbarians follow. Ostraka pile up with the names of forge-masters.

The breakthrough comes from geometry. The geometers of the Lyceum, given every record in the Greek world, refuse to give a single number for how much warmer the world will grow. They give two. No less than this, and no more than that, each edge with its own proof. A bound is something rival cities can agree to without trusting one another, because each can check the proof itself.

The two numbers are carved on a stele at Delphi, where every city already sends envoys. The Amphictyony, the old league that guards the sanctuary, makes the bound its law. Each city may burn only its share of the gap between what the world is now and the upper edge of the bound. It is the first treaty ever written about the air.

III · The great work

The Parasol of Syracuse

Cutting the fires is slow. So a Syracusan geometer, invented here and called Kallias, reaches back to Archimedes’ boast about the place to stand, and asks whether there is a place in the sky where a shade could stand still.

There is. Somewhere on the line between the Earth and the Sun, the Sun’s pull and the Earth’s pull balance. Hang something there, and it stays between us and the Sun, casting a soft shadow that never moves off the world. Kallias finds it with a compass and a straightedge, from Aristarchus’ measurements of how far away the Sun and Moon are. Aristarchus’ Sun is far too close, so his first parasol is the wrong size. The second generation of geometers measures again, finds the error, and the proof survives the correction, as proofs do.

Kallias’ figure · the place to stand, drawn the Greek way
At Β the two pulls cancel, and a shade there sits on the line from Α to Γ. The figure is not to scale: Β is far closer to the Earth than the Sun.

The parasol is not one sheet. It is a cloud of bronze foil, discs a hand wide and thin as a cicada’s wing, beaten in the workshops of Rhodes by the hundred thousand. The bound at Delphi tells them exactly how much shade to build: enough to dim the Sun by about one part in fifty, no more and no less. That is the whole design brief: a number between two proofs.

On a clear day in Syracuse, the Sun looks no different. But there is a shimmer at its edge that astronomers can see through smoked glass, a faint grain in the light like dust in a sunbeam. Children are taught to call it Archimedes’ hand.

Sky II
The Vedic sky
to balance
ऋतं च सत्यं चाभीद्धात्तपसोऽध्यजायत ।“Order and truth were born from kindled heat.” · Ṛgveda 10.190.1
I · The discovery

The rains forget their star

In this sky the fire never leaves the centre of life. The kilns that bake ten thousand bricks for a single altar multiply into kilns for cities. The smiths whose crucible steel will one day become famous feed their furnaces day and night.

The people who notice first are the priests. The whole ritual year is pinned to the sky. Each rite has its season, and each season its nakṣatra, the lunar mansion the moon passes through when it begins. For generations, the monsoon has broken when the moon sits in the same mansions. Now the rains come under the wrong stars. Sowings planned by the calendar fail. The rites, timed to the sky, start falling out of step with the earth.

Here is how this tradition reasons. Every fire is an offering, and every offering rises as smoke to the sky. That is what an offering is. So an invented adhvaryu priest we will call Devavrata keeps a ledger with two columns: what the fires send up, and what the forests and fields take back down. For a thousand years the columns balanced. Now the first column is far longer than the second. The sky, he concludes, is holding offerings it was never meant to keep. Ṛta, the order that keeps seasons and stars in step, has tilted. seed

They didn’t ask what the sky is made of. They asked whether the exchange still balanced, and it didn’t.
II · The struggle

A fee for every fire

Every Vedic rite already ends with a dakṣiṇā, the fee paid to the officiants. Devavrata proposes the obvious extension. Every fire, sacred or industrial, owes a dakṣiṇā to the sky, payable in the only thing that takes smoke back down: trees. So many groves for so many fires.

The hardest fires to charge are the grandest. Kings measure their glory in great sacrifices, and the horse sacrifice alone burns for days. A rāja who plants a forest for his fire looks, to his rivals, like a rāja who could not afford a bigger fire. In the assemblies, the sabhā and the samiti, the argument runs for a generation.

It is settled the way the altar-builders always settled things: with a rule that runs in both directions. The cord-makers had rules to turn a square into a circle of equal area and back again. Now the ritualists write a rule that turns a fire into a forest of equal weight, and a forest back into the fire it permits. A king may burn as grandly as he likes, as long as he can show the grove that balances it. Prestige moves from the size of the fire to the size of the forest.

Within a lifetime, the most admired kings are the ones with the widest groves, and the ledger begins, slowly, to close.

III · The great work

The White Falcon

The groves take decades to grow. So the ritualists turn to their oldest masterpiece, the agnicayana, the great fire-altar laid as a falcon in flight. Its bricks were counted to match the moments of the year, 10,800 of them. seed If an altar can hold the year, they reason, a large enough altar can hold back the Sun.

The White Falcon · laid out on the desert, as the cord-makers would
The falcon is built from the same brick shapes as the ancient altar, scaled up until each wing runs for leagues across the sand. White glaze turns sunlight straight back to the sky.

The White Falcon is laid across the western desert, its wings leagues long, from bricks glazed so white they hurt to look at. Sunlight that would have warmed the sand bounces straight back into the sky. Like every Vedic altar, it is built to the season and dismantled after. Each year the falcon is relaid, a little larger or a little smaller, according to what the ledger says. It is an engineering that can be unbuilt, a rule that runs both ways, in brick.

Along the coasts, the priests of Varuṇa, lord of the waters and keeper of ṛta, add a second offering. Fleets of ships throw fine sea-spray into the low clouds, and the clouds turn whiter and brighter, sending more sunlight back before it ever reaches the land. Offerings rise as smoke. These rise as salt.

Herders on the desert’s edge say that at dawn, when the sun first strikes the glaze, the falcon seems to lift off the sand for a moment and hang there, white against the white sky.

Sky III
The Chinese sky
to cut finer
炼五色石以补苍天。“She smelted stones of five colours to mend the blue sky.” · 《淮南子·览冥训》, of Nüwa
I · The discovery

The plum blossoms come early

In this sky, the Song dynasty’s great age of coal and iron never ends. The furnaces of the north burn black stone, and Kaifeng’s winters smell of it. Shen Kuo, who really did name a strange black oil 石油, “rock oil,” and really did predict 此物后必大行于世, that it would one day be used everywhere, turns out to be exactly right, centuries early. seed

Nobody in this sky sets out to discover anything. The empire simply never stops writing things down. Every county keeps a gazetteer, a 地方志, recording floods, droughts, locusts, the day the river froze and the day the plum trees flowered. The 司天监, the Bureau of Astronomy, collects omens from every corner of the realm.

For a century the entries look like scattered weather. Then a junior clerk at the Bureau is told to tabulate them by county, and then by township, and then decade by decade. Cut finer, lose less. The pattern sharpens with every pass. The plums are blossoming earlier, the river ice is breaking sooner, the frost line is creeping north, and all of it, in every province, at once. seed

Under the doctrine of 天人感应, Heaven answers human conduct with portents. To the court this can mean only one thing. Heaven is responding to something the empire is doing.

II · The struggle

Channel, don’t dam

The emperor does what emperors did when Heaven sent warnings. He issues a 罪己诏, an edict blaming himself. It is sincere and it is beautifully written, and it changes nothing in the furnaces.

The court splits along a line older than the dynasty. One faction wants coal banned outright. The other reaches for the oldest story in Chinese engineering. The flood-tamer Gun tried to dam the great flood, and failed. His son Yu dredged channels and led the water to the sea, and succeeded. Block it and it bursts. Guide it and it goes. seed

Yu’s faction wins, and the empire channels instead of damming. Furnaces near rivers move to water-wheels, and furnaces on the windy plateau to wind. And from the 常平仓, the ever-normal granary, an old institution that bought grain in good years to release in bad ones, comes the most elegant idea of all: an ever-normal granary for the air. Carbon taken in the form of charcoal is buried in the fields, where it enriches the soil and stays out of the sky. The granary was first proposed under the Han by 耿寿昌, the same official who helped edit the Nine Chapters. seed

None of it happens all at once. It happens county by county, ledger by ledger, as everything in this empire does.

III · The great work

补天 · Mending the sky

Every child in this world knows how the sky was mended once before. When the pillars of heaven cracked, Nüwa smelted stones of five colours and patched the hole. There is another story too, of the archer Yi, who shot nine suns out of the sky when ten burned at once. At court, the archers’ faction proposes something like his: block the Sun outright. The proposal is rejected as crude. One does not shoot the Sun. One mends the sky.

So the empire founds a 补天司, an Office for Mending the Sky. Its tools are the empire’s own inventions. Rockets, which the Song first built for war, carry fine dust of the five-coloured stones high above the clouds. Up there the dust hangs for a season, scattering a little sunlight back into space.

The 补天司 method · one patch at a time
The sky above the empire is divided into the same grid as its counties. Each season a few patches are mended, the results are measured, and the next season’s patches are chosen from what was learned.

And because this is the Chinese table, the Office never does it all at once. The sky is divided into a grid, like the counties below. Each season a few patches are mended, the frost dates and blossom dates beneath them are measured, and the method is adjusted. Then it goes one patch further. The procedure never ends, and that is its strength. Each season’s answer is better than the last, and there is always a next one.

The first thing anyone notices is the sunsets. Over Kaifeng the evening sky turns colours no painter has used before: jade, then vermilion, then gold, then white, then deep ink-black. Poets call them the five colours of Nüwa, and the court painters spend a generation trying to catch them.

Part ThreeThe same rains, by magic GPT

Three worlds try to water a sky

Run the three worlds again. This time the trouble is the rain itself, failing, coming late, flooding one valley and missing the next, and this time magic works. Each tradition still meets it alone.

A scene to begin · the granary opens

On the first morning of the harvest, the granary keeper opens her doors. There are fewer baskets than last year.

She has kept the same record for nineteen years. During the first six, a bad season was an exception. Now every family has a story about the rain: it arrived while the grain was drying; it stopped during germination; it flooded one valley and never reached another.

The elders ask what offence has been committed. The farmers ask when to sow. The physician asks why the fevers have changed. The ruler asks how many people can still be fed.

No one has yet said that the climate itself might be changing.

Greek world, with magic

The Council of the Etesians and the Wind Stations

An island city builds towers that read the strength and bearing of the seasonal winds. Physicians send in their illness reports, farmers their flowering dates, sailors their seas. For decades these are local arts. Then the records begin to agree: the Etesian winds have changed their yearly road.

A school that traces itself to Empedocles, the wind-stayer, proposes a chain of wind-turning stations across the islands and mountain passes. They call it a work of the stoicheia, the elements of the world. Its aim is to carry the moist air back to the failing fields.

The assembly

“Who grants you permission to move the wind?” asks a delegate from a neighbouring island.

“The people who would otherwise starve,” replies the engineer.

“Then why have you turned its rain away from us?”

For the first time, a project meant to save one city has become an argument between entire seas.

So the Greeks do what Greeks do with an argument between seas. The Council of the Etesians is founded, a league of rival cities, pilots, physicians and elemental philosophers, and the wind becomes the first thing in the Greek world that every city owns together.

Indic world, with magic

The Rain-Weavers and the varṣa-maṇḍala

The earliest rain-working schools are masters of speech, performance and the timing of the seasonal rites. Centuries later, scholars of water, farmers, physicians, musicians and administrators build a network of measuring stations and ritual grounds, to compare how the rain answers different arrangements of sound, landscape and gathered people.

The great shift is from calling rain here to keeping the rain moving through the whole inhabited world. The work runs through connected river catchments, reservoirs, forests and the paths of the air. Its makers call it the varṣa-maṇḍala, the rain-circle.

The monastery granary

At the seventh assembly, a young grain-keeper interrupts the singers.

“Last year’s rite was successful,” she says. “The clouds came. You all witnessed them.”

“Then what troubles you?” asks the presiding scholar.

“They came too late for the millet. The rice survived, but the millet did not. And the village upstream says that our abundance was their flood.”

They had measured the rain’s arrival. She asks them to measure what it does.

From that day the rain-circle keeps a second ledger, of timings: when each crop wants water, when each valley can take it. The weavers stop composing one monsoon and begin composing many, a living multiplicity of rains, each with its own season and its own village waiting for it.

Chinese world, with magic

The Calendar of Ten Thousand Valleys

Seasonal reports come in from villages, river gauges, seed-houses, travelling physicians and the networks of ritual specialists. A central academy learns to see change across generations: the spring rains that could be counted on now fall as autumn deluges; the upland crops fail while the coasts flood.

Its answer is a vast network of hydraulic and atmospheric adjustments. Each region is given its window for planting, for cloud-working, for storing water and for letting it go. The whole becomes a planetary calendar, many local seasons aligned into one steady pattern.

A report sent upriver

The regional bureau reports that the spring correction succeeded. Every official gauge recorded the scheduled rain.

Yet a woman arrives carrying a flowering branch. “The river has changed its smell,” she says. “The fish came early, but their young have disappeared. Your rain has reached the fields. It has missed the river.”

The scholar who designed the correction asks to examine the branch.

The magistrate asks whether the report can be filed under an existing category.

It cannot. So the academy makes a new one.

The Calendar’s great reform is a door for reports nobody asked for. The fisher, the herbalist and the woman with the branch can now change the schedule, and the Calendar grows more exact for it, county by county, as everything in this world does.

The discovery all three share

Every place has its own good weather

The moment each world reaches for the whole sky, it learns the same thing. A rain on one coast changes the air over another. More rain is a gift before planting and a ruin after it. A river’s health is more than the grain it waters. Heat, wind, soil, insects, forests, bodies and sea-life all answer on their own clocks.

Magic in these worlds can work astonishing control over a single process. Understanding the whole living system is a second art, and the three worlds go on to invent it.

A climate is shared by everyone, and lived by each place in its own way.

So each civilisation grows new kinds of knowing alongside its spells: institutions that welcome correction, ways of listening across distance, archives that keep dissent, and seats at the council for those who cannot sit there themselves, the generations to come and the life beyond the human.

The arts come back into the argument

Seasonal songs, harvest dances, woven sequences and local stories hold long memories the gauges miss. A melody always sung at the first flowering is suddenly sung at the wrong time of year, and a singer notices before a governor does. In these worlds the artist sits inside the work of knowing a changing environment, beside the engineer. The thread runs on into 138 What Artists Built.

None of these three worlds ever heard of the others. And yet every one of their great works sits on a modern desk today.

A shade at the point between Earth and Sun, dimming the light by 1.8 percent, was proposed in all seriousness in 2006. Brightening clouds with sea-spray is a living research programme. Scarcity was a school of thought GPT

This whole thread began with an observation about Mozi and Xunzi: they lived in a time of scarcity, when food was uncertain, bodily harm was common, communities fought, and the ways to ease suffering were few. Their question was what kind of thought could end its causes.

The three archives answer it with respect for how hard that was. The Greek physician reading illness in the winds, the Indic official measuring the rain, the Chinese diviner and the river engineer, and the many farmers and craftspeople whose watching never became a famous book: the urge to organise knowledge against suffering was already there.

And what a society takes to be the cause of its suffering decides whom it empowers to act. If drought is a moral warning, rulers and ritual specialists step forward. If it is a hydraulic problem, engineers and labourers are called. If it is a seasonal pattern, forecasters and farmers gain a new authority. A climate that changes all at once calls on all three together.

Three rooms that never meet

In one world a council argues over whether the wind can belong to a city.

In another, a grain-keeper insists that rain’s timing matters more than its splendour.

In a third, an official opens a file for which no category exists.

None of them has heard of the other two. All three have begun the same generous work: learning to change a world while listening to everything living within it.

The Greek sky hung a proof between the Earth and the Sun. The Vedic sky laid a white bird on the sand. The Chinese sky mended itself one patch at a time. Three ways of knowing gave three ways of cooling a world, and all of them still look up.

Real seeds & sources

Each seed in Part Two points to something that really existed, and each numbered note in Part One to a source you can open. Everything else is imagined.

Seeds of the three skies Claude

  1. Greek · climate rememberedTheophrastus (c. 372–282 BCE) recorded that Cretans believed their climate had changed, with harsher winters and more snow than before, and other ancient writers linked draining lakes and diverting rivers to cooling around Larisa in Thessaly. Plato’s Critias describes deforested Attica as the bones of a wasted body, its soil washed away.
  2. Greek · the commonsAristotle, Politics II.3: what is common to the greatest number receives the least care. Parapegmata, stone star-and-weather calendars with movable pegs, are real. Hero of Alexandria’s aeolipile was a real steam device, never used as an engine.
  3. Greek · the place to stand“Give me a place to stand” is attributed to Archimedes by Pappus. Aristarchus of Samos measured the Sun’s distance as roughly 18 to 20 times the Moon’s, a large underestimate. The nights-warming-faster argument is a real modern fingerprint distinguishing greenhouse warming from a brighter Sun.
  4. Vedic · kindled heatṚgveda 10.190.1: ṛta and satya are born from tapas, kindled heat. Ṛta is the Vedic cosmic order, and Varuṇa its guardian. The Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa ties the ritual year to the nakṣatras. Dakṣiṇā is the real sacrificial fee. Sabhā and samiti are Vedic assemblies.
  5. Vedic · the altarThe agnicayana is the great falcon-shaped fire-altar. The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa counts its space-filling bricks at 10,800, the number of muhūrtas in a year. Altars were built for a rite and rebuilt for the next.
  6. Chinese · rock oilShen Kuo (1031–1095), in 《梦溪笔谈》, named 石油 and wrote 此物后必大行于世. He also inferred a past wetter climate from fossil bamboo found in the dry north at Yanzhou. Northern Song China ran a large coal-and-iron industry.
  7. Chinese · records & portentsDong Zhongshu’s 天人感应 held that Heaven warns rulers through disasters and anomalies. Emperors issued 罪己诏, edicts of self-blame. Local gazetteers 地方志 recorded weather and phenology, and in 1972 the meteorologist 竺可桢 used such records to reconstruct five thousand years of China’s climate.
  8. Chinese · granary & floodThe 常平仓 ever-normal granary was first established under the Western Han by 耿寿昌, who with 张苍 also compiled and revised the Nine Chapters. In the flood legend, Gun dams and fails while Yu channels and succeeds. Nüwa mending the sky is in 《淮南子·览冥训》, and Yi shooting nine suns is a classic myth.
  9. Modern · the three great worksRoger Angel (2006) proposed blocking 1.8% of sunlight with a cloud of small craft near the inner Lagrange point, L1, enough to offset a doubling of CO₂. Marine cloud brightening with sea-spray and stratospheric aerosol injection are active research areas. The 1991 eruption of Pinatubo briefly cooled the world and reddened sunsets worldwide.
