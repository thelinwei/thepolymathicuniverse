The proposition · three traditions

Three ways of knowing, one warming world

This thread grew out of the Three Tables experiments in the Polymathic Learner, where one problem was put to Greek, Vedic and Chinese mathematicians and each answered in its own native way. Doubling the Square and Squaring the Circle drew out three kinds of answer, and three verbs to carry them.

to boundthe Greek table

Archimedes traps the circle between a polygon inside and a polygon outside. The answer is an interval, proved from both sides.

to balancethe Vedic table

The Śulba cord-makers turn a square into a circle of equal area, and the circle back into the square. The answer is a rule that runs in both directions.

to cut finerthe Chinese table

Liu Hui cuts the polygon finer and finer: 割之弥细，所失弥少. The answer is a procedure that can always be refined.

Here the three tables leave mathematics and sit down before a warming planet. The thread runs in three volumes, from the real science, to the deep past, to a future with all of modern physics in hand.

00

The resultoutcome first, the story after

The questions: how much does Earth warm for a doubling of CO₂ (the equilibrium climate sensitivity), and how much more CO₂ can be emitted before a given warming? Climate science answers both. Reading its history through the three tables shows it already uses all three kinds of answer, and shows which one moved the field when.

Syracuse stance · to bound · Charney 1979 → IPCC AR6 2021
“Near 3 °C, between 2.5 and 4.” An interval, closed from several sides at once.

For forty years the headline interval barely moved from 1.5–4.5 °C. It finally narrowed when independent lines of evidence (physical processes, the instrumental record, the deep past) were made to trap it together, as Archimedes trapped the circle from inside and outside.

Board stance · to cut finer · climate models, 1990 → today
Grid cells from 500 km down toward 1 km. The spread did not shrink on the way.

Models cut the planet ever finer, as Liu Hui cut the circle. But the newest generation spread wider, 1.8 to 5.6 °C, because the deciding pieces (clouds) are still smaller than the cut. Refinement converges once the knife reaches what matters.

Altar stance · to balance · carbon budgets, 2009 → AR6 → 2025
About 0.45 °C per thousand billion tonnes of CO₂, and the rule runs both ways.

Warming rises almost in straight proportion to all CO₂ ever emitted. Like the Śulba cords, the rule can be read in either direction: tonnes into degrees, or a temperature limit into tonnes left. That second reading is the carbon budget.

What the experiment found

The biggest change in what climate science could offer the world came from switching answer type. Better precision on the old question mattered less. “How warm for doubled CO₂?” is a Greek question with an interval for an answer. “How many tonnes are left?” is an altar question, answered by a rule that runs from a limit back to a quantity. Once the field could answer the second, a temperature goal could be turned into a budget that ministries, companies and cities can count down.

The interval narrowed by trapping, not by cutting. AR6's tighter range came from combining independent lines of evidence. The model ensemble, cut finer, spread wider.

Liu Hui's promise has a condition. “Cut finer, lose less” holds when what you trim away is the error. For a circle it always is. For the climate, the error lives in clouds a kilometre across, and until the cut is that fine, finer is not yet closer.

How this was run. Unlike 01 and 02, nobody here is ancient. The three stances distilled from those experiments are used as lenses on a living science, and every figure on the page is a published assessment. The stances are ours; the numbers are the field's. The imagined exchange near the end is play.

01

The boundArchimedes' trap, a century of attempts

Arrhenius 1896 · Manabe & Wetherald 1967 · Charney report 1979 · IPCC AR4 2007, AR5 2013, AR6 2021

In 1896 the Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius worked out, entirely by hand, how much the world would warm if the air held twice as much carbon dioxide. His answer was 5 to 6 °C. It was a Board answer, a long procedure ground through column by column, and it was the first number anyone had. In 1967 Syukuro Manabe and Richard Wetherald ran the question through an early computer model of a single column of air and got 2.3 °C. That work later shared a Nobel Prize.

Then, in the summer of 1979, a small committee led by Jule Charney met for five days at Woods Hole and did something different. They compared the models available, weighed what was physically understood and what was not, and gave an interval: warming near 3 °C with a probable error of 1.5 °C, so 1.5 to 4.5. That is the Syracuse move. They gave no single number. They gave a window, with a stated reason for each edge.

The window held, stubbornly. Assessment after assessment repeated it, apart from a brief narrowing in 2007. Then the 2021 report brought a real change. Building on a large community review led by Steven Sherwood, it combined four independent lines of evidence: how the physics of clouds and water vapour behaves, how the planet has actually warmed since 1850, what ice ages and hot epochs in the deep past reveal, and patterns that link models to observations. Each line on its own is weak at one end. Together they close the trap from both sides.

Read the red bars from top to bottom and the Syracuse story is plain. The window barely moves for thirty years, then closes in. AR6 states a best estimate of 3 °C, likely 2.5 to 4, very likely 2 to 5. The teal bars show the opposite motion. More detailed models did not agree more closely. That is the next table's story.

02

The cut割之弥细, on a spinning planet

Global climate models · IPCC FAR 1990 → CMIP5 → CMIP6 → kilometre-scale models

A climate model is Liu Hui's board scaled to a planet. It cuts the atmosphere and ocean into cells, writes the laws of motion, heat and moisture for each, and steps forward in time, one pass after another, for simulated centuries. Around the first IPCC report in 1990, a typical model's cells were about 500 km on a side. By 2007 the finest were near 110 km. Today a new kind of global model runs at a few kilometres, and at about one kilometre it begins to draw individual storm clouds instead of guessing them.

Here is what each cut can see, over a patch of the equator 400 km wide with an island the size of Singapore in the middle:

Fig. 2 · One region, cut finertap

The board kept its promise in one way. Coastlines, mountains, monsoons and ocean currents all sharpened with each cut. On the one number everyone wanted, it did not converge. The CMIP5 models of the early 2010s gave sensitivities from 2.1 to 4.7 °C. The CMIP6 models, more detailed again, gave 1.8 to 5.6, the widest spread of any generation since the 1990s. The reason that keeps coming up is clouds, and how they interact with tiny airborne particles.

This is where Liu Hui's knife and the planet part ways. A circle's error sits in the slivers between the polygon and the arc, and every cut shrinks them. A climate model's largest error sits in processes smaller than its cells. Those still have to be written in as rules of thumb, and adding cells elsewhere doesn't touch them. Cutting finer pays off fully once the cut reaches the cloud. Global models are now arriving at that scale, which is why the kilometre-scale runs are the most watched experiments in the field.

03

The balancea cord that reads in both directions

Allen et al. & Matthews et al., 2009 · IPCC AR5 2013 · AR6 2021 · Indicators of Global Climate Change 2025

In 2009 two research groups found something unexpectedly simple. Peak warming depends, to a good approximation, on one quantity: the total CO₂ ever emitted. Neither the timing of emissions nor the path taken matters much, only the running sum. Plotted against that sum, warming rises close to a straight line. Two complicated effects roughly cancel. Each extra tonne of CO₂ in the air traps a little less heat than the one before, while the oceans take up both heat and carbon a little less readily as they fill. The net result is a line.

The IPCC now puts the slope at about 0.45 °C for every 1000 billion tonnes of CO₂, likely between 0.27 and 0.63. Humanity emitted roughly 2400 billion tonnes between 1850 and 2019, and the line puts that at a little over 1 °C, matching what the thermometers show.

This is the altar's kind of answer, a cord that works both ways. Baudhāyana's rules turned a square into a circle and back. This rule turns tonnes into degrees, or a temperature limit into tonnes. Read in the second direction, it becomes the remaining carbon budget.

Fig. 3 · The two-way cord · tonnes ↔ degreesslide either one

The plain cord in Fig. 3 counts only CO₂. Methane, aerosols and other gases also push temperature up or down, so the assessed budgets set some room aside for them. AR6 gave 500 billion tonnes from the start of 2020 for an even chance of holding warming to 1.5 °C. The 2025 edition of the annual Indicators of Global Climate Change put it at 130 billion tonnes from the start of 2025, a little over three years of current emissions. The 2026 edition reports human-caused warming at 1.37 °C in 2025.

Look at what changed. The sensitivity interval of the Syracuse table describes the planet. The budget hands the planet's answer to the people who have to act on it, in the unit they control. A finance ministry has little to work with in “2.5 to 4 °C per doubling”. It has a great deal to work with in “130 billion tonnes”. The altar-builders needed a rule that let one altar be laid out from the other. Policy needed a rule that lets a temperature be laid out as a quantity.

04

The maths, side by sidethree answer types, written out

Plate G · the bound · energy balance and a trap
ΔF = 5.35 ln(C/C₀); ECS = ΔF(2×)/λ; p(ECS|E₁…E₄) ∝ p(ECS) Π p(Eᵢ|ECS)
How to read itDoubling CO₂ adds about 3.7 watts per square metre of heating. That is the logarithm at work: each doubling adds the same push. How much warming that produces depends on λ, how strongly the planet sheds extra heat, which is where clouds and water vapour enter. The last line is the trap. Each line of evidence Eᵢ rules out some values. Multiplied together, they leave only the values that survive all four, as Archimedes kept only the lengths that fit both the inside and the outside polygons.
Plate C · the cut · what refinement buys
N ≈ 5.1×10⁸ km²/Δx²; work ∝ (1/Δx)³; error = grid part (shrinks) + sub-grid part (clouds)
How to read itHalve the cell size and the surface needs four times the cells. The time step must also shorten, so the work grows about eightfold. The highlighted line is the difference from Liu Hui. His whole error was the grid part, which every cut reduces. A climate model also carries a sub-grid part, the clouds it cannot see, and that part stays the same until the cut becomes fine enough to draw them.
Plate V · the balance · one line, read both ways
ΔT ≈ TCRE × E; E(left) ≈ (T(limit) − T(now))/TCRE − E(non-CO₂)
How to read itForward: multiply tonnes by the slope to get degrees. Backward: take the degrees still allowed, divide by the slope, and subtract an allowance for the other gases. That gives the tonnes still allowed. It is the same move as Baudhāyana's I.58 and I.59: one relation, written so that either side can be the starting point.
05

If the three tables met, againan imagined exchange · play

The voices are the stances from 01 and 02, brought forward to the present. Staged throughout.

Board

I have cut this planet into cells finer than any field I ever measured. “割之弥细，所失弥少。”

Syracuse

And yet your many boards disagree more than they did ten years ago. The interval came from me: four kinds of evidence, each closing a side.

Board

My circle never had clouds. When the cut reaches the cloud, I will close your interval from the inside.

Altar

While you two argue the edges, someone has to lay out the ground. Give me the slope between tonnes and degrees and I will run it backwards for them.

Syracuse

Your slope has its own interval, 0.27 to 0.63.

Altar

Then I will say so, as we always did. Saviśeṣa. The budget comes with its likelihood written on it.

Here the rigour ends and the wondering begins

The question behind this experiment was whether some disciplines are approached the wrong way, and whether a different way of knowing could transform one. The climate case suggests a sharper version of the question: who is the answer for, and what will they do with it?

to boundwhat the planet is
to cuthow it behaves
to balancewhat we may do

A science that only bounds describes the world and stops. A science that only cuts can run forever. A science that balances turns description into instruction. Climate science grew all three, and its reach widened most when the altar's kind of answer joined the other two. A doubling sensitivity is something you contemplate. A budget is something you can start counting down.

That gives a test for any field. List the answer types it currently gives. Then ask which of the three tables is missing, and who is waiting for exactly that answer. In medical diagnosis, the habit of ruling out causes until one remains is pure Syracuse. In city-making, the plan as something never finished is the Board. The table each field lacks may be where its next transformation is waiting.
