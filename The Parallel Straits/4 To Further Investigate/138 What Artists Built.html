A person could found a school. An instrument could become a curriculum. A performance could preserve a city's history. All three changed how knowledge moved.

01   /   The encounter

The people who gave art a public life

What did an artist do in an ancient or early-modern society? The answer could include singing at court, remembering the deeds of a family, training performers, refining the manners of a city, organising workshops, shaping religious and civic ceremonies, preserving craft secrets, or turning a new instrument into a profession.

Our PMU conversation began with a question about the Mohists. Could another intellectual lineage provide a strong counterpart on the artistic side? The first names that came to mind were poets and calligraphers. Then the search shifted toward people whose influence could outlive their personal ability: the makers of institutions.

A brilliant performer can transform an evening. A teacher can transform a generation. Someone who establishes a repertoire, a school, a guild, a workshop, a method of notation or a way of exchanging knowledge might shape the next several centuries.

The artist as organiser

A troupe, a household, a guild, a craft atelier, a public academy. Artists arranged relationships among students, patrons, teachers and communities.

The artist as knowledge-maker

Repertoire, notation, manuals, instruments, gestures, recipes, technical methods and standards of excellence. These could travel beyond the original maker.

The artist as social intermediary

Genealogy, praise, ceremony, cultural diplomacy, civic education and public memory. Some art shaped how people recognised one another.

The artist as authority

Courts and academies supported refinement, prestige and disciplined training; the resulting standards also influenced whose work was valued.

One particularly compelling figure carries all these threads into a single astonishing life—and a legend that has grown even larger than the life itself.

02   /   The blackbird of Córdoba

Ziryab, and the city that learned to listen differently

In the ninth century, a musician known as the Blackbird reached Córdoba. By the time later generations told his story, he appeared to have taught an entire city how to sing, dress, eat and behave.

Ziryab — زرياب↗, commonly identified as ʿAlī ibn Nāfiʿ, was associated with the musical world of the Abbasid east before travelling west through North Africa to al-Andalus. Accounts place his entry into the court of Córdoba in the early ninth century, often around 822. As a singer and instrumentalist, he stood within a demanding culture of court performance, patronage and artistic competition.0102

The celebrated later biography credits him with a fifth oud string, a new musical school, fashionable hairstyles, seasonal clothing, refined dining customs and more. These stories show the breadth of his reputation: music was imagined as capable of reorganising everyday courtly life. The historian Dwight Reynolds, comparing eleventh- and seventeenth-century accounts, shows how the later retelling magnified the single heroic founder and diminished the visibility of other musicians. Carl Davila offers another critical reconstruction of the source tradition.0102

For an illustrated modern retelling of Ziryab, see Muslim Heritage’s Ziryab feature ↗. Its pictorial representations are evocative historical interpretations.

Listen for the other voices

Ziryab’s daughters Hamduna and ʿUlayya are remembered in accounts of his musical circle; ʿUlayya is particularly associated with teaching. Court music also depended on apprentices, household specialists and enslaved performers. The musical transmission behind the celebrated name was a social world, with family relationships, professional obligations, access to patrons, and skills passed between bodies.0301

Here is a more interesting institutional picture than the simple claim that one man founded a conservatory. A teaching household might operate through informal apprenticeship, daughters, students, rivals, patrons and the performance circuit. When those relationships endure, a tradition may become larger than any single building.

A founder’s greatest achievement might be a community that can continue making discoveries after the founder is gone.

And Ziryab's legend continues to act. An ethnomusicological study of the modern project Ziryab and Us showed musicians from different Mediterranean traditions drawing on his symbolic legacy in order to collaborate—and debating what shared heritage should mean. A historical figure had become a reusable cultural meeting place.04

Possible relevance to PMU

Ziryab could inspire an artistic household, a roaming courtly network, or an Al-Merikani institution whose influence extends into hospitality, music, dress, diplomacy and education. An even stranger possibility is a school whose legendary founder has become easier to remember than the many people who actually sustained it. The eventual recovery of those voices could change the school itself.

03   /   Music made teachable

Three Chinese answers to the problem of artistic transmission

Cai Yuanpei sought an educated public capable of aesthetic judgement. Liu Tianhua worked with the instruments, notation and teaching of music. Wei Liangfu helped form a performing language in which breath, phrasing, poetry and dramatic gesture became inseparable.

Civic aesthetic education

Cai Yuanpei 蔡元培↗

1868–1940. His 1917 argument for 以美育代宗教, commonly translated as “replacing religion with aesthetic education,” reached beyond classrooms into the formation of modern public life.0506

Cai asked what a society gains when people learn to experience, discuss and judge artistic works together. He saw aesthetic education as a powerful route to cultivating sensibility and transcending narrower interests. He also became a practical supporter of artistic institutions. His influence belongs to the history of Chinese modern education, including the establishment of national arts institutions and the musical conservatory movement.0507

For PMU, the provocative inheritance would be an academy that values the ability to encounter unfamiliar forms of beauty. A civic curriculum might send its students into other people's studios and ceremonies, and ask them to explain why a work matters to its community before offering their own judgement. Such a school would have to examine its own standards constantly.

Liu Tianhua 刘天华↗ — a musician who made an instrument into a curriculum

1895–1932Erhu · PipaResearch · Pedagogy · Reform

Liu Tianhua was a performer, composer, educator and organiser working during a time of intense re-examination of Chinese music. He studied established traditions, wrote new compositions and technical studies for the erhu and pipa, supported musical research and promoted methods of notation and systematic teaching. In 1927 he helped establish the Society for the Advancement of National Music (国乐改进社).080910

These decisions change an art's social reach. Written exercises let students practise beyond a master's presence. A published score lets repertoire travel. An institution can bring people trained in several musical cultures into productive disagreement. Yet every performance still depends on tone, timing, touch, physical training and interpretation.

For PMU, this is extraordinary terrain: a Lan Fang researcher might record the precise shape of a sound-magic effect; a Sané-Kora performer might demonstrate that the very same notation changes meaning in another social relationship. Together they could invent a new pedagogical method that retains both reproducibility and living responsiveness.

Wei Liangfu 魏良辅↗ — the singer who refined a language of performance

16th centuryKunqu 昆曲Voice · Gesture · Dramatic form

Wei Liangfu is closely associated with the refinement of Kunshan musical practice and the style called 水磨调, often rendered “water-polished melody.” The image suggests extraordinarily patient shaping of vocal inflection, syllable, rhythm and phrase. Kunqu developed into a rich synthesis of singing, acting, instrumental accompaniment and poetic drama, sustained through generations of performers.1112

Now the proposal that made the evening explode with possibility: what if Wei Liangfu had been born a jeli? Imagine his inventiveness shaped by a Mande performance lineage, where musical knowledge can carry genealogy, social history and praise. He would be learning the responsibilities of particular relationships as well as sophisticated musical forms.

And a Sané-Kora–Lan Fang sing-off suddenly becomes a meeting of two complex systems of knowledge. It could begin with dazzling performances and end with a new understanding of what listening itself can accomplish.

04   /   The kiln and the loom

When materials carry culture across oceans

Tang Ying in Sané-Kora. Huang Daopo in Nine Rain. Two placements proposed almost laughingly, and each opens a serious research direction: what happens when the deepest knowledge of a craft enters a society that gives its materials different meanings?

Tang Ying 唐英↗ and the knowledge of porcelain

Tang Ying (1682–1756) helped direct Qing imperial porcelain production at Jingdezhen. His career entangled artistic judgement with clay preparation, glaze chemistry, design, specialised labour, kiln temperatures, administrative organisation and recorded methods. It offers a compelling model of an artistic research institution in which hundreds of craftspeople and multiple technical disciplines contribute to a single finished object.14

During our discussion you asked why African regions generally developed different ceramic technologies from China's large-scale high-fired porcelain industries, and whether African societies appreciated imported porcelain as European patrons did. The history offered a wonderful reversal in perspective.

Different African regions had abundant and sophisticated ceramic traditions of their own. Imported Chinese porcelain acquired additional lives through trade and display. On the Swahili coast, the ruins of Kilwa Kisiwani include architectural spaces ornamented with inset imported Chinese porcelain. In other West African courtly contexts, foreign objects entered local systems of ritual and authority. Europe pursued yet another trajectory, making imported Chinese porcelain a prestigious model for local technical replication.15171819

The extraordinary visual fact

At Kilwa, a porcelain vessel could become part of a building. In a workshop, its value might lie in glaze, heat and translucency; in another society, it could become a visible record of maritime connection. Its physical properties remained available for further invention in both places.

And then you sent Tang Ying to Sané-Kora. Consider the clay-bodied Igbo udu or kimkim, a vessel also used as a percussion instrument. This belongs to a distinct West African cultural history from Mande jeliya; their encounter within Sané-Kora would be an imagined exchange across communities. The ceramic expert and the musician-potter could begin asking new questions together: how does porosity shape resonance? How do openings affect air motion? What changes after a different firing process? Could a porcelain vessel store, scatter, or modulate a magical song?16

The exciting story would place two already knowledgeable traditions in conversation. The Sané-Kora instrument-maker might become Tang Ying's teacher in acoustic practice, while Tang Ying could widen the possibilities of bodies, glazes and kilns. The result could be a material-music research school neither could have built alone.

Huang Daopo 黄道婆↗ and the textile knowledge that travelled

Huang Daopo (traditionally dated c. 1245–1330) is remembered for carrying textile expertise from Hainan to the Songjiang area, where she helped improve cotton spinning and weaving. Her story foregrounds a crucial chain of learning: important techniques were acquired through contact with the Li communities of Hainan and then adapted and transmitted elsewhere.2021

Nine Rain offers many points of encounter: water, tension, humidity, fibres, dyes, sails, shelter, riverine exchange and the labour of making. Imagine a cloth whose pattern is both visual composition and a record of its transmission. Its maker must know who taught each technique, which environment shaped each fibre, and why certain methods should be returned to the communities that developed them.

The extraordinary thing about this placement is how easily a fabric might become an archive, a climatic instrument, a diplomatic gift or a magical construction, all at once.

05   /   More ways to create a school

A fuller cast of institution-builders

The central figures lead to a larger constellation. Some founded named institutions. Others established repertoires, techniques, workshops or traditions of embodied training that continued to organise knowledge long after them.

Yan Cheng 严澂↗

1547–1625 · China
Yushan guqin musician associated with organised musical taste and the Songxian Guan Qinpu (1614). A school can emerge from repertoire, aesthetic deliberation and a community of practitioners.25

Li Yu 李渔↗

1611–1680 · China
Playwright, theatre organiser, publisher and writer on gardens, performance and domestic aesthetics. His publishing milieu contributed to the spread of the Mustard Seed Garden Manual of Painting, first issued in 1679. His example suggests an entire cultural ecosystem, from rehearsal to book production.26

Kanō Masanobu↗

1434–1530 · Japan
Founder of the Kanō painting lineage. Family workshops, apprenticeship, patronage and branch schools sustained an artistic grammar over centuries.23

Zeami Motokiyo↗

c. 1363–c. 1443 · Japan
Noh actor, playwright and theorist whose treatises studied lifelong artistic development and the careful transmission of stage knowledge. A lineage might treat artistic maturity as something revealed slowly through practice.24

V. N. Bhatkhande↗

1860–1936 · India
Musicologist, classifier and educator who gathered repertoire, investigated systems of ragas and helped build institutional Hindustani music education. A powerful precedent for the researcher who also creates an academy.27

V. D. Paluskar↗

1872–1931 · India
Founded the Gandharva Mahavidyalaya in 1901 and pursued broader access to formal musical education. Useful for thinking about the social status of musicians and new publics for established traditions.28

Rabindranath Tagore↗

1861–1941 · Bengal
Santiniketan joined teaching with landscape, literature, performance and international exchange. A precedent for the whole environment of a school becoming part of its pedagogy.29

Ye Chunshan 叶春善↗

1875–1935 · China
An important figure in establishing the Beijing opera training tradition associated with Fu Lian Cheng (1904). This adds the disciplined performing school, with multigenerational repertoire and intensive bodily training.

Xiao Youmei 萧友梅↗

1884–1940 · China
Composer and music educator associated with the founding of China's National Conservatory of Music in Shanghai (1927). A useful pairing with Cai Yuanpei: philosophical and organisational sponsorship meeting the work of a specialist music educator.07

Dai Ailian 戴爱莲↗

1916–2006 · China
Choreographer and educator involved in the development of institutional dance training in modern China. Offers a bodily, choreographic and professional-training counterpart to musical reform.

Beyond the founders lies another major type of institution: the living lineage. In Mande jeliya, knowledge of songs, praise, genealogy and social relationships is cultivated through hereditary and apprenticeship structures. The institution is a continuity of people and obligations as much as any physical school.13

06   /   Great excellence, contested values

The emperor whose school belongs offshore

One artist in the conversation was admitted to the highest company of talent, while his particular vision of artistic authority was given a very different place in the world.

Song Huizong 宋徽宗↗ (1082–1135), emperor of the Northern Song, was a gifted painter and calligrapher and a formidable courtly patron. Under his authority, elite artistic training could become remarkably refined. His institutional legacy invites questions about canon, access, prestige and the extent to which a ruler's taste shapes acceptable artistic achievement.22

Your response was memorable: “I admit his talents. But I don't share his values nor his taste. Too narrow.”

So he remains a possible figure of authority at a distant, offshore institution: a school capable of breathtaking accomplishment, with aesthetic convictions sufficiently powerful to attract admirers and repel others. Its students might arrive in the Straits carrying extraordinary techniques and an exceptionally strict account of beauty. Their encounters with Sané-Kora, Lan Fang and Nine Rain could be deeply generative.

A useful tension

An institution can teach genuine mastery and still produce strong disagreement about what mastery is for. The difference between artistic excellence and authority over taste gives this offshore school its dramatic potential.

There is a historical philosophical companion to the problem, someone who believed music could shape the conduct of whole societies: Xunzi 荀子↗. We will meet him again in the closing pages, where the conversation returned to the circumstances in which these arguments first mattered.

A wider consequence concerns the character of the seven schools themselves. The artist could also be the person who designs a medical ritual, an acoustic instrument, a memorial, a diplomatic gathering, a communal archive, a physical theatre or a new method of collaborative research.

The strongest arts-side counterbalance to Mohism might therefore take the form of a constellation of institutions. A court academy, a hereditary lineage, a publishing workshop, a civic conservatory and a travelling troupe could disagree about the purpose of art while still learning from one another.

Ways to transmit

Scores, manuals, craft recipes, teacher–student relationships, embodied exercises, recitation, performance and long apprenticeships.

Ways to govern

Admission, access, recognition of borrowed techniques, public demonstrations, lineage obligations, patronage and the ethics of influence.

08   /   Experiments in encounter

Five scenes waiting to be written

These are small imaginative laboratories rather than plotted episodes. Each places a historical insight into an encounter between people whose expertise pulls them in different directions.

Ziryab’s missing teachers

In an Al-Merikani library, students proudly recite the founder's accomplishments. A visiting archivist finds a record attributing a celebrated technique to one of his daughters. Further records name household musicians and students whose work had been absorbed into the great man's biography. The school suddenly has a different inheritance to teach. Some students want new portraits. Others want to find the lost compositions.

In Sané-Kora, the kiln begins to sing

Tang Ying listens while a ceramic maker strikes a vessel and changes its pitch with a measured quantity of water. The potter asks what would happen if a thinner, harder wall could be fired. Tang Ying offers a porcelain body. Both spend the evening arguing over shapes and openings; their first successful instrument astonishes the performers, who immediately begin discovering sounds the inventors had never anticipated.

Nine Rain asks who taught the loom

A student presents a magnificent woven garment that channels water along its fibres. The evaluators admire its effect, then ask for the history of its techniques. Each dye, weave and mechanical improvement has travelled through different communities. The student is sent to make a second garment: this one must be understood by the people whose knowledge helped produce it.

The Sané-Kora–Lan Fang sing-off

Lan Fang's singer begins with an impossibly subtle phrase, its ornament changing the resonance of the whole hall. The Sané-Kora singer replies with a song that addresses three listeners by the histories they carry. Each performance astonishes its own knowledgeable audience; each leaves other listeners confused in different ways.

Then an elderly spectator sings a fragment of a tune belonging to neither side. Both performers recognise a possibility in the other's answer. Their next piece begins together. The judges realise that the event has become a lesson in how to listen.

The emperor’s invitation

A student from the Straits receives admission to the distant academy of Huizong. Its works are beautiful beyond anything they have imagined. The curriculum demands unwavering adherence to a particular refinement. The student spends a season learning techniques they could scarcely have dreamed of, while gathering sketches of the strange and unruly forms that the academy refuses to display. Eventually, the student requests permission to hold an exhibition.

09   /   The thought that arrived at the end

The question beneath the argument

Only after the artists, courts, lineages, porcelain, textiles and musical encounters had filled the evening did you offer the interpretation that changed the ancient philosophical dispute.

“They lived in a time of scarcity. And tech. Resources were rare. Ways to alleviate suffering were rare. Suffering caused by climate, lack of food, suffering by bodily harm, mutual killing, etc. Hence, what thought can end the causes of the suffering?”

Your response during the conversation, 18 November 2026

That response deserves its place here, after the cast is assembled, because it gives the historical disagreements their human setting.

Mozi 墨子↗ and the Mohist tradition objected to costly elite music in terms of labour, material expenditure and the welfare of people exposed to hunger, aggression and deprivation. Xunzi 荀子↗, writing generations later, accepted the need to organise society while giving music and ritual a central role in cultivating emotions, shaping conduct and bringing people into ordered relationships. In the Discourse on Music (乐论), he takes seriously the social effects of musical practice.33343031

In his Discourse on Ritual (礼论), Xunzi began from human desire and the conflict that can arise when claims collide. Ritual, differentiation and deliberate cultivation offer his answer to the social management of these pressures. Mozi’s emphasis on frugality and collective benefit, and Xunzi’s confidence in education and ritual, arose within the capacities and constraints of their respective worlds.3230

These were philosophies concerned with how societies could endure. Crop failure, war, limited means of treating bodily harm, uneven access to resources and relatively fragile systems of coordination made the question of alleviating suffering immediate. Their answers reflected the range of interventions, information and institutions available to them. The questions were material, political, ethical and educational at once.

Four useful kinds of scarcity

Tonight's further reflection suggested a broader vocabulary. Material scarcity limits food, safety, medicine and labour. Relational scarcity limits trust, mutual understanding and the repair of damaged obligations. Imaginative scarcity limits the alternatives a society can collectively conceive. Institutional scarcity limits the means to preserve, test, transmit and govern knowledge.

In PMU, a useful spell can improve food security or protect a settlement from floods. Its transmission may create new schools; its distribution may reshape power; its consequences may require new relationships. A musical practice can carry memory, coordinate people, preserve grief, celebrate life or give strangers a first chance to recognise one another. These forms of labour increasingly cross.

The ancient arguments can therefore be carried forward as an ongoing inquiry: Which kinds of suffering can our existing knowledge relieve? Which forms of life deserve cultivation? Who may determine the purpose of a school? And what new powers of imagination could change the answers?

A school may begin with a master’s extraordinary skill. Its larger achievement is to enlarge the world that other people can make.

That seems a fitting place to leave this investigation: Ziryab's daughters still singing, a potter teaching Tang Ying, Huang Daopo's techniques moving across communities, Wei Liangfu entering a jeli household, and an offshore emperor's students discovering unfamiliar forms of beauty.
