  1. Yes, the jeli sang and spoke in the presence of foreign envoys, and the singing was the protocol. In 1352 messages from outside reached the king of Mali through his jeli. Songlines, Manas, the jeli and the ashiq

Your friend’s list, opened out.

Your friend began with record-keeping songs and moved, as the thread went on, to epics and to wandering mystics. The four together cover most of the ways a culture can trust a voice: as a map, a charter, a genealogy and a teaching.

Mali Empire, c. 1235 → Mali, Guinea, Senegal and The Gambia today

The jeli: the word as a hereditary trust

Some seventy generations of one family, still singing the founding of an empire.

Jeli (griots) are born into a handful of Mande families, among them the Kouyaté, Diabaté, Cissoko, Kanté and Suso. The Kouyaté line begins with Balla Fasséké, jeli to Sunjata Keita, who founded the Mali Empire around 1235. The Keita are the royal lineage; the Kouyaté have been their voice ever since. Toumani Diabaté, the kora master who died in 2024, traced his own family back some seventy generations.

Read moreRead less

A jeli keeps a social map: who descends from whom, which families owe each other what, and which deeds each name carries. The Sunjata epic is the charter of that map. It tells how the empire was founded and also how its peoples received their roles, the jeli’s own included. A performance can run for hours, moving between sung praise, spoken narration and instrumental passages on kora, balafon or ngoni.

When a jeli sings your family’s praise names (jamu), you are being placed, in public, inside that map. The praise is a record and a reminder at once: it tells the room who you are and what your ancestors did, and it quietly sets the standard you are now expected to live up to.

The two families your friend named sit on the two sides of this relation. The Keita are the nobles whose history is kept; the Diabaté, like the Kouyaté, are keepers. That is why Salif Keita, a Keita by birth, shocked his family when he chose to sing for a living, taking up work his lineage had always commissioned from others. The workshops your friend attended and the Kouyaté recordings you found on YouTube are this same tradition, taught from parent to child, with the lineage itself part of the authority.

Songlines: the map is sung, and checked by kin

Stories that still describe coastlines the sea covered more than seven thousand years ago.

A songline is a route across Country held as a sequence of songs. Each verse belongs to a place, so singing the song in order is walking the land in order: waterholes, ridges and the deeds of the ancestral beings who made them. The Seven Sisters songline crosses the Western and Central Deserts and passes between language groups, each of whom holds its own stretch.

Read moreRead less

The coastline finding your friend mentioned comes from the geographer Patrick Nunn and the linguist Nicholas Reid (2015). They gathered stories from 21 places around Australia’s coast that describe land now under the sea: islands once joined to the mainland, reefs that people once walked on. For each place they calculated how far the sea would have to fall for the story to be true, then matched that depth against the post-glacial sea-level curve. The stories come out at roughly 7,000 to 13,000 years old, from the time when the sea, which had stood about 120 metres lower at the height of the last ice age, was rising to where it is now.

How does a story last that long? Reid points to kin who check. A man learns the stories of his father’s country, and his sister’s children are formally responsible for making sure he knows them correctly. Three generations stand in a ring of correction, so memory is a duty spread across people rather than an object kept in one place.

The songs are also law. Who may sing which verse, where and to whom is itself regulated, so the sung map carries rights of access and responsibility for the land it describes.

Manas: half a million lines in one voice

The longest recorded telling runs to 500,553 lines, from a single reciter.

Manas is a trilogy: Manas, his son Semetey and his grandson Seytek, and the long work of uniting the forty Kyrgyz tribes. Its reciters, the manaschi, perform in a heightened, near-trance state, carried by rhythm, tone and gesture. Sayakbay Karalaev’s version, collected between 1936 and 1952, is the half-million-line figure your friend remembered, and Karalaev is on Kyrgyzstan’s 500-som note.

Read moreRead less

Many manaschi describe their calling as a dream in which Manas or his companions appear and command them to recite. The ability arrives as a summons, and the epic is carried as a duty. Every manaschi’s version differs; there is no master text, only lines of performers.

Manas is also one of China’s three great epics of its minority peoples, beside Gesar and Jangar. Jusup Mamay (居素普·玛玛依), a Kyrgyz manaschi from Akqi in Xinjiang, recited a full eight-part version that was published in Kyrgyz and translated into Chinese.

The epic doubles as a record of the steppe: it names more than a hundred peoples and touches almost every route of the Silk Road. UNESCO inscribed the trilogy in 2013, and today the Kyrgyz national commission has put recordings of several renowned manaschi on YouTube, while young reciters train in schools.

The ashiq: lover, wanderer, poet of the saz

The name means “one in love”, and many say the calling came in a dream, with a cup.

Ashiq (Turkish âşık, Azerbaijani aşıq, Armenian ashugh) are minstrel-poets who sing with the long-necked saz. Azerbaijan’s classical repertoire alone holds about 200 songs, 150 dastans (sung epics) and nearly 2,000 poems. Their art joins poetry, storytelling, dance and music, and they are the region’s great improvisers, composing verse on the spot.

Read moreRead less

In Sufi usage the beloved is God. Many Turkish and Azerbaijani ashiqs tell of becoming one in a dream in which a saint offers them a cup (bade) and shows them the face of a beloved; they wake as poets. The wandering that follows is a search, and the songs carry morality tales and esoteric teaching in plain, singable verse, the double layer your friend described.

Ashiqs also duel. In a contest (atışma in Turkish, deyişmə in Azerbaijani) two poets trade improvised verses on a set rhyme and theme until one is cornered, with the audience as judges. Names to start with: Âşık Veysel, the blind 20th-century poet of Anatolia, and Sayat-Nova, the 18th-century ashugh of the Tbilisi court who sang in Armenian, Georgian and Azerbaijani.

In Xinjiang, ashiq names Uyghur Sufi mendicants who gathered at saints’ shrines (mazar) and sang at the great shrine festivals; some were ironworkers, barbers or grave-diggers, some women. The festivals ended across the region by 2015. Their songs live on inside the Twelve Muqam, the Uyghur classical canon, which grew partly out of what dervishes sang at the shrines. The Stephen Jones post below discusses a documentary of the kind your friend saw.

Thread II

The jeli at the table

Speech made official by song, from 1352 to the 2024 installation of a Malaysian king.

How was the music framed when a jeli stood between his ruler and a stranger? The sources answer clearly at court, in the epics, and in the modern state.

The short answer

In the Mande world, and among the Akan and Dagomba nearby, words between a ruler and anyone outside passed through a professional of the word. The visitor heard the dynasty’s history performed, heard his own lineage praised and placed, and spoke into a room where every word was witnessed. Music and chanted speech were how a meeting became official.

The medieval record gives us the reception and the channel (Ibn Battuta in 1352). The epics give us the jeli as go-between who crosses into an enemy’s court. The modern record gives us state dinners for visiting presidents, griots travelling as spokespersons, and griots organised as mediators in Mali’s crises.

Four things the singing did, which a Western diplomatic protocol does with letters of credence, interpreters and minutes:

The channel. The ruler’s words reach a foreigner only after passing through the jeli’s or okyeame’s mouth. That keeps a ceremonial distance, lets the words be shaped, and leaves the ruler room to adjust.
The witness. What is said and given in front of the jeli is public and remembered. A gift praised in song becomes part of the giver’s reputation, carried onward to the next court.
The placement. Praise names set the stranger inside a genealogy and its alliances, including the Mande joking kinships (senankuya) that let allied clans tease each other and draw the heat out of a meeting.
The licence. The jeli stands outside the contest for rule. Hoffman and Hale both describe how that lets griots approach both sides of a quarrel without being attacked, and say what nobles cannot.

The court did not pause for music. The music was how the court did its business.

Court of Mansa Sulayman of Mali, 1352–53

A king who speaks through another man

Messages from outside reached the king through his jeli.

Ibn Battuta, the Moroccan traveller, was himself a foreign visitor when he watched Mansa Sulayman speak through his jeli, Dugha. Messengers arriving at court handed their message to the griot. The same man taught the princes, led the musicians and ran the ceremonies.

Read moreRead less

On feast days Dugha performed before the king and played an instrument of wooden keys over gourds, the balafon. Masked poets then recited to the king the deeds of the kings before him and urged him to be worthy of them. Every envoy entered a hall where history was being performed aloud, and their words joined that history through the jeli’s mouth.

In the Sunjata epic the jeli is also the one sent across lines. Balla Fasséké goes into the fortress of Sumanguru, Sunjata’s enemy, plays the enemy king’s own balafon, and so charms him that Sumanguru claims him as his own jeli. The story already makes the jeli a go-between whose person can cross into a hostile court.

Asante kingdom (Ghana), c. 1700 → today

The okyeame: a spokesman with a golden staff

The chief does not speak directly. The linguist speaks, and shapes the words.

Akan rulers address the world through an okyeame (plural akyeame), usually translated as “linguist”. The Asantehene and his paramount chiefs keep several. Over the centuries they have served as counsellors, historians, judicial advocates, military attachés, foreign ministers and ambassadors, and they carry a gold-leafed staff, the okyeame poma, whenever they act in office.

Read moreRead less

The staff tops are carved proverbs: a spider for Ananse, who brought proverbs to the Akan; a man climbing a tree with another pushing him (“one who climbs a good tree always gets a push”). Raising a proverb while speaking frames the exchange as royal speech, deliberate and metaphorical, to be read the way a proverb is read. One such staff came to Singapore’s National Museum in 2015–16, on loan from the British Museum.

The folklorist Kwesi Yankah (Speaking for the Chief, 1995) shows that the okyeame edits as he relays: he adds proverbs, softens or sharpens, so the chief’s words arrive already shaped, and the chief keeps room to adjust what was said. A ruler who never speaks directly can never be caught in a careless sentence.

Akan courts also speak in drums. The atumpan talking drums reproduce the tones of Twi, and drummers recite praise and history on them at durbars, so a visiting delegation is announced, greeted and praised in drum language before anyone speaks.

Drummers who settle a constitutional question

In 1958 the state council asked the chief drummer whether a king with a deformity had ever ruled.

In Dagbon the lunsi, hereditary players of the hourglass drum, are the court’s historians, archivists and recorders of its political order. They chant the genealogies that link every royal, living and dead, and teach them to the princes. When a claim to office is disputed, they are called to say what tradition holds.

Read moreRead less

In 1958 the eligibility of a candidate with physical deformities was disputed, and the chief drummer, the Namoo Naa, was called before an emergency meeting of the Dagomba State Council to say whether such kings had ruled before. The drum chant was the precedent, and the drummer stood as the court of record.

The most prestigious performance is Samban luŋa, an all-night chant of the kingdom’s epic history. Dagomba listeners distinguish three kinds of good drummer: those who know the history, those with sweet voices, and those with supple wrists. The first are the ones the old men want, and the ones called in a dispute. Much of the First Kingdom, from the mid-15th to the late 17th century, is known to history almost entirely through this chant.

Bamako, Conakry and Dakar, 1958 → today

Griots of the republics

Mali’s president called on Toumani Diabaté to play for visiting heads of state.

The office outlived the kings. The most accomplished griots travel with presidents and serve as their spokespersons, and a great griot may receive a state funeral or a postage stamp, as Banzoumana Sissoko did in Mali. Toumani Diabaté played government dinners in Bamako, and Mali’s president often called on him to perform for visiting heads of state: the jeli at the reception, in a modern hall.

Read moreRead less

Independence leaders used the role knowingly. Sékou Touré of Guinea built national orchestras and ballets that toured the world as cultural embassies, and griots sang his praise. Salif Keita’s “Mandjou” is a praise song for Touré, who had met the band on an official visit in 1974 and made Keita an officer of Guinea’s National Order of Merit in 1976.

In Senegal, Youssou N’Dour, whose mother came from a griot family and who calls himself a modern griot, served as Minister of Culture and Tourism from 2012, and from 2013 as a special adviser with ministerial rank, charged with promoting the country abroad.

Griots mediate too. Every ethnic group in Mali has at least one hereditary category of specialists in conflict mediation, and RECOTRADE, the largest association of “traditional communicators” in West Africa, now organises them for elections, public health and peace work. During the 2012–15 crisis, the Timbuktu singer Khaira Arby said the griots had offered to mediate between Bamako and the north, and she, Bassekou Kouyaté and Toumani Diabaté kept urging reconciliation from the stage.

The nobat: no sultan without the drums

A Malay ruler is installed when the nobat sounds. The music itself is regalia.

The nobat is a court ensemble of drums, the long nafiri trumpet, serunai and gong, and it is counted among the royal regalia beside the keris and the royal headdress. No Malay sultan is legitimately installed until he is drummed in by it. Its players come from hereditary families, in Perak the Orang Kalur, and only they and royalty may handle the instruments.

Read moreRead less

The nobat came to the Malay courts with Islam from around the 13th century, from the Middle Eastern naubat. It is described as the only court ensemble of the Islamic world still played in its original setting, for living rulers. It plays by royal command: installations, royal weddings, births and funerals, and in Kedah daily before prayers. People once said they heard a baby’s cry inside the drums, and the ensemble is treated as sacred.

It sounds at the federal level too. When Sultan Ibrahim was installed as Yang di-Pertuan Agong in July 2024, the Royal Nobat of Perak played “Raja Berangkat” as the royal couple went to the throne and “Nobat Tabal” as he finished the oath, before the triple cry of Daulat Tuanku.

Legitimacy travels with it. When Melaka fell to the Portuguese in 1511, the nobat and regalia went with the fleeing royal line to Johor, and later succession disputes turned on who held them: in Riau-Lingga a royal widow kept the regalia and refused to recognise the Bugis-favoured heir.

India, from c. 1500 BCE; UNESCO 2008

Vedic chanting: error correction by permutation

Six words become sixty, chanted forward and back, so that no syllable can drift.

The Vedas were carried by voice for some three thousand years, and the reciters built checksums into the chant. Beyond the plain text (saṃhitā), students learn it word by word (pada), in overlapping pairs (krama: ab, bc, cd), and then in braided and bell-shaped permutations, jaṭā and ghana, where each phrase runs forward, back and forward again.

Read moreRead less

A slip in one mode produces an obvious clash in another, so a community of reciters can find and fix errors without any written master copy. Ghana is the hardest: ab ba abc cba abc, then bc cb bcd dcb bcd, and so on through the text. A scholar who masters it is honoured as a ghanapāṭhī.

Pitch accent is kept as well, and reciters mark the accents with movements of the hand, so the body joins the record. UNESCO counts thirteen surviving recitation branches, each a lineage with its own text and sound.

For anyone drawn to the physics and mathematics of music, this is information theory in sound: a code designed for a noisy channel, human memory across generations, with the error checking held by a community of voices.

The guslar who showed how Homer worked

Avdo Međedović heard a 2,294-line song once and sang it back at 6,313 lines.

Milman Parry, a Harvard classicist, went to the Balkans in the 1930s to test an idea: that the Iliad and Odyssey were composed in performance, out of formulas, by singers who did not read. With his student Albert Lord he recorded singers of the South Slavic epic, sung to the one-stringed gusle. The greatest was Avdo Međedović, an illiterate farmer who dictated The Wedding of Smailagić Meho over a week in July 1935: 12,311 lines.

Read moreRead less

To test him, Lord had another singer perform a song Avdo had never heard. Avdo listened once, then sang it back nearly three times as long and richer in description. The singer recomposes each time from a stock of formulas and themes, so there is no fixed text to forget, only a grammar of telling.

This changed how the West reads its own founding poems: Homer became a tradition of singers. The Parry Collection at Harvard holds 3,584 twelve-inch discs from the 1930s, and nearly one seventh of them are Avdo, singing or talking.

Gesar bards: the epic arrives in a dream, or in a mirror

Some bards read the epic off a blank mirror, or a car windscreen.

The epic of King Gesar of Ling, sent down to subdue monsters and unite tribes, runs to some 120 episodes, and Tibetans class its bards by how the epic came to them. Babdrung wake from a dream of a deity able to recite far beyond any memorisation. Tradrung see the scenes in a mirror, on paper or in a windscreen and sing what they see. Others learned by hearing or read from books, and terdrung, sometimes unable to read, “unearth” new episodes and write them down.

Read moreRead less

Tibetan masters perform with a bronze mirror, expressive faces and sound effects, wearing a hat with eyes and silk streamers; Mongolian Gesar singers accompany themselves on the fiddle. Performances are woven into daily life: when a child is born, the episode of Gesar’s descent into the world is sung. The epic is called “beads on a string”, episode after episode.

Since the 2000s inspired bards have been recognised officially at prefectural, provincial and national levels, recorded, transcribed and invited into schools, so that a panel of experts now certifies a gift that comes in a dream. Manas reciters tell of the same kind of calling: across the plateau and the steppe, the right to sing an epic is something received.

Tuva and western Mongolia; a herders’ practice, heard worldwide since the 1990s

Throat singing: listening to rivers in overtones

One voice, two pitches, learned by copying water and wind.

In khöömei a singer holds a low drone and shapes the throat and mouth to amplify single harmonics above it, so a melody of overtones floats over the fundamental. Tuvan legend says the first throat-singers were copying sounds rich in harmonics: gurgling streams, wind, the echo off a cliff.

Read moreRead less

Theodore Levin and Valentina Süzükei call Tuvan music timbre-centred. Herders listen less for the tune than for the colour of a sound, and they hold that the spirit of a mountain or river lives in the sounds it makes. Singing by a stream or into a cliff becomes a duet with the place, and the singer’s body becomes a landscape.

Throat singing is learned like a language, by immersion rather than lessons. In Levin’s 1999 account a large share of male herders could do it, and the old taboo against women singing it was fading. Mongolia’s khöömei and its long song, urtyn duu, are both on UNESCO’s lists.

For the physics: the voice is a harmonic-rich source passed through a tunable filter, the resonances of the vocal tract, and the singer narrows that filter until one harmonic stands out. Levin’s Scientific American article below shows it in spectrograms.

The Dong Grand Song: an encyclopaedia for choirs

Polyphony with no conductor, sung in the drum tower, carrying a people’s knowledge.

The Dong have no script of their own, and the Grand Song (侗族大歌) is how they keep history, ecology and ethics. It is multi-part singing with no instruments and no leader, taught by masters to choirs of disciples grouped by age and sometimes gender, and sung formally in the village drum tower, the same building that holds rituals and meetings. A Dong saying: rice nourishes the body, songs nourish the soul.

Read moreRead less

UNESCO calls it a Dong encyclopaedia. The songs narrate the past, hold practical and scientific knowledge, carry love songs and teach respect for elders and neighbours. One family of songs imitates nature: the well-known cicada songs set a sustained low line under high voices that mimic insect calls.

Choirs usually sing in three parts and sometimes as many as six. Song is also how villages meet one another: guests arriving at a Dong village are often stopped at the gate by songs that they must answer in song before they may enter, and whole villages visit each other for evenings of singing back and forth.

Sápmi: northern Norway, Sweden, Finland and the Kola Peninsula; revived from 1968

Joik: you sing a person, not a song about them

“A joik is not about. A joik is.” Nils-Aslak Valkeapää

A joik belongs to a person, an animal, a place or a mood. Sámi say you do not joik about someone; you joik them. A young person may receive their own joik, which follows them through life and keeps them present after death, for as long as it is sung.

Read moreRead less

Joiks are often wordless, built of vocables around a melodic phrase tied permanently to their subject, and are often described as circles with no fixed start or end. Anthropologists read joik as knowing through presence; one story tells of a yoiker who frightened a pair of elk by singing the bear’s melody. Because a joik is a person’s own, Sámi musicians ask outsiders to use original joiks only with Sámi collaborators.

Nils-Aslak Valkeapää (Áillohaš) led the revival from 1968, recording joiks with reindeer bells, barking dogs and other sounds of the land, and performed at the opening of the 1994 Lillehammer Winter Olympics. Mari Boine carried joik onto world stages from the 1980s.

Ethiopian highlands, Gondar and Addis Ababa; court minstrels in the imperial era

Azmari: wax and gold

Praise on the surface, critique underneath, and an audience trained to hear both.

Azmari are Ethiopian minstrels who sing with the one-stringed masinqo fiddle or the krar lyre, improvising verses about the people in the room. Their art rests on sem-ena-werq, “wax and gold”, from qiné poetry: the wax is the literal sense, and as the wax melts the gold, the hidden meaning, comes through, as in lost-wax casting.

Read moreRead less

In the imperial era azmari served at court and in noble houses. Today they work in azmaribet, azmari houses, and at weddings, church feasts and spirit-possession ceremonies. They praise guests by name and are tipped for it, while the gold of the same verse can tease, warn or criticise. Listeners are expected to catch the second meaning; the skill belongs to the audience as much as to the singer.

Wax and gold made song a channel for politics. Singers used it to criticise governments past the censors, and people say that by asking what the azmari is singing about you can learn the burning questions of the day. The anthropologist and filmmaker Itsushi Kawase (川瀬慈) has documented azmari in Gondar for years.

Perlis, Malaysia; at the Esplanade, Singapore, May 2023

Awang Batil: one man, one brass bowl, two masks

The storyteller who drums on a water bowl played the Esplanade in 2023.

Awang Batil is a Perlis form of penglipur lara, “soother of sorrows”: a single storyteller who tells hikayat while beating a batil, a brass water bowl, and switching to violin, serunai or drum. He puts on two masks, the hulubalang (warrior) and the Wak Nujum (royal soothsayer), when those characters enter. Pak Romli Mahamud is its master, heir to his father Mahmud Wahid.

Read moreRead less

The form began as Awang Belanga, drummed on a cooking pot; soot on the pot’s base led storytellers to brass, which also rings sharper. The long tales were told when a host invited the storyteller home, often after the rice harvest: a story as a gift to a household.

Pak Romli was named successor by his father in 1974 and took up the role fully in 1997, the fifth generation of his line. The batil he uses was his father’s. In May 2023 he brought three tales, Awang Akar Larak, Awang Nakal and Awang Parang Besar, to the Esplanade’s Pesta Raya, presented by Pusaka. Close by in the same Malay world are the sung pantun exchanges of Melaka’s dondang sayang and the court nobat above.

The okyeame’s proverb staff as an instrument; drummer-historians called to testify, as in Dagbon; a joking-kinship code between houses that lets rivals tease and cool a meeting.

Joik as presence: a working sung to a person or place that is that person or place. Original joiks belong to Sámi people, so Sund’s would be its own invented songs in that spirit.
