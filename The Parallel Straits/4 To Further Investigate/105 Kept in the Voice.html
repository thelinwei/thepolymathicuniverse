(African empires beside the dynasties you know, the jeli who held their memory, and music as an instrument of state)

The record is a person, so the record has a seat at the table.

Claude3 November 2026 · reference · Sané-Kora’s real-world roots

The outcomeThree things to carry out

  1. Africa’s empires ran alongside the dynasties you know. Kushite kings ruled Egypt while the Zhou ruled China. Aksum minted gold coins in the century of the Three Kingdoms. Mansa Musa’s gold moved through Cairo while the Yuan held Beijing, and Great Zimbabwe’s walls rose through the Song.
  2. Many of these states kept their archive in people. The record was a trained voice, so the keeper of that voice held a seat in government, in the law court and in the embassy.
  3. Music, in these systems, is a ledger, a telegraph, a court of appeal and a diplomatic channel. China had its own version: blind court musicians who sang the royal genealogies, and envoys who negotiated by reciting the Odes.

Prologue

Ibn Battuta at the court of Mali

1352: a king who speaks through another man.

In 1352 the Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta stood in the audience hall of Mansa Sulayman of Mali and watched the king speak through another man. That man, Dugha, repeated the king’s words to the court, played an instrument of wooden keys over gourds, and on feast days led masked poets who recited to the king the deeds of the kings before him. Ibn Battuta had met the jeli: the hereditary keeper of the Mande word.

The jeli opens a door onto a whole family of civilisations that stored their constitutions, genealogies, treaties and histories in trained human memory, performed with music. This page walks through the empires, then the keepers, then what music can do when it is given the work of a state.

III
Thread III

The empires, era by era

c. 3100 – 1000 BCE

The Nile’s long foundations

Kemet (Ancient Egypt)c. 3100 – 30 BCE

Pyramids, hieroglyphic writing, a 365-day civil calendar, surgery and mathematics on papyrus, and roughly three thousand years of state continuity. Kingship was understood as the daily upkeep of ma’at, cosmic order.

ReachFrom Nubia to the Levant, with expeditions down the Red Sea to the land of Punt in the Horn of Africa.

Kermac. 2500 – 1500 BCE

Nubia’s first great kingdom, centred at the Third Cataract of the Nile. Its builders raised the massive mud-brick temples called deffufas, and at its height it allied with the Hyksos against Egypt.

ReachControlled the flow of gold, ivory and ebony between the African interior and Egypt.

Meanwhile

ChinaErlitou culture, then the Shang (c. 1600–1046 BCE) and their oracle-bone script.

WestMinoan Crete, Mycenaean Greece; Hammurabi’s Babylon in the Near East.

c. 1000 BCE – 350 CE

Iron, and the kings who conquered Egypt

Kush (Napata, then Meroë)c. 1070 BCE – 350 CE

In the 8th century BCE the Kushite kings Piye and Taharqa conquered Egypt and ruled it as its 25th Dynasty. Later, at Meroë, Kush became a great iron-working centre, created its own Meroitic script, and built more pyramids than Egypt ever did. Ruling queens, the Kandakes, governed in their own right; Kandake Amanirenas fought Rome to a negotiated peace in which Augustus remitted the tribute he had demanded.

ReachThe Nile corridor from Egypt’s southern border deep into today’s Sudan, trading with Rome, Arabia and India.

Carthage814 – 146 BCE

A Phoenician-founded city in today’s Tunisia that became the Mediterranean’s leading sea power, with a merchant republic’s constitution that Aristotle praised. Hannibal marched its army and elephants over the Alps into Italy.

ReachNorth Africa, Iberia, Sardinia and Sicily, with sailors exploring the Atlantic coast of Africa.

Meanwhile

ChinaWestern and Eastern Zhou, Confucius and the Warring States, Qin unification in 221 BCE, then the Han.

WestGreek city-states and Alexander; Rome grows from republic to empire.

c. 100 – 940 CE

Aksum, one of the four great kingdoms

Aksumc. 100 – 940 CE

The third-century prophet Mani counted Aksum among the four great kingdoms of the world, alongside Rome, Persia and China. It minted its own gold, silver and bronze coinage, raised carved granite stelae up to about 33 metres tall, and developed the Ge’ez script, which writes each syllable by marking vowels onto consonants. King Ezana adopted Christianity around the 330s, making Aksum one of the first Christian states.

ReachThe Red Sea trade between Rome and India; in the 6th century it ruled across the water in Yemen.

Meanwhile

ChinaLate Han, Three Kingdoms, the Northern and Southern dynasties, Sui, and the Tang from 618.

WestConstantine’s Rome, the fall of the Western Empire in 476, Byzantium, Charlemagne.

c. 700 – 1235 CE

Gold, salt and the Sahara crossing

Ghana (Wagadu)c. 300 – 1200

The Soninke kingdom between the Senegal and Niger rivers grew rich taxing the trans-Saharan exchange of West African gold for Saharan salt. The Andalusian geographer al-Bakri described its capital in 1068 as twin towns, one for the king and one for Muslim merchants, and noted that the throne passed to the king’s sister’s son.

ReachIts gold fed the mints of North Africa and al-Andalus.

Kanem, later Kanem–Bornuc. 700 – 1893

A cavalry state around Lake Chad whose Sayfawa dynasty ruled for roughly eight centuries, among the longest-reigning dynasties anywhere on earth.

ReachThe central Sahara routes to Libya and Egypt.

The Swahili city-statesc. 900 – 1500 at their height

Kilwa, Mombasa, Malindi, Mogadishu and dozens more: stone towns of coral masonry whose mosques set Chinese porcelain bowls into their walls. Kilwa minted its own coins, and Ibn Battuta in 1331 counted it among the most beautiful cities he had seen. Kiswahili was written in Arabic script.

ReachThe whole monsoon world of the Indian Ocean: Arabia, Persia, Gujarat, and on to China.

Ife, and Zagwe Ethiopiac. 1000 – 1400

Ife, sacred origin city of the Yoruba, cast naturalistic copper-alloy and terracotta heads of astonishing refinement. In the Ethiopian highlands, King Lalibela’s builders carved eleven churches downward out of living rock.

Meanwhile

ChinaTang, Five Dynasties, then the Song with printing, paper money and the compass at sea.

WestThe Carolingians, the Holy Roman Empire from 962, the first Crusades.

c. 1235 – 1600 CE

The Mande world and the great enclosures

Malic. 1235 – 1600

Sunjata Keita defeated the sorcerer-king Sumanguru of Sosso around 1235 and, by tradition, convened the assembly of Kurukan Fuga, which set out the roles of clans and professions and the rights of persons. A century later Mansa Musa made his pilgrimage to Mecca (1324) and spent so much gold in Cairo that its value there sank for years. The Catalan Atlas of 1375 draws him enthroned with a golden orb. Timbuktu and Djenné became cities of mud-built mosques and libraries.

ReachFrom the Atlantic coast to the Niger bend, an area comparable to Western Europe.

Songhaic. 1464 – 1591

Under Sonni Ali and Askia Muhammad, the largest state in West African history. Timbuktu’s scholars wrote and copied works on law, astronomy, medicine and grammar; hundreds of thousands of these manuscripts survive in family libraries. Songhai fell to a Moroccan musket army at Tondibi in 1591.

Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe, Mutapac. 1075 – 1760

A sequence of Shona states on the southern plateau. Great Zimbabwe’s Great Enclosure is a curving dry-stone wall about 11 metres high, laid without mortar. Chinese celadon and Persian glass have been excavated on site, carried in through the gold and ivory trade.

ReachLinked through Sofala and Kilwa to the Indian Ocean.

Benin (Edo)c. 1180 – 1897

The Oba’s court supported a guild of brass casters whose plaques record ceremonies, battles and foreign visitors: a visual archive of state. The kingdom was ringed by a vast system of earthen walls and moats. The British looted the palace in 1897, scattering the Benin Bronzes across the world’s museums.

Kongoc. 1390 – 1914

A centralised kingdom with shell currency and fine raffia textiles. Its king Afonso I wrote to the king of Portugal in 1526 protesting the slave trade in his realm, letters that survive.

Solomonic Ethiopia1270 – 1974

A dynasty whose founding epic, the Kebra Nagast, traced its kings to Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. Ethiopia later defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896 and remained independent through the colonial partition.

Meanwhile

ChinaYuan, then Ming. Zheng He’s fleets reached the Swahili coast; a giraffe from Malindi arrived at the Ming court in 1414 and was hailed as a 麒麟.

WestDante, the Black Death, the Renaissance, Columbus, the Reformation.

c. 1600 – 1900 CE

Stools, cavalries and caliphates

Oyoc. 1600 – 1836

A Yoruba cavalry empire whose king, the Alaafin, governed alongside the Oyo Mesi, a council of seven nobles empowered to remove him.

Dahomeyc. 1600 – 1894

Known for the Agojie, standing regiments of women soldiers, and for painted clay bas-reliefs on palace walls that record each king’s reign.

Asante1701 – 1901

By tradition the priest Okomfo Anokye called the Golden Stool down from the sky for Osei Tutu, and it became the seat of the nation’s soul. Asante ran a network of great roads with runners, weighed gold dust with brass weights cast as proverbs, and gave the Queen Mother the power to nominate the king.

Sokoto Caliphate1804 – 1903

Founded by the scholar Usman dan Fodio, the largest state in 19th-century West Africa. His daughter Nana Asma’u, poet and teacher, trained networks of women educators who carried her teaching poems from village to village.

Zulu Kingdom1816 – 1897

Reorganised by Shaka into a formidable military state that defeated a British army at Isandlwana in 1879.

Meanwhile

ChinaThe Qing, from 1644 to 1912.

WestThe Atlantic slave trade, the Enlightenment, industrialisation, and the Berlin Conference of 1884–85 that partitioned Africa. By 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent.

IV
Thread IV

Traditions that grew differently

Set against the Chinese and European worlds, these are the ideas that stand out most sharply.

Mali, 13th century

A constitution that is spoken

The Kurukan Fuga charter lived as recited text, carried by jeli and renewed in performance. UNESCO recognised it in 2009. A law that must be performed to exist is a law the community hears again every time.

Mande and wider West Africa

Joking kinship as a peace treaty

Senankuya binds certain clans and peoples into a permanent license to tease one another. Insults that would start feuds elsewhere become a ritual of closeness, and joking partners are obliged to help and mediate for each other.

Asante

Sovereignty held in an object

The Golden Stool is the soul of the nation, and no king ever sits on it. Sovereignty rests in the thing, and the person is its servant. In 1900, when a British governor demanded to sit on it, Queen Mother Yaa Asantewaa led the war in its defence.

Oyo, Asante, Rwanda and others

Sacred kings who could be recalled

The Oyo Mesi could present the Alaafin with an empty calabash, a ritual signal that he must give up his reign and his life. Asante chiefs could be “destooled” by their councils. Divine kingship came paired with formal machinery for ending it.

Kush, Akan, Dahomey, Ghana

Power held by women by design

Ruling Kandakes in Kush, Queen Mothers who nominate and counsel kings among the Akan, women’s regiments in Dahomey, and succession through the mother’s line in Ghana and among the Akan.

Oromo (Ethiopia), Igbo (Nigeria)

Generations as government

The Oromo Gada system moves every man through grades in eight-year steps, and leadership passes to a new generation-set every eight years (UNESCO, 2016). Among the Igbo, the saying Igbo enwe eze, “the Igbo have no king”, describes villages governed by assemblies, age grades and titled elders.

Yoruba

A binary oracle of 256 signs

Ifá divination casts sixteen palm nuts or a divining chain to produce one of 256 odu, each a doubled four-line binary figure, each opening onto a body of memorised verses. Set it beside the 64 hexagrams of the 易: two binary systems of signs, each paired with a vast oral or written commentary.

Luba, Akan, Ejagham

Memory you can touch

Luba lukasa boards studded with beads and shells, read by touch to recite history; Akan talking drums that speak tonal language over distance; nsibidi ideographs in southeastern Nigeria; adinkra symbols printed on cloth as proverbs.

Thread V

Why a singer could sit beside a king

A jeli (also jali; in French, griot and griotte) is a hereditary professional of the word in the Mande world of Mali, Guinea, Senegal and The Gambia. Jeli are born into families such as the Kouyaté, Diabaté, Cissoko, Kanté and Suso, and their craft, jeliya, joins genealogy, history, counsel, mediation, praise and music on the kora, balafon and ngoni. Women jeli, the jelimusolu, are among the most celebrated singers of the tradition.

The founding story already shows the whole job. In the Sunjata epic, Balla Fasséké Kouyaté is Sunjata’s jeli. Sent into the enemy’s fortress, he finds Sumanguru’s sacred balafon and plays it so beautifully that the sorcerer-king keeps him as his own bard, so the first jeli is at once musician, envoy and agent inside the opposing court. That balafon, the Sosso Bala, is still kept by the Kouyaté family at Niagassola in Guinea, and UNESCO recognised its cultural space in 2001. At Kangaba in Mali, the Diabaté jeli of Kela recite the full epic every seven years when the sacred house is re-roofed.

The record is a person, so the record has a seat at the table.
VI
Thread VI

Why the keepers held such power

Whoever holds the genealogy holds legitimacy

Rule rested on descent. The jeli could recite a ruler’s line back to Sunjata’s companions, and that recitation was the proof. To be sung into the lineage was to be confirmed in office.

The king’s word needs a carrier

In Mande thought speech carries nyama, a potent force. A ruler who speaks through a jeli keeps his own word in reserve: the message can be softened, reframed or withdrawn, and a hard demand can be delivered with grace. This is the same logic as a press secretary or a foreign minister.

The archive cannot be burned

A library can be seized. A trained lineage of reciters, spread across families and villages, carries the record wherever the people go and survives the fall of any single capital.

Performance is notarisation

When a jeli recites a settlement at a wedding, a treaty or an enthronement, everyone present becomes a witness. The public hearing is the registration.

Song is memory engineering

Melody, meter, refrains, set formulas and the repeating cycles of the kora and balafon give long texts a structure that a trained mind can hold for a lifetime and pass on with remarkable stability.

Praise and shame enforce norms

The jeli praises a noble by reminding him of his ancestors’ generosity and courage. Praise is also a standard to live up to, and the withheld song is a public verdict.

Ineligible for the throne, trusted with its secrets

Jeli belong to the nyamakala, the hereditary artisan professions, and stand outside the contest for rule. Their structural neutrality makes them credible envoys between rival houses, and custom held their persons protected.

A Chinese mirror: 瞽史

The Zhou court had its own singers of state. The 《周礼》 assigns the blind court musicians, 瞽矇, to recite the Odes and the royal genealogies:

瞽矇掌播鼗、柷、敔、埙、箫、管、弦、歌，讽诵诗，世奠系，鼓琴瑟。《周礼·春官·瞽矇》

And in the 《国语》, a minister reminds the king that good rule listens through many channels, music among them:

故天子听政，使公卿至于列士献诗，瞽献曲，史献书，师箴，瞍赋，矇诵……《国语·周语上》

China then took the path of the written 史官, and the sung genealogy faded into the brush. The jeli path kept the voice at the centre for another two thousand years. The Han 乐府, collecting folk songs so the court could read the temper of the people, is a later branch of the same tree.

VII
Thread VII

Keepers of the word around the world

KeepersAcross Africa
Akan, Ghana

Okyeame

The royal spokesman, carrying a staff topped with a carved proverb. The chief speaks to the public, and to foreign envoys, through him.

Akan, Ghana

Drum poets and the atumpan

Master drummers who “speak” the tonal Twi language on paired drums, reciting history, praise names and summons across distances.

Oyo, Yoruba

Arokin

Hereditary court historians who recited the list of Alaafins and their deeds, alongside the oriki praise poetry that names the qualities of persons and lineages.

Yoruba

Babalawo

Ifá priests who memorise an immense corpus of verses attached to the 256 odu, consulted for decisions from the household to the throne.

Zulu and Xhosa, South Africa

Imbongi

Praise poets who walk before the chief. Within praise they may voice public criticism. An imbongi performed at Nelson Mandela’s inauguration in 1994.

Kingdom of Rwanda

Abiru

Guardians of the ubwiru, the esoteric code of kingship that governed succession and royal ritual, alongside court poets of dynastic genealogy.

Luba, DR Congo

Mbudye, “men of memory”

A society that read kingdom history from the lukasa board by touch, the beads and shells cueing places, people and events.

Ethiopia

Azmari

Singer-poets with the one-string masenqo who practise sem-enna-werq, “wax and gold”: verses with a surface meaning and a hidden one beneath.

Wolof, Senegal; Hausa, Nigeria

Gewel and maroka

Close cousins of the jeli: hereditary praise singers, genealogists and masters of ceremony.

KeepersAcross Asia
Rajasthan and Gujarat, India

Charans and Bhats

Bards and genealogists of the Rajput clans. Bhats keep family registers of descent; Charans, held inviolable, stood as guarantors of treaties and debts. The closest Asian match to the jeli.

Kyrgyz and Kazakh

Manaschi and akyn

Reciters of the Manas epic, hundreds of thousands of lines long, and improvising poets whose public contests, aitys, still air social criticism on national stages.

Tibet and Mongolia

Gesar bards

Singers of the Gesar epic, many describing the text as received in a dream.

India

Vedic reciters

Lineages who have kept the Vedas orally for some three thousand years using interlocking recitation patterns, such as ghanapatha, that make errors self-correcting.

Yamato Japan

Kataribe 語部

Hereditary reciters of clan and court tradition. The Kojiki of 712 was set down from the recitation of Hieda no Are, a court attendant famed for memory.

KeepersAcross Europe
Iceland, 930 – 1271

The lawspeaker

An elected officer who recited one third of the whole law aloud from the Law Rock at each summer Althing, completing it every three years. The law existed as performance.

Ireland and Wales

Filid and bards

Poets with legal rank, genealogists to kings. A fili’s satire was feared as a force that could raise blisters on the face, a cousin of nyama.

Norse courts

Skalds

Court poets whose intricate verse fixed the deeds of kings and served as sources for the sagas.

Ancient Greece

Aoidoi and rhapsodes

The singers behind Homer: formula, meter and performance holding the Greek world’s shared past.

KeepersAcross the Americas and the Pacific
Inca

Quipucamayoc

Keepers of knotted-cord records of census and tribute, working beside the amauta sages and the court poets who recited dynastic history.

Hawai‘i, Aotearoa, Polynesia

Genealogical chanters

Keepers of whakapapa and of chants such as the Hawaiian Kumulipo, a 2,102-line cosmogony that runs from the origin of the world to a royal line.

Aboriginal Australia

Song custodians

Songlines encode routes, water, law and kinship across the land; to sing the country is to hold its map and title.

VIII
Thread VIII

Ledger, telegraph, court, embassy

Music organises time. Anything that organises time can organise memory, signals, disputes and alliances. Across the world, four great uses appear again and again.

song as recordThe ledger
Epics as archives. Sunjata, Manas, Gesar and the Vedas carry history, law and genealogy across centuries.
Songlines as maps and title. Aboriginal songs encode the country; they have been presented as evidence of connection to land in Australian native title claims.
Teaching poems. Nana Asma’u of Sokoto wrote her instruction in verse so that women educators could carry law and theology from home to home by memory.
News ballads. Mexican corridos report battles, floods and crimes; Trinidad’s calypso has long been called the poor man’s newspaper.
song as signalThe telegraph
Talking drums. The Akan atumpan and Yoruba dùndún reproduce the pitch contours of tonal speech, sending messages from village to village faster than a runner.
Whistled language. Silbo Gomero on La Gomera whistles Spanish across ravines; it is taught in island schools.
Military music. The Ottoman mehter band marched with the army, its sound a declaration of the sultan’s presence to friend and enemy alike.
song as interventionThe court
Song duels. Among the Inuit of East Greenland, two parties to a quarrel faced each other with drum songs and the audience judged; the verdict settled the matter.
Licensed critique. During the Apo festival of the Bono and Asante, people sang open ridicule of chiefs, a seasonal release valve built into the polity. Ewe halo songs let rival towns trade sung abuse in place of blows.
Improvised debate. Kazakh and Kyrgyz aitys poets duel in verse on public issues before large audiences, today on national television.
Listening to the people. The Han 乐府 gathered folk songs so the court could gauge the people’s condition.
Song as movement. Estonians gathered in their hundreds of thousands to sing between 1987 and 1991, the Singing Revolution; South Africa’s freedom songs carried the anti-apartheid struggle.
song as diplomacyThe embassy
赋诗言志. In the 《左传》, envoys of the Spring and Autumn states recited chosen Odes at banquets to state their positions; a well-chosen stanza could signal alliance, reproach or refusal.
季札观乐. In 544 BCE Ji Zha of Wu listened to the music of each state performed at Lu and read from it each state’s fortunes.
Tang court music. The 十部乐 assembled ensembles from Kucha, Gaochang, Korea and beyond: an empire’s reach performed in a single hall.
Jazz Ambassadors. From 1956 the US State Department sent Dizzy Gillespie, Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington on world tours. The Philadelphia Orchestra played in Beijing in 1973, part of the thaw.
Shared ensembles. Daniel Barenboim and Edward Said founded the West-Eastern Divan Orchestra in 1999, seating Israeli and Arab musicians side by side.
IX
Thread IX

The jeli today

Toumani Diabaté (1965 – 2024)

Malian kora master from a long jeli lineage who carried the instrument into collaborations with flamenco, blues and orchestras.

Sona Jobarteh

Gambian-British, widely described as the first woman from a griot family to become a professional kora virtuoso; she founded a school in The Gambia that teaches through the tradition.

Bassekou Kouyaté

Malian master of the ngoni, the lute that may be the jeli’s oldest instrument, played with a full ensemble.

Youssou N’Dour

Senegalese singer of gewel descent through his mother, who went on to serve as his country’s minister of culture and tourism. A praise singer’s lineage, in cabinet.

Imagining other forms

Each of these is a jeli function lifted into a present-day setting, ready to be designed.

The studio jeli

Every new project opens with a short recitation of its lineage: which earlier projects it descends from, who taught whom, which client conversation first planted it. The practice hears its own genealogy at every beginning, and newcomers are sung into it.

The cohort epic

A graduating cohort’s journey from first year to final review is composed into a performed piece, and re-performed for each incoming year, the way Kangaba re-roofs its house every seven years. The school remembers itself in a voice.

The crit as song duel

Critic and student exchange in a set, poetic form before an audience that judges the exchange. The structure carries the heat, as senankuya and the Inuit song duel do.

Sung minutes

Decisions of a board or a neighbourhood committee are composed into a short piece and performed at the next meeting. Everyone present becomes a witness, and a decision with a melody is a decision people remember.

A neighbourhood lukasa

A tactile memory board for a street or an estate, its beads and textures standing for people, shops and events, read aloud by elders to children.

The okyeame for an institution

A designated voice, human or AI, that carries an organisation’s word between parties and keeps the record of what was said: a spokesperson and an archive in one role.

Diplomacy by exchanged pieces

Two organisations, cities or schools open a partnership by trading composed works, each answering the other, in the spirit of 赋诗言志.

The jeli shows that a society can choose where its memory lives. Put it in a voice, and memory travels with the people, renews itself in every hearing, and gathers everyone present into its keeping.

Notes on dates and sources

Dates for African polities are the commonly cited spans; most grew gradually from older states, and archaeology keeps refining them. The Sunjata epic exists in many versions, varying by family and region, and that variation is part of the tradition. The text of the Kurukan Fuga charter we read today was assembled in 1998 by scholars and jeli from oral sources; the assembly itself is known through tradition. “Griot” is a French-derived word; Mande speakers say jeli or jali.
